# Research Pass 06 — Actual Muslims in Early America

## Governing question
Were Muslims merely hypothetical figures in founding-era debates, or were Muslims actually present in colonial and early United States society?

## Bottom line
Muslims were not hypothetical foreigners waiting to arrive in a later America. Muslims were physically present in British North America and the early United States, overwhelmingly through the Atlantic slave trade. The surviving record identifies literate West African Muslims who prayed, fasted, wrote Arabic, preserved Qur'anic material, resisted forced religious assimilation, formed relationships with other Muslims, and in several cases left unusually rich documentary traces.

The central contradiction for the book is therefore severe: American political thinkers could debate whether a hypothetical Muslim should possess civil rights or hold federal office while actual Muslims were living in America without freedom because they were enslaved.

This pass does NOT establish that the Founders personally knew the religious identities of enslaved Muslims around them. The Library of Congress specifically cautions that there is no evidence the Founders knew the religious convictions of their bondsmen. Keep that distinction intact.

## 1. Muslims were present at the founding
The Library of Congress states that there may have been hundreds, perhaps thousands, of Muslims in the United States in 1776, brought through slavery from African regions where Islam flourished. The exact number cannot be known because slave records generally did not record religion.

The National Park Service similarly warns that the total number is impossible to determine because enslaved people's religion was rarely documented and enslavers were often inattentive to it.

Evidence grade: A for Muslim presence; estimates of population size should be presented as estimates, not census counts.

## 2. Ayuba Suleiman Diallo — a Muslim in colonial Maryland before the Revolution
Ayuba Suleiman Diallo, also known in English sources as Job ben Solomon, was a West African Muslim scholar, writer, merchant, translator and aristocrat who was enslaved in colonial Maryland in the early 1730s. Smithsonian records identify him as an enslaved person and scholar/translator. The National Museum of African American History and Culture records that he maintained Islamic practice, suffered harassment while praying on Kent Island, Maryland, and wrote an Arabic letter seeking contact with his father in Africa. He ultimately regained his freedom and returned to Africa after traveling through England.

This matters chronologically. A documented practicing Muslim was living in the English colonies decades before the Declaration of Independence and more than half a century before Article VI.

Evidence grade: A.

## 3. Yarrow Mamout — Muslim, enslaved man, freedman, property owner and investor
Yarrow Mamout (c. 1736–1823), born in Guinea, was enslaved as a teenager and brought to Maryland. After approximately 44 years in slavery he became free and lived in Georgetown. Smithsonian sources describe him as a Muslim who could read and write Arabic before capture, became a brick maker and basket weaver, saved money, purchased stock in the Columbia Bank of Georgetown, and bought property.

Charles Willson Peale recorded in 1818 that Yarrow 'professes to be a mahometan' and was heard in Georgetown singing praises to God. Peale painted him in 1819. James Alexander Simpson painted another portrait in 1822.

Yarrow is particularly valuable narratively because he destroys the image of Islam as a late twentieth-century import. An openly Muslim Black man was a familiar Georgetown resident, homeowner and investor in the capital region during the founding generation.

Evidence grade: A.

## 4. Omar Ibn Said — the Muslim slave who wrote back in Arabic
Omar Ibn Said was born around 1770 in West Africa, probably in the region of the Gambia River/Senegambia. He was highly educated and Muslim. Captured and transported to Charleston in 1807 at about age 37, he escaped slavery, was recaptured in Fayetteville, North Carolina, and spent sixteen days in jail, where he began writing Arabic on the walls.

He eventually entered the household of James Owen and John Owen, later governor of North Carolina, and remained enslaved until his death in old age.

In 1831 Omar wrote his autobiography in Arabic. The Library of Congress describes it as the only known extant autobiography written in Arabic by an enslaved person in the United States. The manuscript itself survives in the Library of Congress.

The document begins with Surah al-Mulk, the Qur'anic chapter concerning dominion or sovereignty. A Library of Congress scholarly discussion notes the significance: ultimate ownership belongs to God. Scholars have reasonably explored whether Omar's choice functioned as an indirect critique of human ownership, but this is interpretation rather than an explicit statement by Omar and must be labeled accordingly.

The surviving translation also contains Omar's plea that he cannot fully write his life because he has forgotten much of his language and Arabic. The manuscript demonstrates that an enslaved Muslim was not simply an object described by American observers; he left his own account in the intellectual and religious language he carried from Africa.

Evidence grade: A for manuscript, biography and Qur'anic opening; B/C for particular interpretations of why Surah al-Mulk was chosen.

## 5. Bilali Muhammad — Islamic jurisprudence written in Georgia
Bilali Muhammad was an enslaved West African Muslim on Sapelo Island, Georgia. National Park Service and Smithsonian materials identify him as literate in Arabic and as the author of a famous Arabic document often called the Bilali Diary or Bilali Document. He prayed toward Mecca, and family/community memory preserved Muslim-linked practices.

The document is important for the broader Sharia project because it is not merely proof that an enslaved person knew Arabic. Scholarship identifies it as a religious/legal text drawing on Islamic jurisprudence. This deserves a dedicated later source pass before making detailed claims about its specific Maliki legal contents.

Evidence grade: A for Bilali's Muslim identity, Arabic literacy and manuscript; detailed fiqh identification should be independently verified against manuscript scholarship before publication.

## 6. Muslims communicated with one another
The Smithsonian's National Museum of African American History and Culture documents relationships among enslaved Muslims: Bilali Mohammad and Salih Bilali were described as close friends; Omar Ibn Said and Lamine Kebe corresponded; Ayuba Suleiman Diallo wrote in Arabic to his father. Arabic literacy sometimes allowed enslaved Muslims to communicate beyond what enslavers understood.

This complicates any depiction of early American Muslims as completely isolated curiosities. Slavery prevented the easy formation of durable mosques and institutions, but evidence survives of interpersonal Muslim networks and written communication.

Evidence grade: A/B depending on individual relationship and surviving document.

## 7. Religious practice under coercion
Early Muslims faced obvious structural obstacles to Islamic practice under slavery. Smithsonian/NPS accounts document interference with prayer, fasting, dietary rules and religious observance. Ayuba was harassed while praying. A Muslim enslaved in Louisiana was reported in 1822 as lamenting that slavery prevented him from obeying his religion.

Some enslaved Muslims outwardly adopted Christian practices while retaining Islamic belief. This requires careful treatment. Do not use modern anti-Muslim conspiracy rhetoric about 'taqiyya' as if concealment under enslavement validates claims that ordinary Muslims are authorized to lie to conquer non-Muslims. Concealment under coercion/persecution is precisely the historical context that modern polemics often erase.

Evidence grade: A/B.

## 8. The constitutional contradiction
This is the major interpretive finding of Pass Six.

The previous passes established that founding-era Americans explicitly discussed whether Muslims should enjoy civil rights, whether Muslims could hold office, and whether a Muslim could theoretically become president.

Pass Six establishes that Muslims were already here.

But many were enslaved.

Therefore the book must hold two truths together:

1. The developing American principle of religious liberty was capable of encompassing Muslims.
2. The American political and racial order simultaneously denied actual Muslim human beings the liberty those principles proclaimed.

This is not a reason to dismiss the constitutional principle. It is a reason to distinguish the principle from the people who incompletely applied it.

Potential narrative line:

> America imagined the rights of the hypothetical Muslim while enslaving the real one.

And the larger formulation:

> The American idea could be larger than the Americans who first articulated it.

## 9. Why this changes the modern immigration narrative
The common popular story places Muslims outside the American historical narrative and treats Islam as something that arrived after the country was already formed. The evidence does not support that chronology.

Muslims were present in colonial America. A practicing Muslim was enslaved in Maryland before the Revolution. Muslims were present around the founding. Muslim lives continued into the early republic. Arabic Islamic manuscripts were physically written on American soil.

A defensible formulation is:

> Islam is not new to America. Muslims were here before the Constitution.

Do not overstate this as 'Islam founded America' or claim institutional continuity from enslaved Muslims to the constitutional founding without evidence.

## 10. A remarkable image for the eventual book
There is a potential narrative juxtaposition:

In elite political rooms, Americans debated whether a theoretical 'Mahometan' might someday occupy the presidency.

Elsewhere in the same American world, actual Muslims prayed under slavery, wrote Arabic, attempted to maintain dietary and fasting practices, and struggled to preserve their faith.

The hypothetical Muslim had constitutional possibilities the real Muslim could not exercise.

That tension belongs near the center of the story.

## 11. Evidence discipline
### Directly established
- Muslims lived in colonial and early United States society.
- Many arrived involuntarily through the Atlantic slave trade.
- Ayuba Suleiman Diallo practiced Islam in colonial Maryland.
- Yarrow Mamout openly professed Islam and later became a free Georgetown homeowner/investor.
- Omar Ibn Said was a highly educated Muslim enslaved in the Carolinas and wrote an Arabic autobiography in 1831.
- Bilali Muhammad was an enslaved Muslim Arabic writer on Sapelo Island.
- Some enslaved Muslims maintained religious practice and Arabic literacy despite coercion.

### Do not overclaim
- Exact percentage of enslaved Africans who were Muslim remains uncertain; estimates vary and records are incomplete.
- Do not say the Founders personally knew enslaved Muslims unless individual evidence establishes it.
- Do not claim enslaved Muslims directly influenced the Constitution without a documented transmission chain.
- Do not treat every Arabic-speaking enslaved African as Muslim.
- Do not describe outward Christian practice by enslaved Muslims as evidence for modern conspiracy claims about Muslim deception.
- Do not assert a specific jurisprudential school/content for the Bilali manuscript until specialist scholarship is separately verified.

## 12. Evidence grades for the book
A — Muslim presence in colonial/early America; Omar manuscript; Yarrow's documented Muslim identity; Ayuba's Muslim identity; Bilali's Muslim identity and Arabic manuscript.

B — Broader estimates of the scale of the enslaved Muslim population and reconstruction of community/network practices, depending on source.

C — Interpretations of coded resistance in individual Arabic writings unless directly stated or supported by strong specialist consensus.

E — Any claim that these Muslims directly authored, supplied, or caused constitutional provisions without evidence.

## 14. Pass Six conclusion
The question is no longer whether the founding generation could imagine Muslims in America. Muslims were already in America.

They were praying in Maryland, speaking and writing Arabic, carrying Qur'anic learning across the Atlantic, preserving Islamic practice under enslavement, becoming free property owners, and leaving manuscripts that survive today.

The founding contradiction is therefore sharper than either a patriotic myth or an anti-American myth allows:

> America developed a constitutional language capable of protecting Muslim conscience while an American system of slavery denied freedom to actual Muslims living on its soil.

That contradiction should not be hidden. It may be the most powerful evidence of why constitutional principles have to be judged not only by who first announced them, but by whether later generations are willing to apply them to people the original society failed to protect.
