# Pass 24 — Islamic Law Specialist Dossier

## Purpose

This dossier creates the book's internal reference standard for Islamic law before Steve conducts the Central Arkansas interviews.

The goal is not to produce “the Islamic answer.” The goal is to prevent the manuscript from collapsing distinct categories—divine ideal, jurisprudence, personal devotion, judicial doctrine, political theory, modern legislation, and extremist ideology—into the single English phrase “Sharia law.”

This pass is deliberately adversarial. It preserves doctrines that create genuine conflicts with modern American constitutional norms while also preserving disagreement, historical context, interpretive diversity, and the transformation of Islamic law under the modern nation-state.

Primary baseline sources for this pass include the Law Library of Congress overview of Sharia and Islamic law, Library of Congress manuscript collections, Harvard Law School's Program in Islamic Law and scholarship by scholars including Intisar Rabb and Noah Feldman, plus comparative scholarship on contested doctrines such as apostasy.

This dossier is a research framework, not a substitute for specialist review. Chapters 1–5 and 22 should receive qualified Islamic-law review before publication.

---

# 1. THE FIRST RULE: SHARIA IS NOT A SINGLE STATUTE BOOK

A defensible introductory formulation is:

**Sharia is the broad Islamic conception of God's guidance for human life. Fiqh is the human jurisprudential effort to understand and formulate rules from that guidance.**

The Law Library of Congress describes Sharia broadly as the totality of divine commands and exhortations intended to regulate human conduct and guide believers. It also makes an essential distinction: legal rules are part of Sharia, but not everything within Sharia is “law” in the modern state-law sense.

After Muhammad's death, jurists developed methods for deriving legal rules. Their work became fiqh—jurisprudence—and developed into multiple schools.

Therefore:

> “Sharia says X” is often an incomplete sentence.

The necessary follow-up questions are:

- Which source?
- Which school?
- Which jurist?
- Which century?
- Which jurisdiction?
- Is this devotional, ethical, juridical, judicial, or political?
- Is it classical jurisprudence or a modern state code?
- Is the speaker describing an ideal, a rule, an interpretation, or actual enforcement?

# 2. CORE VOCABULARY CONTRACT

These terms should be used consistently throughout the book.

## Sharia / Sharīʿa

Broadly, the divinely ordained path/guidance in Islamic thought.

It encompasses more than what an American lawyer means by “law.” It can include worship, ethics, family relations, commercial conduct, ritual, diet, charity, and legal rules.

**Do not automatically translate Sharia as “Islamic criminal law.”**

## Fiqh

Human jurisprudence: the body/process of juristic understanding of Islamic legal norms.

Fiqh is fallible human interpretation even when jurists believe they are attempting to understand divine guidance.

This distinction is essential to the book because political rhetoric often treats a historically plural jurisprudential tradition as if it were one enacted code.

## Faqīh / Fuqahāʾ

A jurist / jurists trained in fiqh.

## Uṣūl al-fiqh

The jurisprudential methodology or “roots” used to derive and reason about legal rules.

## Furūʿ al-fiqh

The substantive “branches” or resulting legal doctrines/rules.

## Madhhab

A school of jurisprudence.

The four major surviving Sunni schools are Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i, and Hanbali. Shi'a jurisprudence has its own traditions, with Twelver Ja'fari jurisprudence especially important.

The Library of Congress notes that the four Sunni schools historically developed distinctive interpretive methodologies and became influential in different regions while recognizing one another as authoritative Sunni schools.

**Do not write as though there is one Sunni rule on every question.**

## Fatwa

A nonbinding juristic opinion/advisory response issued by a qualified scholar or mufti in response to a legal-religious question.

A fatwa is not inherently equivalent to a court judgment, statute, executive order, or criminal sentence.

## Mufti

A jurist qualified to issue fatwas.

## Qadi / Qāḍī

A judge operating within an Islamic judicial framework.

Historically, qadis could adjudicate disputes using jurisprudential doctrine, but their appointment, jurisdiction, institutional independence, and relationship to political rulers varied enormously across place and period.

A qadi is **not** simply the Islamic equivalent of an Article III federal judge.

## Ijtihad

Juristic exertion/reasoning used to derive or determine rules from recognized sources and interpretive methods.

The Law Library of Congress describes ijtihad as the effort of translating divine commands and exhortations into specific legal rules.

Ijtihad is not judicial review.

## Taqlid

Following/working within established juristic authority or school doctrine rather than independently deriving law from foundational sources.

The simplistic story that “the gates of ijtihad closed” permanently across the Islamic world should not be repeated without specialist qualification; modern scholarship has substantially complicated that older narrative.

## Ijmaʿ

Consensus in Islamic jurisprudential theory, with major disagreement over whose consensus, how established, and its authority.

Ijma is **not popular voting** and should never be described as an Islamic version of an election.

## Qiyas

Analogical reasoning: extending a rule from an established case to another case based on a legally relevant common rationale, with school-specific differences.

## Maslaha

Public interest/welfare reasoning used within parts of Islamic jurisprudence, particularly associated with questions where jurists consider benefits and harms under recognized methodological constraints.

Maslaha is not the U.S. Constitution's General Welfare Clause.

## Maqasid al-Sharia

The objectives/purposes of Sharia.

Later juristic theory famously articulated protected interests often summarized as religion, life, intellect, lineage/family, and property, with modern scholars expanding/reframing maqasid toward dignity, freedom, rights, development, and justice.

The familiar five-objective list is a juristic formulation, not a literal Qur'anic five-item constitutional provision.

## Siyasa / Siyasa shar'iyya

Governance/political-administrative authority and policy, including theories of ruler action compatible with or serving Sharia.

This category is crucial because premodern Muslim governance was not simply “jurists write every rule and ruler enforces the Qur'an.” Political rulers issued regulations and exercised administrative power alongside juristic institutions.

## Qanun / Qānūn

State/ruler-made regulation or positive law, particularly important in Ottoman and modern contexts.

The historical relationship among qanun, Sharia, fiqh, ruler authority, and courts varied.

## Hisba

A historically variable institution/concept concerned with commanding right, preventing wrong, market regulation, public morality, and administrative oversight.

It can contain elements that resemble market inspection or public accountability, but also morality enforcement.

**Do not romanticize hisba as an Islamic inspector general.**

## Bay'a / Bayʿa

An oath/pledge of allegiance associated with political legitimacy.

Bay'a is not a modern democratic election, though modern writers may interpret consultation/consent traditions in democratic ways.

## Shura / Shūrā

Consultation. Qur'an 42:38 praises those whose affairs are conducted by mutual consultation.

Shura can support Islamic arguments for consultative or democratic government, but **shura is not Congress** and does not itself establish universal suffrage, representative districts, separation of powers, or modern electoral democracy.

# 3. SOURCES OF ISLAMIC JURISPRUDENCE

A simplified Sunni framework commonly identifies:

1. Qur'an;
2. Sunnah/Hadith;
3. ijma (consensus);
4. qiyas (analogy);

with additional methodologies varying among schools, including maslaha, istihsan (juristic preference/equitable reasoning in certain traditions), custom ('urf), presumptions, and other doctrines.

Shi'a jurisprudential methodology differs in important respects and must not be treated as merely another Sunni madhhab.

The sources do not mechanically produce a single answer. Jurists disagree about authenticity, textual meaning, general/specific rules, abrogation, analogy, necessity, public interest, custom, evidentiary standards, and how sources interact.

This is why fiqh became a jurisprudential tradition rather than a single code.

# 4. WHAT CLASSICAL FIQH ACTUALLY COVERED

The Library of Congress's Islamic-law collections themselves demonstrate the range.

Classical works include doctrine concerning:

- ritual purity;
- prayer;
- fasting;
- pilgrimage;
- marriage;
- divorce;
- inheritance;
- commercial transactions;
- contracts;
- debt;
- evidence;
- litigation;
- settlements;
- taxation;
- property;
- slavery/manumission;
- warfare;
- crime and punishment;
- public administration.

A Library of Congress manuscript of the Hanafi *Kanz al-daqa'iq*, for example, includes ritual life, marriage, divorce, inheritance, contracts, commercial transactions, and manumission.

This produces a useful corrective:

> A person who hears “Sharia” and thinks only of amputation or stoning is hearing one narrow and politically amplified portion of a much larger legal-religious civilization.

That observation does **not** erase the criminal doctrines. It puts them in proportion.

# 5. THE MODERN-STATE PROBLEM

One of the book's most important distinctions is between **premodern juristic law** and **modern centralized state enforcement**.

Historically, Islamic jurisprudence developed through jurists, schools, legal texts, fatwas, courts, ruler regulations, local practice, and multiple overlapping institutions.

The modern nation-state tends to demand:

- centralized legislation;
- standardized codes;
- bureaucratic enforcement;
- appellate hierarchies;
- state-selected judges;
- territorially uniform rules.

Harvard's Program in Islamic Law highlights scholarly debates over how colonialism, administrative centralization, codification, and postcolonial states transformed Sharia and Islamic legal institutions. Modern governments sometimes select one juristic interpretation and convert it into positive statutory law, which can produce something institutionally different from classical plural fiqh.

This matters enormously.

A modern statute labeled “Sharia” may be:

1. based on a particular school;
2. selectively codified by a legislature;
3. interpreted by state judges;
4. shaped by colonial or European legal forms;
5. combined with modern criminal/administrative law;
6. presented politically as “God's law.”

The resulting system should not automatically be projected backward as “what Sharia has always been.”

Nor should classical jurisprudence be romanticized simply because modern codification changed it.

# 6. SHARIA IN MODERN CONSTITUTIONS

Modern Muslim-majority states use Islamic constitutional language in dramatically different ways.

Some constitutions identify Islam as the state religion.

Some describe Sharia or Islamic principles as “a source” of legislation.

Some describe it as “the” or “a principal” source.

Some prohibit legislation contrary to Islamic norms.

Some maintain specialized Sharia courts, often with limited personal-status jurisdiction; others integrate Islamic rules into national courts and codes.

Intisar Rabb's work on Islamic constitutionalism in Iraq emphasizes the institutional problem created when a written constitution simultaneously invokes Islamic law, democratic institutions, and rights guarantees: **who interprets the Islamic-law constraint, and how does that interpreter relate to elected institutions and courts?**

This is a far better question than simply asking whether a constitution “has Sharia.”

The constitutional danger from an American perspective appears most clearly where a religious norm becomes a legally supreme constraint interpreted through governmental power in ways inconsistent with protected civil rights.

# 7. THE HARD CASES

The book must neither hide nor flatten these.

## APOSTASY (RIDDA)

### Historical baseline

Major strands of classical Islamic jurisprudence treated apostasy by a legally accountable Muslim as a public/legal offense and developed severe sanctions, including capital punishment under specified conditions. Details varied by school, gender, repentance opportunity, mental capacity, public conduct, and other factors.

Comparative scholarship confirms that the majority classical position associated apostasy with capital punishment, while modern Muslim scholars vigorously dispute the textual basis, historical rationale, and modern applicability.

### Critical textual distinction

The Qur'an repeatedly condemns unbelief and apostasy religiously and speaks of divine judgment, while the direct temporal death penalty is not laid out in the Qur'an as a simple penal statute for every private change of belief.

The classical death doctrine developed principally through hadith, juristic reasoning, and the historical political/legal understanding of ridda.

### Modern reform argument

Some modern scholars argue that classical apostasy rules were tied to political betrayal, rebellion, wartime defection, or the fusion of religious membership and political allegiance in early Muslim society—not private conscience as understood by modern religious-freedom law.

Others defend substantial parts of the classical doctrine.

### Safe book formulation

> Classical Islamic jurisprudence contains a serious apostasy doctrine that can conflict directly with modern freedom of religion when treated as a state-enforced punishment for changing belief. Muslim scholars today disagree sharply over whether the classical rule was correctly understood, historically contingent, politically grounded, or religiously binding in modern states.

### Constitutional conflict

An American government punishing a person merely for leaving Islam would violate core constitutional religious liberty.

A Muslim's religious liberty necessarily includes another person's civil right not to be Muslim.

This is one of the book's strongest symmetry tests.

## BLASPHEMY

Islamic jurisprudential traditions developed sanctions for insults to God, Muhammad, the Qur'an, and sacred matters, with substantial school/jurisdictional variation and overlap with apostasy doctrines.

Modern Muslim-majority states differ dramatically in blasphemy legislation and enforcement.

### Safe formulation

> Severe blasphemy doctrines are real parts of Islamic legal history and remain legally consequential in some modern states. They are not compatible with an American governmental power to punish religious criticism merely because it is sacrilegious.

Do not imply every Muslim supports such laws.

Do not imply the doctrine is fictional.

---

## HUDUD / ḤUDŪD

Hudud refers to a category of offenses/punishments traditionally understood as having specially fixed divine limits/sanctions, though juristic classification and application require careful treatment.

Frequently discussed offenses include theft, unlawful sexual intercourse (zina), false accusation of zina (qadhf), highway robbery, and alcohol consumption in various juristic treatments; apostasy is classified differently across juristic works and should not casually be placed into one universal hudud list.

### Evidentiary/avoidance doctrine

Classical jurists developed demanding evidentiary rules and doctrines of doubt (shubha) that could prevent hudud application. Modern scholarship, including work associated with Intisar Rabb, emphasizes juristic doubt and avoidance doctrines.

### Do not make the apologetic mistake

High evidentiary barriers do not make corporal punishment compatible with the U.S. Constitution.

### Modern debate

Harvard's 2025 *Journal of Islamic Law* special issue on hudud demonstrates active contemporary scholarly debate over moratoriums, suspension, abolition, constitutional grounding, and modern enforcement across countries including Indonesia, Iran, Morocco, Pakistan, and Saudi Arabia.

### Safe formulation

> Hudud punishments are not inventions of anti-Muslim propaganda. They are genuine parts of classical Islamic criminal jurisprudence. Their interpretation, evidentiary application, suspension, codification, and legitimacy in modern states are deeply contested. State-enforced corporal punishments can create direct conflicts with modern American constitutional norms.

---

## JIHAD

### Essential correction

Jihad cannot honestly be reduced either to:

- “holy war,” or
- “inner spiritual struggle only.”

The Arabic root concerns striving/exertion. Islamic religious discourse has used jihad in multiple moral and spiritual senses, while classical jurisprudence also developed an unmistakably armed/military doctrine of jihad.

Historical legal literature includes both defensive and expansionary/imperial frameworks, rules governing warfare, treaties, protected populations, truces, and political authority.

### Safe formulation

> Jihad is a multivalent Islamic concept that includes moral/religious striving but also possesses a genuine military history and classical legal doctrine. Acknowledging the military doctrine does not establish that an individual Muslim who speaks of jihad means violence; denying the military doctrine is historically indefensible.

### Constitutional boundary

Religious belief about warfare does not create private authority to violate American criminal law or independently wage war.

Political advocacy, theology, and speech remain different categories from criminal violence.

---

## GENDER AND FAMILY LAW

Islamic jurisprudence historically provided women with recognized legal capacities in areas such as property ownership, dower, inheritance, marriage rights, and access to legal processes relative to many surrounding historical systems.

It also developed **sex-differentiated rules** in areas including:

- inheritance;
- marriage authority;
- divorce mechanisms;
- maintenance;
- testimony in some contexts;
- guardianship;
- polygyny;
- family roles.

Modern Muslim thinkers and states respond differently: retain, reinterpret, equalize, codify, reform, or reject particular classical rules.

Saudi Arabia's 2022 family legislation, for example, is a modern codified family-law instrument based on interpretations of Islamic law and illustrates how contemporary states translate jurisprudence into statutory form.

### Safe formulation

> Islamic legal history contains both meaningful legal rights for women and status distinctions incompatible with a modern principle of identical civil citizenship if imposed by the American state. Both propositions can be true.

The interviews with Muslim women must determine how these doctrines are actually understood and lived in Arkansas rather than assuming either oppression or harmony.

---

## DHIMMA / NON-MUSLIM STATUS

Classical Muslim polities developed protected status frameworks for certain non-Muslim communities, particularly Jews and Christians, allowing substantial communal/religious life while imposing differentiated political, fiscal, and legal status.

This was historically significant protection in many contexts but **was not modern equal citizenship**.

### Safe formulation

> Dhimmi status could protect religious minorities within premodern Islamic empires while simultaneously treating them as legally unequal to Muslims. Calling it either simple “tolerance” or simple “persecution” without context loses the structure.

### Constitutional boundary

A modern American state cannot assign diminished citizenship based on being Christian, Jewish, Muslim, Hindu, atheist, or another religion.

---

## SLAVERY

Classical Islamic jurisprudence regulated slavery rather than abolishing it, while also encouraging or requiring manumission in various circumstances and recognizing legal protections unavailable in some other slave systems.

The Qur'an and fiqh emerged in a slaveholding world.

This should be treated with the same historical honesty used when discussing biblical slavery and American Christian slaveholding.

Do not turn comparative slavery into competitive moral accounting.

# 8. SHARIA AND PERSONAL RELIGIOUS LIFE

For many Muslims, the parts of Sharia encountered most frequently are not state punishment or constitutional theory.

They may include:

- prayer;
- fasting;
- dietary rules;
- charity/zakat;
- family obligations;
- honesty in contracts;
- debt and finance;
- sexual ethics;
- dress/modesty;
- burial;
- religious holidays;
- inheritance planning;
- marriage;
- moral conduct.

This is why “Do you follow Sharia?” is often a poor interview question unless the speaker defines what they mean.

A Muslim may answer yes while meaning “I pray, fast, give charity, eat halal, avoid interest, and try to live ethically.”

Another may mean support for Islamic jurisprudence in family matters.

Another may advocate Islamic constitutional government.

Those are politically and legally different answers.

# 9. FATWA IS NOT A COURT ORDER

This distinction deserves explicit treatment because popular rhetoric frequently describes religious opinions as parallel sovereign law.

A fatwa is ordinarily an advisory juristic opinion.

A Muslim may seek a fatwa concerning:

- whether a financial product is permissible;
- fasting during illness;
- divorce;
- inheritance;
- medical treatment;
- prayer;
- diet;
- ethical conduct.

Its civil legal effect depends on the surrounding legal system and actions of individuals/institutions—not merely on its status as a fatwa.

In the United States, an imam or scholar does not acquire governmental jurisdiction simply by issuing a religious opinion.

The analogous constitutional question is not “Does the religion have rules?” Christianity, Judaism, and other traditions plainly do.

The question is **who possesses coercive civil authority.**

---

# 10. RELIGIOUS ARBITRATION IS NOT SOVEREIGNTY

Muslims, Jews, Christians, businesses, unions, and private parties may sometimes use voluntary arbitration or mediation under American law.

Religious motivations or norms can inform private dispute resolution subject to ordinary legal limits.

This does not create a parallel sovereign government.

The relevant questions include:

- Was participation voluntary?
- Is there an enforceable arbitration agreement?
- Can a secular court enforce the award without deciding theological truth?
- Does enforcement violate public policy or statutory rights?
- Was there fraud, duress, incapacity, or another ordinary contract defense?

Pass 23 controls the American-law side of this question.

Pass 24 controls the terminology side.

---

# 11. MAQASID, MASLAHA, AND MODERN REFORM

Modern reform-minded Muslim thinkers often draw upon maqasid (objectives), maslaha (public welfare), contextual interpretation, Qur'anic moral principles, and renewed ijtihad to argue for:

- religious freedom;
- democratic government;
- constitutionalism;
- equal citizenship;
- women's rights;
- human rights;
- suspension/rejection of classical corporal punishment;
- separation between religious authority and coercive state enforcement.

This is not the only modern Islamic position.

It is also not necessarily an invention detached from Islamic jurisprudence; reformers commonly argue from within inherited Islamic concepts.

The manuscript should identify the thinker and argument rather than writing “Islam teaches democracy.”

---

# 12. MODERN ISLAMIC CONSTITUTIONALISM

The question “Is Islam compatible with democracy?” is too crude.

Better questions:

- Who possesses sovereignty?
- Who interprets religious law?
- Can elected lawmakers legislate contrary to juristic doctrine?
- Can courts invalidate laws as un-Islamic?
- Which court?
- Which interpretation of Islam?
- Are non-Muslims equal citizens?
- Can women hold every office?
- Can citizens change religion?
- Can religious doctrine be criticized?
- Are individual rights enforceable against a religious majority?

Modern Islamic constitutional arrangements answer these differently.

Intisar Rabb's scholarship demonstrates that once Islamic law becomes a constitutional source, the institutional relationship between jurists, judges, legislatures, and democratic sovereignty becomes decisive.

Noah Feldman's work argues that classical Islamic governance sometimes constrained rulers through a juristic legal class outside direct executive control, while modern state centralization changed that balance. This is an important scholarly thesis, not an uncontested description of every Islamic polity.

---

# 13. THE “RULE OF LAW” PARALLEL

Islamic civilization developed a powerful ideal that rulers themselves were constrained by law and religious/legal norms rather than possessing unlimited personal legislative authority.

This can legitimately be studied as part of the history of constitutionalism and rule-of-law thought.

But:

> Islamic rule-of-law traditions are not the U.S. Constitution.

They lacked many defining institutions of modern American constitutionalism, including the same conception of popular sovereignty, equal citizenship, religious neutrality, bicameral federal legislature, written rights enforcement, Article III judiciary, and constitutional amendment structure.

Use as comparison, not ancestry.

---

# 14. WHAT THE ANTI-SHARIA CASE GETS RIGHT

A serious critic is correct that:

1. Islamic jurisprudence contains historical doctrines of corporal punishment.
2. major classical traditions developed severe apostasy rules.
3. blasphemy laws remain consequential in some modern Muslim-majority states.
4. classical family law contains gender distinctions.
5. classical Muslim governance did not embody modern religion-neutral equal citizenship.
6. jihad possesses a military/legal history.
7. some modern political movements seek state enforcement of their interpretation of Islamic law.
8. some modern constitutions give Islamic law privileged/supreme legal status.
9. such state-enforced doctrines can conflict with American constitutional rights.

The book should concede all nine without hesitation where properly sourced.

---

# 15. WHAT THE ANTI-SHARIA CASE OFTEN GETS WRONG

It becomes unreliable when it assumes:

1. Sharia means only criminal punishment.
2. every Muslim follows one legal school identically.
3. classical doctrine automatically reveals an individual American Muslim's political intent.
4. a fatwa is equivalent to a government order.
5. voluntary religious arbitration is a rival sovereign court system.
6. prayer, halal observance, fasting, zakat, Islamic finance, and family ethics are evidence of a plan to replace the Constitution.
7. all Islamic jurisprudence is immutable.
8. Muslim reformers are automatically insincere.
9. “taqiyya” makes any Muslim denial evidence of deception.
10. every invocation of Sharia means support for the Taliban, Iran, Saudi Arabia, ISIS, or any other particular government/movement.

# 16. THE TAQIYYA PROBLEM

Taqiyya historically concerns concealment of belief under threat/persecution, especially significant in Shi'a history, though broader Islamic doctrines concerning concealment under coercion exist.

Modern anti-Muslim rhetoric sometimes transforms taqiyya into a universal claim that Muslims are religiously commanded to lie to non-Muslims about political intentions.

That formulation creates an unfalsifiable conspiracy theory:

- if a Muslim says they want constitutional democracy, they are lying;
- if they deny wanting theocracy, the denial proves taqiyya;
- therefore no testimony can disconfirm the accusation.

The book should distinguish legitimate historical theology from this modern conspiratorial use.

Specialist verification is required before final wording.

# 17. SHARIA AND AMERICAN CONSTITUTIONALISM — THE CLEAN MODEL

The book should use four categories.

## Category A — Protected individual religious exercise

Examples may include:

- prayer;
- fasting;
- halal diet;
- religious dress;
- charity;
- worship;
- teaching doctrine;
- voluntary religious counseling.

Constitutional/statutory protection depends on context under Pass 23.

## Category B — Protected religious/political advocacy

A Muslim may argue that American law should reflect Islamic moral principles, just as Christians, Jews, atheists, and others may advocate policies grounded in their convictions, subject to ordinary law.

Religious motivation does not itself invalidate political participation.

## Category C — Private arrangements with civil-law consequences

Marriage contracts, wills, finance, mediation, arbitration, and other arrangements may interact with ordinary American contract/family/property law.

Their enforceability is determined by American civil law, not simply because a religious authority declares them valid.

## Category D — Coercive religious sovereignty

A government cannot simply punish apostasy, enforce religious observance, create religious qualifications for citizenship/office contrary to constitutional protections, or subordinate constitutional rights to a religious authority merely because adherents call the rule Sharia.

The same principle applies to Christian, Jewish, or other religious sovereignty.

# 20. INTERVIEW LISTENING GUIDE

When an Arkansas Muslim says:

> “I follow Sharia.”

Do not interpret it.

Ask:

> “What does following Sharia mean in your life this week?”

When they say:

> “Islam says...”

Ask:

> “Is that something Muslims generally agree on, your school/community's position, or your own interpretation?”

When they say:

> “That's not Sharia.”

Ask:

> “Would a serious Muslim scholar disagree with you? What would their argument be?”

When they say:

> “The Constitution protects us.”

Ask:

> “What happens if your religious understanding conflicts with American civil law?”

When they say:

> “Islam guarantees freedom of religion.”

Ask:

> “Does that include the legal right of a Muslim to become Christian or atheist?”

When they say:

> “Jihad doesn't mean violence.”

Ask:

> “How do you understand the military doctrine of jihad in classical jurisprudence?”

When they say:

> “Hudud was never really applied.”

Ask:

> “Do you believe the punishment itself is legitimate in principle, or do you reject it?”

These follow-ups distinguish a real interview from apologetics.

# 21. HOW THIS PASS CHANGES THE BOOK

Pass 24 strengthens the architecture in five ways.

## First

The book no longer needs to answer the impossible question “Is Sharia good or bad?”

It can ask which Sharia, which interpretation, which institution, and which civil consequence.

## Second

The hardest doctrines can be acknowledged without turning them into collective accusations against American Muslims.

## Third

Ordinary Muslim observance can be described accurately without pretending the hard jurisprudential history does not exist.

## Fourth

The constitutional issue becomes institutional rather than theological:

> Who possesses coercive civil authority?

## Fifth

The interviews now have a specialist vocabulary against which unexpected answers can be recognized and investigated.

# 22. BOOK THESIS AFTER PASS 24

The strongest formulation is no longer:

> Sharia is harmless.

That is unsustainable.

Nor is it:

> Sharia is incompatible with America.

That is too categorical to describe a tradition that includes prayer, charity, contracts, family ethics, jurisprudence, political theory, and historically coercive doctrines.

The stronger formulation is:

> **Sharia is a vast, internally contested religious-legal tradition. Much of what an American Muslim may call Sharia belongs to the ordinary exercise of religion. Some historical and modern interpretations contain doctrines that would violate American constitutional rights if imposed through government. The American constitutional answer does not require deciding which interpretation is “true Islam.” It protects conscience while reserving coercive civil sovereignty to constitutional law.**

That proposition is strong enough to protect the Muslim who prays according to Sharia.

It is strong enough to protect the Christian who fears Islamic government.

It is strong enough to protect the former Muslim who rejects Sharia entirely.

And it is strong enough to tell any religious majority—Muslim or Christian—that sincere belief in divine law does not by itself confer governmental jurisdiction over everyone else's conscience.
