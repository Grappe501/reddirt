# Pass 21 — Central Arkansas Muslim Interview Field Dossier

## Mission

This is the operational reporting manual for the human phase of the Sharia / religious-liberty project.

The interviews are not being conducted to find Muslims who will validate a thesis. They are being conducted to discover what Muslims in Central Arkansas actually believe, how they live, where they disagree, what they fear, what they think Americans misunderstand, and how their religious commitments interact with American constitutional citizenship.

The interviews must be capable of changing the book.

This dossier is designed so Steve Grappe can enter the Central Arkansas Muslim community, explain the project honestly, recruit a deliberately diverse group, record comparable testimony, pursue unexpected leads, and leave with material rigorous enough for Chapters 21–26 and the Epilogue.

---

# 1. THE REPORTING PRINCIPLE

Do not ask any Muslim to “represent Islam.”

Ask each person to represent themselves.

Every interview should distinguish among:

- personal belief;
- personal practice;
- interpretation of Islam;
- description of Islamic history or law;
- description of other Muslims;
- political preference;
- constitutional/legal claim;
- personal experience.

When a participant says “Islam says,” “Sharia says,” “Muslims believe,” or “the Qur'an says,” follow with:

> Is that your understanding, the position of your school or community, or something you believe Muslims generally agree on?

This one follow-up will prevent enormous evidentiary problems later.

---

# 2. SAMPLE DESIGN

## Minimum viable reporting sample

Do not close the Muslim reporting phase with fewer than approximately **12 substantive Muslim interviews**, unless access or safety problems make that impossible.

The minimum sample should include:

- mosque/imam leadership;
- Black American Muslim;
- immigrant Muslim;
- second-generation Muslim;
- convert;
- at least three Muslim women across differing backgrounds/views;
- young adult/student;
- parent;
- conservative/traditional Muslim;
- reform/progressive or interpretively different Muslim;
- professional/business/community voice.

Some individuals may satisfy several categories.

Additionally seek, outside that Muslim sample:

- at least one former Muslim/internal critic;
- one serious Islamic-law scholar;
- one conservative Christian skeptic;
- one Christian religious-liberty defender;
- one constitutional/religious-liberty lawyer.

## Strong reporting sample

**20–30 substantive interviews** across the full project.

The goal is not statistical representation. It is **perspective saturation**: continue interviewing until major themes are repeating and meaningful disagreements have been captured rather than accidentally excluded.

# 3. PRIORITY RECRUITMENT ORDER

## Tier One — Open the community

### A. Senior mosque/community leader

Purpose:

- explain the project;
- establish trust;
- learn community history;
- identify diverse participants;
- understand leadership's view of Sharia and citizenship;
- ask for introductions rather than a curated list of only favorable voices.

Important request:

> I don't only want people who agree with you or with me. I need people of different ages, backgrounds, levels of observance and viewpoints, including people who may be uncomfortable with some of my questions.

### B. Community historian / longest-tenured member

Purpose:

Reconstruct Central Arkansas Muslim history, early prayer groups, mosques, Black Muslim communities, immigrant communities, interfaith relationships, Second Presbyterian history, institutional growth, discrimination, and changing public attitudes.

### C. Women community connectors

Do not let male mosque leadership determine the entire female sample.

Ask women separately whom Steve should speak with.

### D. Black American Muslim connector

Essential to prevent Arkansas Muslim history from beginning with immigration.

# 4. TARGET INTERVIEW ROLES

## Essential Muslim roles

### 1. Imam / senior religious leader

Need: theology, jurisprudence, Sharia vocabulary, institutional history, American citizenship, hard cases.

### 2. Longtime Central Arkansas Muslim

Need: local history and change over decades.

### 3. Black American Muslim

Need: American Muslim history outside immigrant narrative; race, religion, belonging, civil rights.

### 4. Immigrant Muslim

Need: comparison between country of origin and American religious liberty without assuming either is better in every respect.

### 5. Second-generation American Muslim

Need: Islam as American identity rather than immigrant adaptation.

### 6. Convert to Islam

Need: freedom to enter religion; family reaction; discovery of Sharia; American religious marketplace.

### 7–9. Muslim women — minimum three where possible

Seek diversity:

- hijabi/non-hijabi;
- immigrant/American-born;
- conservative/reform;
- married/single;
- professional/parent/student.

Need: lived religion, gender, family law, autonomy, stereotype testing.

### 10. Young Muslim / college-age adult

Need: schools, social media, identity, peer attitudes, politics, future.

### 11. Muslim parent

Need: raising Muslim children in Arkansas, schools, holidays, religious education, belonging.

### 12. Conservative/traditional Muslim

Need: strongest internally conservative interpretation. Do not build sample entirely from Muslims whose views fit liberal constitutional expectations.

### 13. Reform/progressive Muslim

Need: internal disagreement and interpretive change.

### 14. Muslim attorney or legally sophisticated participant

Need: Sharia/U.S. law distinction, contracts, family matters, arbitration, constitutional rights.

### 15. Muslim physician/business owner/professional

Need: ordinary ethical Sharia in work, money, charity, scheduling, food, prayer.

### 16. Interfaith participant

Need: lived Christian-Muslim relationship and Arkansas interfaith history.

## Essential non-Muslim/adjacent roles

### 17. Former Muslim / internal critic

Need: exit rights, family/community pressure, doctrinal criticism, what religious liberty means after belief ends.

Safety is more important than attribution.

### 18. Islamic-law scholar

Need: specialist correction of claims emerging from interviews.

This person does not need to be in Arkansas.

### 19. Conservative Christian skeptic

Need: strongest serious fear of Sharia/Islam from inside Arkansas Christianity.

### 20. Christian religious-liberty defender

Need: a Christian theological case for protecting Muslim liberty.

### 21. Arkansas constitutional/religious-liberty lawyer

Need: doctrinal boundaries.

### 22. Holt source

Participant, attorney, prison-religion scholar, or person capable of reconstructing the human/legal story.

# 5. INTERVIEW OPENING / CONSENT SCRIPT

Use conversationally rather than mechanically.

> I'm researching a book about Sharia, religious liberty, Islam and the American constitutional tradition. I began this project with assumptions that the research has already forced me to change. I'm not interviewing you because I expect you to defend Islam or speak for every Muslim. I want to understand your experience and what you actually believe.
>
> Some questions will be ordinary questions about your life and Arkansas. Some will be difficult questions about things like apostasy, blasphemy, jihad, women, religious government and the Constitution. You can decline any question.
>
> I'd like to record our conversation so I can quote you accurately. Are you comfortable with that? And before we begin, should this conversation be on the record with your name, on background, or do we need to discuss another attribution arrangement?

Record the consent response.

Do not promise quotation approval. It is reasonable to offer factual verification of names, dates, biography, Arabic terminology, or complicated doctrinal descriptions.

# 6. UNIVERSAL MUSLIM INTERVIEW — CORE QUESTIONS

These should be asked, in substantially comparable form, of nearly every Muslim participant.

Do not fire them like a survey. Follow stories.

## Person first

1. Tell me about yourself before we talk about Islam. Where did you grow up? What brought you to this part of Arkansas?

2. What does an ordinary week in your life look like?

3. How would you describe yourself religiously if I hadn't given you any categories?

4. What role does Islam actually play in your daily life?

## The word Sharia

5. When you hear the word **Sharia**, what does it mean to you?

6. If I followed you around for an ordinary Tuesday, where—if anywhere—would I see Sharia in your life?

7. Is Sharia primarily law to you? Morality? Worship? Guidance? Something else?

8. Who has authority to interpret Sharia for you?

9. Can two sincere Muslims disagree about what Sharia requires?

10. Have you ever disagreed with an imam, scholar, parent, or Muslim community about what Islam requires? What happened?

## America and Arkansas

11. What does being American mean to you?

12. What does being an Arkansan mean to you?

13. Do you ever experience your Muslim and American identities as being in tension?

14. What freedoms in America matter most to your ability to live as a Muslim?

15. Have you ever felt that Arkansas made practicing your religion easier than you expected?

16. Have you ever felt unwelcome, discriminated against, or treated as suspicious because you were Muslim?

17. Have Christians in Arkansas ever surprised you positively?

18. What do you think Muslims misunderstand about Christians here?

19. What do you think Christians here misunderstand about Muslims?

## Constitution and citizenship

20. Should Muslims and Christians have exactly the same civil rights in the United States?

21. Should an atheist have the same civil rights as a Muslim or Christian?

22. Should a Muslim be constitutionally eligible to be President of the United States?

23. Should a Christian be allowed to vote based partly on Christian convictions?

24. Should a Muslim be allowed to vote based partly on Islamic convictions?

25. Is there any circumstance in which a religious command should automatically become American civil law simply because believers consider it God's law?

26. If your understanding of Islamic teaching conflicts with enforceable American law, how do you think a Muslim should respond?

27. Are there circumstances in which you believe American law is morally wrong but you remain legally obligated to follow it? How do you think about that?

## Neighbor question

28. What would you most want a Christian neighbor in rural Arkansas to understand about your faith?

29. What would you want to understand better about that Christian neighbor?

30. What question do you wish non-Muslims would ask you but almost never do?

31. What question are you tired of being asked?

32. What have I failed to ask that I need to understand?

# 7. HARD-CASE MODULE

Transition respectfully:

> I want to ask you the questions I would want someone to ask if they were skeptical of this book. I'm not asking because I assume you believe these things. I'm asking because these are among the reasons Americans say they fear Sharia.

Then proceed.

## Apostasy

1. Should a Muslim have the legal right to stop being Muslim?

2. Should leaving Islam ever be punished by government?

3. Some classical Islamic jurists prescribed severe punishment for apostasy. What is your understanding of that tradition?

4. If your child became Christian, atheist, or left Islam, what would religious faith require of you?

5. Is freedom to leave Islam part of religious liberty?

## Blasphemy

6. Should insulting Islam, Muhammad, the Qur'an, Christianity, Jesus, or any religion ever be a crime merely because it is blasphemous?

7. What should a Muslim do when someone deliberately insults Islam?

## Hudud / criminal punishment

8. What do you understand hudud punishments to be?

9. Do you believe punishments such as flogging, amputation, or stoning should ever be enforced by an American government?

10. If not, how do you reconcile that with the historical jurisprudence?

## Jihad

11. What does jihad mean to you?

12. Does jihad have an armed or military meaning in Islamic history?

13. Who, if anyone, has legitimate authority to declare or conduct armed jihad?

14. Can an individual Muslim ever claim religious authority to use violence contrary to American criminal law?

## Gender

15. Should women and men possess identical civil citizenship and political rights?

16. Can a woman lead a government?

17. Can a woman disagree with her husband on religion?

18. Should a woman have the civil right to divorce?

19. How do you understand Islamic inheritance rules that distinguish between men and women?

20. Which criticisms of Muslim treatment of women do you believe are fair?

## Non-Muslims

21. Should Christians, Jews, atheists, Hindus and Muslims possess identical citizenship under American law?

22. Can a non-Muslim legitimately govern a Muslim citizen?

23. Do you believe the classical dhimmi framework should have any civil legal role in modern America?

## LGBTQ civil rights

24. Muslims disagree strongly about sexuality and gender. Whatever your religious belief, what civil rights should LGBTQ Americans possess?

25. How should a Muslim respond when religious conviction and another person's civil rights appear to conflict?

Do not turn this into a theological litmus test. The book's concern is conscience, civil equality, and governmental coercion.

## Religious government

26. Would you want the United States government to officially declare Islam the true religion?

27. Would you want American laws required to conform to Sharia?

28. If 70 percent of Americans became Muslim, should they be able to make Islamic religious observance legally compulsory?

29. If 70 percent of Arkansas became evangelical Christian, should they be able to make Christian religious observance legally compulsory?

30. What is the constitutional principle that should govern both situations?

## The strongest criticism

31. What interpretation of Islam do **you** consider dangerous?

32. What criticism made by anti-Sharia Americans do you think is legitimate?

33. What criticism do you think is profoundly wrong?

34. Is there anything Muslims themselves need to confront more honestly about Islamic history or jurisprudence?

This final question is especially important.

# 8. IMAM / ISLAMIC SCHOLAR MODULE

Ask after the universal questions.

1. How do you explain the distinction between Sharia and fiqh to someone who knows nothing about Islam?

2. What sources generate Islamic jurisprudence?

3. How much disagreement exists among the major schools?

4. What does an imam in Arkansas actually have authority to do?

5. What does an imam **not** have authority to do?

6. Can your mosque impose a civil legal judgment on someone who refuses its authority?

7. What happens when religious counseling conflicts with civil law?

8. How do you counsel Muslims about obeying American law?

9. Are American Muslims religiously obligated to honor the Constitution or civic agreements under which they live? Explain the reasoning rather than giving me only yes/no.

10. How do you understand apostasy jurisprudence historically and today?

11. How do you understand blasphemy jurisprudence?

12. How do you understand hudud in a modern constitutional state?

13. What is the strongest scholarly Islamic argument for equal citizenship of non-Muslims?

14. What is the strongest Islamic argument against that position?

15. Can Islamic jurisprudence change?

16. What cannot change?

17. Who decides?

18. What do American anti-Sharia activists get right about classical Islamic law?

19. What do they get wrong?

20. If an American Muslim said the Constitution is illegitimate because only Sharia may govern, what would you tell them?

21. What books or scholars should I read who disagree with you?

22. Who in Arkansas should I interview who would challenge your interpretation?

That final question is mandatory.

# 9. MUSLIM WOMEN / FAMILY-LAW MODULE

Do not presume victimhood or harmony.

1. What do outsiders misunderstand about Muslim women?

2. What do Muslim communities sometimes misunderstand about Muslim women?

3. What religious practices are personally important to you?

4. If you wear hijab, why? If you do not, do you feel pressure either way? Ask only where appropriate.

5. Have you experienced pressure from family/community concerning dress, marriage, career, or religious practice?

6. How are marriage decisions made in your family/community?

7. What is a nikah to you?

8. How important is civil marriage in addition to religious marriage?

9. If a Muslim woman wants a divorce and her husband refuses religious cooperation, what should happen?

10. Should civil law always remain available to protect her rights?

11. How do you understand traditional Islamic inheritance distinctions?

12. Do you believe men and women should have identical political and civil citizenship?

13. What part of Islamic gender teaching do you find most beautiful?

14. What part do you find most difficult?

15. Have American stereotypes about Muslim women ever affected you personally?

16. Is there a criticism of Muslim communities' treatment of women that you think Muslims should take more seriously?

2. What institutions or people existed before the current immigrant-founded mosque infrastructure?

3. What role did the Nation of Islam play locally?

4. How did Sunni communities develop?

5. Were Black Muslims and immigrant Muslims institutionally connected? When?

6. Where were the tensions?

7. Where was the solidarity?

# 13. INTERFAITH HISTORY MODULE

1. When did organized Muslim-Christian/Jewish dialogue begin here?

2. Who initiated it?

3. What do you remember about Second Presbyterian Church and Muslim meetings there?

4. Were rooms actually provided for Muslim meetings? When? Who made that decision?

5. Who else was present?

6. Are there church minutes, newsletters, photographs, correspondence, or participants who can verify it?

7. Was there opposition?

8. What did Christians learn from Muslims?

9. What did Muslims learn from Christians?

10. Did 9/11 change those relationships?

11. Did later anti-Sharia politics change them?

12. What relationship exists today?

No historical anecdote from this module becomes fact solely because one person remembers it. Use testimony to locate records and corroborating witnesses.

# 15. CONSERVATIVE CHRISTIAN SKEPTIC MODULE

Find someone thoughtful enough to make the strongest case, not a caricature.

1. What concerns you about Islam?

2. What concerns you specifically about Sharia?

3. Which Islamic doctrines do you believe conflict with American values or law?

4. What evidence most shaped your view?

5. Do you distinguish between an observant Muslim neighbor and an Islamist political movement?

6. Should Muslims have the same religious-liberty protections as Christians?

7. Should Muslims be eligible for every public office, including president?

8. Should a mosque have the same zoning rights as a church?

9. Should a Muslim prisoner receive religious accommodation on the same terms as a Christian prisoner?

10. Should Muslims be able to advocate political policies based on Islamic conviction in the same way Christians advocate policies based on Christian conviction?

11. What would you want the Constitution to protect for you if Muslims became 80 percent of Arkansas voters?

12. What constitutional rule would prevent Islamic theocracy without empowering Christian theocracy?

13. Is America a Christian nation? Define exactly what you mean by that.

14. Should American civil law ever be legally required to conform to the Bible?

15. If yes, who determines the correct biblical interpretation?

16. What Muslim religious practice do you believe Americans should defend even if they dislike Islam?

17. What would change your mind about any part of this subject?

# 22. FIELD NOTES AFTER EVERY INTERVIEW

Immediately after leaving, before memory fades, record:

1. What surprised me?
2. What challenged the thesis?
3. What strengthened the thesis?
4. What did the participant avoid?
5. What did I avoid asking?
6. What emotional moment mattered?
7. What physical detail might reconstruct the scene accurately?
8. What claim needs verification?
9. Who should I interview because of this conversation?
10. What question should be added to future interviews?

The question “What challenged the thesis?” is mandatory.

---

# 23. FOLLOW-UP / VERIFICATION PROTOCOL

After transcription:

- verify spelling of names;
- verify titles and affiliations;
- verify dates;
- verify Arabic terminology;
- identify Qur'an/hadith/legal citations independently;
- verify claims about Islamic jurisprudence with scholarship/specialist;
- verify Arkansas historical anecdotes through documents or additional witnesses;
- separate memory from documentary fact;
- verify legal claims with primary law;
- preserve full context around controversial quotations;
- do not publish a participant's characterization of another person as fact without verification;
- flag statements that create safety concerns;
- re-contact participant for factual clarification when necessary.

A participant may clarify what they meant. That does not mean the participant receives editorial control over the book.

# 24. RECRUITMENT SCRIPT FOR THE MOSQUE LEADER

A useful conversational request:

> I'm trying to build a serious record of Muslim life and belief in Arkansas rather than writing about Muslims from the outside. I need your help finding people, but I don't want only the people who will give me the easiest answers. I want older and younger Muslims, Black American Muslims, immigrants, people born here, converts, women, parents, professionals, traditional Muslims, reform-minded Muslims and people who disagree with one another. I will ask difficult questions, but I will ask them respectfully and in context. I want the book to be strong enough that someone skeptical of Islam cannot dismiss it as propaganda—and fair enough that Muslims recognize themselves as human beings rather than suspects.

Then ask:

> Who are the first five people I should meet?

And:

> Who would disagree with you that I should also meet?

# 25. FIRST-DAY INTERVIEW PLAN

If Steve receives broad access to Central Arkansas Muslim leadership, the strongest first sequence is:

### Interview 1 — Senior mosque/community leader

Establish vocabulary, history, introductions, institutional perspective.

### Interview 2 — Longtime community historian/elder

Build local chronology and identify archives.

### Interview 3 — Black American Muslim elder/community member

Correct the historical frame immediately.

### Interview 4 — Muslim woman community connector

Build female participant network independently.

### Interview 5 — Second-generation/young Muslim

Introduce generational contrast.

Do **not** conduct all leadership interviews first. That risks allowing institutional gatekeepers to define the sample.

# 26. REPORTING SATURATION CHECK

After every five Muslim interviews, review the sample.

Ask:

- Are all participants from one ethnicity?
- Are all highly educated professionals?
- Are all mosque leaders?
- Are women speaking for themselves?
- Are Black American Muslims present?
- Are young Muslims present?
- Are conservative Muslims present?
- Are reform-oriented Muslims present?
- Have we heard disagreement?
- Have we heard criticism of Muslim communities from Muslims?
- Are participants giving identical polished institutional answers?
- Are we missing people less connected to mosque leadership?
- Are we confusing socioeconomic class with Muslim experience?

If the answers reveal imbalance, recruit deliberately to correct it.

# 27. THE QUESTIONS THAT CAN CHANGE THE BOOK

Across the full interview corpus, pay special attention to these questions:

### A.

> When you say Sharia, what do you mean?

If ordinary Arkansas Muslims define it radically differently from the political rhetoric, Chapter 21 becomes powerful.

If they do not, the book must change.

### B.

> Should a Muslim have the legal right to leave Islam?

This tests conscience from inside the tradition.

### C.

> Should American law ever be required to conform to Sharia?

This tests civil sovereignty.

### D.

> What anti-Sharia criticism is legitimate?

This breaks the apologetics frame.

### E.

> What do Muslims need to confront more honestly about Islam?

This surfaces internal criticism.

### F.

> What would you want a Christian neighbor in rural Arkansas to understand about your faith?

This humanizes the constitutional question.

### G.

To the Christian skeptic:

> What constitutional rule would prevent Islamic theocracy without empowering Christian theocracy?

This is one of the book's decisive questions.

### H.

To the former Muslim:

> What should religious liberty protect for a Muslim—and what should it protect for you?

This may become one of the deepest passages in the book.

# 29. WHAT NOT TO DO

Do not ask:

“Why do Muslims support terrorism?”

Ask about the actual claim being investigated.

Do not ask:

“Does Islam oppress women?”

Ask specific questions about marriage, divorce, dress, leadership, inheritance, autonomy, and civil rights.

Do not ask:

“Is Islam a religion of peace?”

The phrase is too vague to produce useful evidence.

Do not treat a polished imam answer as the community answer.

Do not treat one frightening answer as the community answer.

Do not treat one former Muslim's experience as universal.

Do not treat one Muslim woman's experience as universal.

Do not hide contradictory testimony because it damages the argument.

Do not use an interviewee's theological claim as historical fact without verification.

Do not turn disagreement into betrayal.

# 32. FINAL FIELD PRINCIPLE

The project should enter the mosque without asking Muslims to prove that Islam deserves freedom.

That reverses the constitutional order.

Their liberty is not contingent upon giving Steve the correct answers about apostasy, jihad, women, Sharia, or America.

The hard questions matter for understanding belief, political ideas, internal conflict, and the boundary between religious conscience and civil coercion.

But the deeper investigation asks whether a constitutional system can protect people who disagree profoundly about God without giving any one of them ownership of everybody else's conscience.

That is why the Muslim interview, the Christian interview, and the former-Muslim interview ultimately belong in the same book.

And it is why the most important question in the field may turn out not to be:

> What is Sharia?

It may be:

> Who gets to decide what another person's conscience is allowed to believe?
