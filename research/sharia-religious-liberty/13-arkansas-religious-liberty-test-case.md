# Research Pass 13 — Arkansas as the Religious-Liberty Test Case

## Governing question

If Arkansas claims to offer unusually broad protection for religious liberty, does that protection apply with equal force when the protected believer is Muslim?

## Bottom line

Arkansas has unusually strong religious-liberty language on paper. Article 2, section 24 of the Arkansas Constitution declares that all people possess a natural and indefeasible right to worship according to conscience, that no human authority may control or interfere with conscience, and that no preference may be given by law to any religious establishment, denomination, or mode of worship. Article 2, section 25 directs the General Assembly to protect every religious denomination in the peaceable enjoyment of public worship.

Arkansas also has a statutory Religious Freedom Restoration Act and a 2023 Conscience Protection Act that strengthened its protections. The statute uses a compelling-interest / least-restrictive-means test for substantial burdens on religious exercise, commands broad construction in favor of free exercise, and expressly defines protected religious organizations to include churches, synagogues, shrines, mosques, and temples.

That statutory word — **mosque** — matters. Arkansas did not write a Christian-only conscience law. The text itself includes Islamic institutions.

At the same time, Arkansas has recently experienced major church-state litigation in which federal courts found that the state crossed constitutional lines by giving governmental preference to a particular version of the Ten Commandments. In March 2026, a federal district court permanently enjoined enforcement of Act 573 as to the six school-district defendants before it, holding that the law’s mandated government-selected Protestant Ten Commandments display violated the Establishment Clause and plaintiffs’ Free Exercise rights. On March 31, 2026, another federal district court held that Arkansas’s state-capitol Ten Commandments monument and the statute requiring it violated the Establishment Clause, and also found an Equal Protection violation involving exclusion of a competing religious monument.

Arkansas therefore makes an unusually strong laboratory for the book’s central proposition: **religious liberty is only genuinely universal if it protects the religion the majority does not control.**

## 1. Arkansas Constitution: conscience is framed as a natural right

The Arkansas Constitution of 1874, Article 2, section 24, provides in substance:

- people possess a natural and indefeasible right to worship according to conscience;
- no one may rightly be compelled to attend, erect, or support a place of worship or ministry against consent;
- no human authority may control or interfere with the right of conscience;
- no legal preference may be given to any religious establishment, denomination, or mode of worship above another.

Article 2, section 25 adds that the General Assembly is to enact suitable laws protecting **every religious denomination** in the peaceable enjoyment of public worship.

This is unusually powerful language because it does not merely prohibit one state church. It speaks in terms of conscience, noncompulsion, equality among modes of worship, and protection of every denomination.

### Arkansas appellate interpretation

Arkansas courts have treated this language seriously. In *Thorne v. Arkansas Department of Human Services* (2010), the Arkansas Court of Appeals called Article 2, section 24 an **even more sweeping provision** than the First Amendment in the context before it and emphasized that courts are not instruments of religious orthodoxy.

That framing is extremely useful for this project. The state’s own legal tradition already contains the principle that government should not decide whether a citizen’s religious beliefs are sufficiently conventional.

### Research grade

**A — direct constitutional text and reported Arkansas appellate interpretation.**

## 2. Arkansas RFRA and the 2023 Conscience Protection Act

Arkansas enacted a state Religious Freedom Restoration Act in 2015. In 2023, Act 733 — the Conscience Protection Act — amended and expanded that framework.

The 2023 law defines a compelling governmental interest in demanding terms and provides that government may not substantially burden a person’s exercise of religion, even through a generally applicable rule, unless the burden in the particular instance is essential to further a compelling governmental interest and is the least restrictive means of doing so.

The law defines “exercise of religion” broadly enough to include action motivated by a sincerely held religious belief whether or not that action is compulsory or central to a larger system of belief.

It also commands that the subchapter be construed in favor of a **broad protection** of free exercise to the maximum extent permitted by the Arkansas and United States Constitutions.

Most important for this research project, the related nondiscrimination provisions define “religious organization” to include a:

- church;
- synagogue;
- shrine;
- **mosque**;
- temple;
- religious school or similar religious entity.

The statute therefore answers one question in black-letter law: **Arkansas’s statutory religious-liberty protections are not textually limited to Christianity.**

### Strong book line

> Arkansas did not accidentally leave Muslims inside its conscience law. The legislature wrote “mosque” into the definition.

### Research grade

**A — enacted statutory text.**

## 3. Holt v. Hobbs: the Muslim Arkansan at the Supreme Court

The most powerful Arkansas-specific Muslim religious-liberty case is *Holt v. Hobbs*, 574 U.S. 352 (2015).

Gregory Holt, also known as Abdul Maalik Muhammad, was an Arkansas prisoner and a devout Muslim. He believed his faith required him to wear a beard. Arkansas prison policy generally prohibited beards, except that inmates with certain medical conditions could wear quarter-inch beards.

Holt sought a religious accommodation. Although he believed he should not trim his beard at all, he proposed a compromise: a half-inch beard.

Arkansas denied him.

He sued under the federal Religious Land Use and Institutionalized Persons Act, or RLUIPA.

The Supreme Court unanimously ruled in his favor.

Justice Samuel Alito wrote for the Court that Arkansas had not shown that forbidding a half-inch beard was the least restrictive means of achieving its asserted security interests. The Court found the contraband argument weak, especially because Arkansas permitted longer hair on inmates’ heads and quarter-inch medical beards. The Court also identified less restrictive alternatives for identification, including photographing the inmate with and without the beard.

The Court further emphasized something critical to this project: a religious claimant’s protection does not depend upon showing that every member of his religion shares the same belief. Even an idiosyncratic but sincere religious belief can receive protection.

### Why Holt matters narratively

This is not an abstract Muslim-liberty hypothetical from 1788.

It is Arkansas.

It is a Muslim.

It is a state government burdening his religious practice.

And the United States Supreme Court unanimously telling Arkansas that religious liberty protects him too.

### Strong book lines

> Arkansas already had its Muslim test case. It reached the United States Supreme Court.

> Religious liberty became concrete in Arkansas when a Muslim prisoner asked to grow half an inch of beard.

> The Supreme Court did not ask whether most Arkansans approved of the practice. It asked whether government had justified burdening his sincere religious exercise.

### Important nuance

*Holt* was decided under RLUIPA, not directly under Arkansas Article 2, section 24. Do not blur those doctrines.

### Research grade

**A — unanimous United States Supreme Court decision.**

## 4. Arkansas is now ranked first in a private religious-liberty index

In July 2026, First Liberty Institute’s Center for Religion, Culture & Democracy announced that Arkansas ranked **#1 nationally** in its 2026 Religious Liberty in the States index, with a score of 89 percent of the legal safeguards measured by the index.

The index is produced by First Liberty, a private religious-liberty legal organization. It is not an official federal or state ranking and should never be described as a neutral governmental certification that Arkansas is objectively “the most religiously free state.”

First Liberty says Arkansas rose from sixth place in 2025 to first place in 2026, in significant part because of state legislation protecting people and institutions from being forced to participate in wedding ceremonies that conflict with religious objections.

Governor Sarah Huckabee Sanders appeared at the Arkansas State Capitol for the announcement. First Liberty quotes her as saying:

> “Religious liberty is America’s First Freedom, and Arkansas is leading the nation in protecting it.”

She also said that every American should be free to live according to faith and conscience.

### Important correction to earlier working notes

The exact statement previously remembered as Sanders calling Arkansas “the most religiously free state in America” has **not yet been verified in that exact wording** from a primary Sanders source.

What is now verified is that:

- First Liberty ranked Arkansas #1 in its 2026 index;
- the ranking was announced at the Arkansas Capitol with Sanders;
- First Liberty quotes Sanders saying Arkansas is “leading the nation in protecting” religious liberty.

Do not put the unverified “most religiously free state in America” phrase in quotation marks unless a primary recording, transcript, post, or press release containing it is found.

### Strong rhetorical application

The verified statement is already enough:

> If Arkansas is leading the nation in protecting religious liberty, then the test is not whether Baptists can worship comfortably. The test is whether the Muslim receives the same protection when his conscience becomes inconvenient.

### Research grade

**A/B — A for the existence and methodology of First Liberty’s own ranking and Sanders’s quoted statement in First Liberty’s announcement; B for broader claims about what “#1” means because the ranking depends on a private index’s selected metrics.**

## 5. The Ten Commandments school-display litigation

Arkansas enacted Act 573 in 2025. The law required public schools to display a specified “historical representation” of the Ten Commandments in classrooms and libraries. The mandated text was drawn from the Protestant King James Bible.

In *Stinson v. Fayetteville School District No. 1*, U.S. District Judge Timothy Brooks permanently enjoined the six school-district defendants before the court from complying with Act 573 on March 16, 2026.

The court held that failing to permanently enjoin the law would violate both the Establishment Clause rights of Arkansas public-school children and parents and the plaintiffs’ Free Exercise rights.

### Why this belongs in a religious-liberty book

The case illustrates the other half of religious liberty.

Free exercise protects religion **from government**.

The Establishment Clause also protects citizens **from government using its authority to privilege religion**.

Those principles reinforce rather than contradict each other.

The mandated Protestant wording also sharpens the question of equality among believers. Jews, Catholics, Orthodox Christians, Muslims, nonreligious families, and even different Protestant traditions need not understand the Decalogue or its numbering in precisely the same way.

### Strong book line

> Religious liberty cannot mean the government protects your right to follow your conscience and then selects someone else’s scripture for your child’s classroom wall.

### Research grade

**A — federal district court final order, subject to appellate review.**

### Current-status caution

This is a district-court judgment and may be appealed. In final publication, update the appellate status immediately before publication.

## 6. The Arkansas State Capitol Ten Commandments monument

Arkansas also required a Ten Commandments monument on the State Capitol grounds.

In *Cave v. Jester*, Chief U.S. District Judge Kristine Baker issued a 148-page opinion on March 31, 2026, holding that the statute requiring the monument and the monument itself violated the Establishment Clause.

The court found that the Ten Commandments monument was the only religious monument on the Capitol grounds in the relevant record and concluded that the state’s actions did not satisfy constitutional neutrality principles.

The court also found an Equal Protection violation involving the exclusion of the Satanic Temple’s proposed Baphomet monument from equal competition for placement after Arkansas altered its statutory approval process.

The judge enjoined enforcement of the Ten Commandments Monument Display Act and ordered removal of the monument, but stayed the removal portion pending appeal or expiration of the appeal period.

### Why this matters to the Muslim test

The Muslim question does not require advocating for a Qur’an monument at the Capitol.

The stronger question is simpler:

**Would Arkansas accept the same governmental religious preference if the monument contained Qur’anic commandments instead of biblical ones?**

If the answer is no, the principle is not religious liberty. It is religious preference.

### Strong book line

> The easiest way to test whether a government policy is about “religion” or about one favored religion is to change the scripture in the hypothetical.

### Research grade

**A — federal district court final opinion, subject to appellate review.**

## 7. Muslims are part of Arkansas history, not a hypothetical future population

The Encyclopedia of Arkansas and the Islamic Center of Little Rock provide a useful history of Muslim communities in the state.

The earliest known mosque described by the Encyclopedia of Arkansas was established in Little Rock in 1969 and primarily served African-American Muslims associated at the time with the Nation of Islam. The community later became known as the Islamic Center for Human Excellence.

The Islamic Center of Little Rock’s own history records that in 1979 there were already African-American Muslim families worshiping near downtown Little Rock and Muslim families from South Asia and the Middle East living in Little Rock, North Little Rock, and Jacksonville.

Families began gathering in homes around 1980 for Qur’an study and religious education. A Little Rock chapter of the Muslim Association of Arkansas began organized fundraising in 1984. Second Presbyterian Church in Little Rock allowed the growing Muslim community to use Sunday-school rooms for meetings, illustrating a local history of Christian-Muslim cooperation.

The Islamic Center of Little Rock was formally organized in 1992. Its permanent mosque was completed in the mid-1990s. The community later established Huda Academy.

Other Arkansas communities developed as well:

- the Islamic Center of Jonesboro dates to 1983;
- the Islamic Center of Northwest Arkansas began in the 1990s;
- a Bentonville Islamic center emerged as northwest Arkansas’s Muslim population grew alongside the region’s major employers.

### Important local narrative

The story of Arkansas Islam is not simply immigrant Islam.

African-American Muslim institutions existed in Little Rock before the Islamic Center of Little Rock’s later immigrant-led organization.

That distinction should be preserved. It prevents the common mistake of presenting American Islam as purely foreign or post-9/11.

### Strong book line

> Muslims did not suddenly arrive in Arkansas when politicians started warning about Sharia. Arkansas Muslims were already praying, raising families, building institutions, attending universities, practicing medicine, and participating in interfaith life decades earlier.

### Research grade

**A/B — A for institutional self-history and established dates where corroborated; B for broader population/history claims from secondary state encyclopedia materials.**

## 8. The Arkansas contradiction

Arkansas presently contains all of the elements needed for a powerful case study.

On one side:

- a constitution declaring conscience natural and indefeasible;
- a command that no religious establishment or mode of worship receive legal preference;
- a state RFRA;
- a strengthened 2023 conscience law;
- an express statutory reference to mosques;
- a private 2026 index ranking Arkansas first for the legal safeguards it measures;
- a governor publicly saying Arkansas is leading the nation in protecting religious liberty.

On the other side:

- Arkansas lost a unanimous U.S. Supreme Court religious-accommodation case brought by a Muslim prisoner;
- federal courts in 2026 held that state-backed Ten Commandments policies crossed constitutional church-state lines.

This does not prove Arkansas is uniquely hostile to Muslims or uniquely hypocritical. It proves something more useful:

**Arkansas is an unusually good place to test whether broad religious-liberty rhetoric survives contact with actual pluralism.**

## 9. The Muslim as Arkansas’s stress test

The book’s Arkansas chapter should probably ask a sequence of increasingly uncomfortable questions.

If a Baptist prisoner sincerely believed his faith required a particular practice, would Arkansas protect him?

If yes, why not a Muslim prisoner?

If a Christian school or church receives the protections of Arkansas RFRA, does a mosque?

The statute itself says yes.

If government displays Christian scripture because it teaches morality and law, could it display Qur’anic commands about justice, contracts, charity, or honoring parents for the same reason?

If not, what principle distinguishes the two?

If Arkansas says no human authority may interfere with conscience, does that protect a Muslim girl wearing hijab?

A Muslim employee requesting a religious accommodation?

A mosque seeking equal treatment in zoning?

A Muslim prisoner seeking halal meals or religious grooming?

A Muslim family voluntarily incorporating religious principles into a marriage agreement, will, or private mediation arrangement subject to ordinary civil law?

Those are not fringe hypotheticals. They are exactly the situations in which a religious-liberty system reveals whether it is neutral.

## 10. What Arkansas law does *not* mean

Broad religious freedom does not mean religious conduct automatically defeats neutral civil law.

Arkansas RFRA itself permits burdens when government proves a compelling interest and least restrictive means.

A mosque cannot commit fraud because fraud is religiously motivated.

A church cannot assault someone because doctrine supposedly requires it.

A Muslim cannot impose corporal punishment on another citizen by invoking Sharia.

A Christian cannot impose biblical criminal penalties on another citizen by invoking scripture.

The state’s job is therefore twofold:

1. protect sincere voluntary religious exercise aggressively; and
2. preserve constitutional rights and neutral civil authority when religious conduct becomes coercive or injurious.

That is precisely the framework developed throughout this research project.

## 11. Proposed Arkansas narrative spine

### Opening scene: Holt

Open with an Arkansas prison and a half-inch beard.

Do not begin with abstract theology.

Begin with a Muslim prisoner asking the State of Arkansas for half an inch of religious accommodation and losing repeatedly until the United States Supreme Court unanimously tells Arkansas it has not justified the burden.

Then reveal the state constitutional language:

> No human authority can interfere with conscience.

Then reveal the 2023 statutory language:

> mosque.

Then move to the 2026 claim that Arkansas is leading the nation in religious liberty.

Then ask:

**Does that promise belong to the Muslim?**

### Middle: government preference

Move from free exercise to establishment through the two Ten Commandments cases.

The point is not hostility toward Christianity.

The point is that Christianity is safest when government cannot decide which Christianity receives preference.

That is a Madisonian argument as much as a Muslim-liberty argument.

### Closing: the test

End at an Arkansas mosque, ideally through original interviews in a later fieldwork phase.

A Muslim Arkansan should get the final word on what religious liberty means in his or her own life.

Potential closing formulation:

> Arkansas says no human authority may control conscience. It says every religious denomination should enjoy its worship in peace. Its statute names the church, the synagogue, the mosque, and the temple. If those words mean what they say, the Muslim is not an exception to Arkansas religious liberty. The Muslim is one of the clearest ways to prove it exists.

## Final Pass 13 finding

The Arkansas research gives the project a remarkably clean local test.

Arkansas’s law does not say religious liberty belongs to Christians.

It does not say no human authority may interfere with Baptist conscience.

It does not define a protected religious organization as merely a church.

The state constitution speaks in universal terms, and the modern statute actually names the **mosque**.

So the question for Arkansas is no longer theoretical:

> If Arkansas intends to lead America in religious liberty, will it defend the Muslim’s conscience with the same energy it defends the Christian’s?

That is not an attack on Christianity.

It is the constitutional test of whether religious liberty is real.

And Arkansas has already supplied the answer once: in *Holt v. Hobbs*, the United States Supreme Court unanimously said the Muslim’s religious exercise counted too.
