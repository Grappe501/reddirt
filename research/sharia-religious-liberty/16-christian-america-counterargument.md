# Research Pass 16 — The Christian America Counterargument

## Purpose

Build the strongest serious historical case that the United States was founded as a Christian nation, then test that claim against the constitutional record. The goal is not to ridicule the argument but to distinguish several propositions that are often collapsed into one:

1. Colonial America was culturally and institutionally Christian.
2. Many founders were personally Christian or deeply influenced by Christian moral ideas.
3. Some states maintained explicitly Christian or Protestant establishments, tests, or public-support systems.
4. Founding-era political culture treated religion as important to republican virtue.
5. The federal Constitution created a legally Christian national government.

The evidence strongly supports 1–4 in important ways. It does not support 5.

## Core conclusion

The best evidence supports describing founding-era America as a predominantly Christian society whose political culture was deeply influenced by Christianity, while the federal constitutional order deliberately stopped short of establishing Christianity as the legal identity of the United States.

The most important distinction is:

> Christian society is not the same thing as constitutionally Christian state.

The federal Constitution did not erase Christianity from public life. It also did not make Christianity a legal qualification for federal citizenship or office.

## Steelman: the strongest case for “Christian America”

### 1. Colonial roots were overwhelmingly Christian

The European settler societies that became the United States were overwhelmingly Christian. Several colonies had established churches or public systems for supporting Protestant worship. Mark David Hall notes that at least nine of the thirteen colonies had established churches and that officeholding tests commonly required Christianity or Protestantism.

This is a strong historical fact. Any account portraying colonial America as culturally secular is untenable.

Evidence grade: A.

### 2. State constitutions could be explicitly Christian or Protestant

The Massachusetts Constitution of 1780 is one of the strongest examples. It described public worship, piety, religion, and morality as important to good government and authorized public support for Protestant teachers. John Adams’s draft went even further, requiring the governor and other officeholders to profess the Christian religion. The final Massachusetts Constitution retained significant Christian establishment features.

North Carolina’s 1776 Constitution disqualified from civil office anyone who denied God, Protestantism, or the divine authority of the Old or New Testaments, even while separately prohibiting establishment of one denomination over another.

These documents are devastating to any simplistic claim that the founding generation uniformly believed government must be religiously neutral in the modern sense.

Evidence grade: A.

### 3. Founders frequently connected religion and republican morality

George Washington’s Farewell Address said religion and morality were indispensable supports of political prosperity and warned against expecting national morality to prevail without religious principle.

John Adams likewise argued that religion and morality helped sustain republican liberty. His 1796 diary praised Christianity for diffusing moral duties across all classes of society. His 1797 inaugural address spoke favorably of a people who professed Christianity and described decent respect for Christianity as an important public recommendation.

These statements are important. They show that prominent founders did not imagine republican government thriving in a vacuum of religion.

Evidence grade: A.

### 4. Public religion was normal at the national level

The Continental Congress called days of fasting, humiliation, prayer, and thanksgiving. Washington’s wartime general orders directed soldiers to observe Congress’s days of prayer and described God as the giver of victory.

The Northwest Ordinance of 1787 declared that religion, morality, and knowledge were necessary to good government and the happiness of mankind, while also protecting peaceful religious worship.

Founding-era public life therefore cannot fairly be described as aggressively secular or hostile to religious expression.

Evidence grade: A.

### 5. The First Amendment did not create a religion-free public square

The First Amendment prohibited Congress from establishing religion and protected free exercise. It did not prohibit presidents from invoking God, legislatures from opening with prayer, or citizens from making religious arguments in political life.

The Library of Congress notes that Washington and Adams were public patrons of religion and that there was little founding-era support for a modern concept requiring public officials to avoid religious language entirely.

That point is valid and should be conceded.

Evidence grade: A/B depending on formulation.

### 6. Christianity was one important intellectual source among several

Mark David Hall makes the serious scholarly argument that Christian political ideas—especially covenantal thinking, natural law as understood through Protestant traditions, human sinfulness, moral accountability, and liberty of conscience—substantially influenced founding-era political thought.

This argument cannot responsibly be dismissed simply because Locke, classical republicanism, common law, and the Enlightenment also mattered.

The better conclusion is plural causation: Christianity was one important intellectual stream among several.

Evidence grade: B for broad influence; C where specific constitutional clauses are claimed to derive from specific theological doctrines without direct documentary linkage.

## Where the Christian-nation claim weakens

### 1. The federal Constitution did not impose a Christian test

Article VI states that no religious test shall ever be required for federal office or public trust.

This is especially significant because the founders knew what religious tests looked like. Several states already had them. They could have copied them into the federal Constitution. They did not.

The North Carolina ratification debates show that opponents understood this consequence clearly. They warned that pagans, deists, and Muslims could hold office. Defenders did not respond by saying Christians were secretly required. They defended religious liberty.

Evidence grade: A.

### 2. The Constitution did not declare Christianity the national religion

The federal Constitution contains no clause declaring the United States a Christian nation, recognizing Christianity as the official faith, or making biblical law the legal source of federal authority.

The Constitution’s religion language is chiefly restrictive: no religious test, then later no establishment and free exercise.

The phrase “Year of our Lord” is calendar dating convention, not an establishment clause.

Evidence grade: A.

### 3. The First Amendment barred a federal establishment

Whatever state establishments survived at the founding, the First Amendment prohibited Congress from making a law respecting an establishment of religion.

At minimum, the national government was not authorized to create a national Christian church or federal religious establishment.

The fact that some states maintained establishments for years afterward does not convert the federal government into a Christian establishment; it shows that early federalism allowed state-level religious arrangements that the national government did not itself adopt.

Evidence grade: A.

### 4. Founding-era Christianity was not monolithic

“Christian” encompassed Congregationalists, Anglicans/Episcopalians, Presbyterians, Baptists, Quakers, Methodists, Catholics, Unitarians, and other groups whose theological and political disagreements could be profound.

Some major founders were orthodox Christians; others were heterodox, Unitarian, or skeptical of central Christian doctrines. It is historically hazardous to speak of a single founder theology.

Evidence grade: A/B.

### 5. State establishments actually strengthen the federal distinction

Massachusetts and North Carolina are not evidence that Article VI secretly established Christianity. They demonstrate the opposite analytical point: founding Americans knew how to write explicit Christian qualifications when they wanted them.

Massachusetts could require Christian profession.
North Carolina could privilege Protestant belief.
The federal Constitution did neither.

This is one of the most important arguments of the entire pass.

> The absence of a federal Christian test is meaningful precisely because explicit Christian tests were familiar drafting tools.

Evidence grade: A.

### 6. Religious public culture is not the same thing as legal religious supremacy

Presidential prayer, thanksgiving proclamations, chaplains, biblical rhetoric, and public invocations of Providence are evidence of a religious political culture.

They do not by themselves prove that Christianity was the legally supreme source of federal law.

A society can be culturally religious without making religious identity a condition of citizenship.

Evidence grade: A as conceptual distinction.

## The Northwest Ordinance problem — and opportunity

The Northwest Ordinance is often cited by Christian-nation advocates because it says religion and morality are necessary to good government.

That is real evidence.

But Article 1 of the same ordinance protects any peaceful person from being molested on account of his mode of worship or religious sentiments.

So the document combines strong public affirmation of religion with broad liberty of conscience.

This is not modern secularism.
It is also not simple Christian establishment.

It illustrates the mixed constitutional culture we keep finding.

## Washington is not a simple Christian-nationalist proof text

Washington’s Farewell Address strongly linked religion to morality.

But Library of Congress materials show that Alexander Hamilton drafted an even stronger sentence asking whether morality required a generally received and divinely authoritative religion. Washington did not include that sentence.

That does not make Washington secular. It does caution against converting his support for religion into a claim that he endorsed one nationally authoritative creed.

Washington’s separate letters to Jews, Baptists, Quakers, and others emphasized liberty of conscience and good citizenship rather than theological conformity.

Evidence grade: A.

## John Adams is genuinely complicated

Adams provides some of the strongest evidence for the Christian-America side.

He drafted explicitly Christian officeholding requirements for Massachusetts. He repeatedly praised Christianity’s moral role. He described himself as a regular churchgoer and publicly spoke favorably of Christianity.

But Adams’s theology evolved toward Unitarianism, and his federal presidential oath and constitutional office did not depend on Christian profession.

He therefore demonstrates an important distinction:

> A founder could personally believe Christianity was crucial to republican morality while still supporting a federal constitutional order that did not require Christianity for national citizenship or office.

## The most defensible formulations

### Strong and supportable

- “America’s founding culture was overwhelmingly Christian.”
- “Christian ideas significantly influenced many founders.”
- “Several founding-era states maintained explicitly Christian or Protestant legal preferences.”
- “Many founders believed religion and morality were essential to republican government.”
- “Public religious language and practice were normal at the founding.”
- “The founders did not envision a public square scrubbed clean of religious argument.”

### Too broad or false without qualification

- “The Constitution established America as a Christian nation.”
- “Federal office was reserved for Christians.”
- “The First Amendment was intended only to protect Christian denominations.”
- “Biblical law was made the supreme law of the United States.”
- “All founders were orthodox Christians.”
- “Religious liberty at the founding meant liberty only for Christians.”

## Implications for the Sharia project

This pass does not weaken the Muslim-liberty thesis. It sharpens it.

The argument should not be:

“America was really secular, therefore Muslims belong.”

That is historically too simple.

The stronger argument is:

**America was born from a society saturated in Christianity. Many founders believed Christianity or religion generally was indispensable to republican virtue. Several states explicitly privileged Christianity. Yet when the federal constitutional order was written, it did not require Christianity for federal office and later prohibited a federal establishment. That is precisely why Muslim citizenship became constitutionally imaginable.**

This lets us concede almost everything legitimate in the Christian-America case without conceding federal Christian supremacy.

## The Muslim as constitutional stress test after Pass 16

The strongest version of the project now becomes:

> The remarkable thing is not that early America was non-Christian. It plainly was deeply Christian. The remarkable thing is that a deeply Christian society nevertheless created a federal constitutional structure broad enough to permit a Muslim, Jew, dissenter, or unbeliever to possess civil rights without first becoming Christian.

That is historically stronger than arguing that religion did not matter.

## Key rhetorical lines

**“Christian culture and Christian constitutional supremacy are not the same claim.”**

**“The founders knew how to write a Christian religious test. Several states did. The federal Constitution did not.”**

**“America did not become religiously free because Christianity was absent from its founding. Religious liberty emerged inside a society where Christianity was overwhelmingly present.”**

**“The constitutional achievement was not the disappearance of Christianity. It was the refusal to make Christian identity the price of federal citizenship.”**

**“A Christian majority built a government that, at its federal level, did not require you to be Christian.”**

## Evidence judgment

### A — Direct primary-source fact
- Explicit Protestant/Christian provisions in Massachusetts and North Carolina constitutions.
- Article VI no religious test.
- First Amendment establishment/free-exercise text.
- Washington Farewell Address religion/morality passage.
- Congressional prayer/fasting proclamations.
- Northwest Ordinance religion/morality language and worship protection.

### B — Strong historical interpretation
- Christianity was a major influence on founding political culture.
- Most European-descended colonists lived within Christian cultural frameworks.
- Founders expected religion to remain visible in public life.

### C — Requires careful proof
- Specific constitutional provisions derived directly from specific biblical or theological doctrines.
- Particular natural-rights formulations were uniquely Christian rather than jointly Christian, classical, common-law, and Enlightenment.

### E — Do not publish as fact
- The Constitution made Christianity the official religion.
- Federal officeholders had to be Christians.
- Article VI secretly preserved a Christian qualification.
- The First Amendment protected only Christian sects.

## Final Pass 16 thesis

**The strongest historical evidence supports calling founding-era America deeply Christian in culture, institutions, public rhetoric, and intellectual influence. It does not support calling the federal Constitution a Christian establishment. The constitutional order that emerged from this Christian-majority society deliberately refused to make Christian profession a qualification for federal office and prohibited a federal establishment. That distinction is exactly what makes Muslim religious liberty part of the American constitutional story rather than an exception to it.**
