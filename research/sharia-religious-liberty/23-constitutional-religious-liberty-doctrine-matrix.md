# Pass 23 — Constitutional Religious-Liberty Doctrine Matrix

## Mission

This pass builds the legal backbone for Chapters 17–20 and 24–26.

The book's constitutional argument must be more precise than slogans such as “the Constitution protects religion until it violates the law” or “church and state are separate.” Neither formulation accurately describes modern American doctrine.

The central legal problem is jurisdiction:

- What may a person believe?
- What religious conduct may government burden?
- When must government grant an accommodation?
- When may government regulate conduct despite religious motivation?
- When does government itself cross the line into religious discrimination, religious coercion, or establishment?
- May religious citizens participate politically from religious conviction?
- May private parties voluntarily use religious norms in contracts or dispute resolution?
- What happens when religious rules conflict with otherwise governing civil law?

This matrix is an internal research framework, not legal advice. Every time-sensitive doctrine and Arkansas case should be rechecked immediately before publication.

---

# I. THE CONSTITUTIONAL ARCHITECTURE

## 1. Article VI — Federal Religious Tests

### Textual rule

Article VI requires federal and state officers to support the Constitution by oath or affirmation, while providing that no religious test may be required as a qualification to federal office or public trust.

### What it establishes

The federal government cannot condition federal office/public trust on profession of a religious creed or performance of a religious test.

### Historical significance for this project

Pass 22 demonstrates that founding-era Americans knew how to write explicit Christian and Protestant qualifications when they wanted them. Article VI chose a different federal rule.

The North Carolina ratification debate is especially probative because participants explicitly recognized that the absence of a religious test could permit Muslims to hold federal office.

### Important limitation

Article VI's text says “under the United States.” It did not itself erase founding-era state religious tests.

Modern state religious tests are independently constrained by the First and Fourteenth Amendments. *Torcaso v. Watkins* (1961) invalidated Maryland's requirement that a notary declare belief in God.

### Safe book formulation

> The Constitution did not merely forget to specify a federal Christian creed. Article VI affirmatively prohibited a religious test for federal office and public trust, and ratification-era Americans argued about the religious breadth that rule could permit.

### Do not write

“Article VI immediately abolished every state religious test.”

# II. INCORPORATION — WHY THE FIRST AMENDMENT NOW MATTERS TO STATES

## 2. First Amendment originally addressed Congress

The First Amendment begins “Congress shall make no law...”

At the founding, the Religion Clauses did not operate against state governments in the same modern manner.

## 3. Cantwell v. Connecticut (1940)

The Supreme Court held the Free Exercise Clause applicable to state government through the Fourteenth Amendment.

This is essential to the book because modern Arkansas religious-liberty disputes cannot be described simply by quoting the original federal arrangement of 1791.

### Safe formulation

> The federal religious-liberty system changed after the Fourteenth Amendment. By the twentieth century, the Supreme Court applied First Amendment religious-liberty protections against state governments as well as the federal government.

---

# III. FREE EXERCISE — BELIEF AND CONDUCT

## 4. Belief receives extraordinarily strong protection

Government cannot constitutionally prescribe religious orthodoxy or require a person to profess religious belief merely to obtain civil status or public office.

*Torcaso* is especially useful for this project because Maryland argued that nobody was forced to become a notary. The Supreme Court rejected the idea that government may attach a forbidden religious qualification simply because public office is voluntary.

### Book consequence

A Muslim does not have to prove Islam true, moderate, compatible with Christianity, or approved by the majority before possessing freedom of belief.

The same is true for Christians, Jews, Hindus, atheists, former Muslims, and other conscience positions.

---

## 5. Conduct is protected, but not absolutely

Free Exercise protects more than private thought. It protects religious action as well.

But religious motivation does not create a universal exemption from civil law.

The modern doctrinal question depends heavily on what kind of law burdens the religious practice.

---

# IV. EMPLOYMENT DIVISION v. SMITH — THE BASELINE RULE

## 6. Smith (1990)

*Employment Division v. Smith* established the important baseline that neutral and generally applicable laws ordinarily do not violate the Free Exercise Clause merely because they incidentally burden religious practice.

### Why this matters

The Constitution does **not** simply say:

> “My religion requires it, therefore civil law cannot regulate it.”

That proposition is false.

### But Smith is not the whole doctrine

The neutrality/general-applicability inquiry has become extremely important and can trigger strict scrutiny where government treats religious conduct differently from comparable secular conduct or creates discretionary exceptions.

Federal and state statutes may also provide stronger accommodation rights than the constitutional baseline.

### Safe formulation

> Religious motivation does not automatically place conduct beyond civil regulation. But government also cannot disguise religious discrimination as neutral regulation, and statutes such as RFRA and RLUIPA can require accommodations beyond the constitutional floor.

---

# V. LUKUMI — GOVERNMENT CANNOT TARGET A RELIGION

## 7. Church of Lukumi Babalu Aye v. Hialeah (1993)

Hialeah enacted ordinances directed at Santeria animal sacrifice.

The Supreme Court held that laws burdening religion that are not neutral or generally applicable are subject to rigorous scrutiny.

The case is extremely useful to the Sharia project because it concerns a minority religious practice many Americans found unfamiliar or disturbing.

### Core principle

Government cannot selectively pursue a secular objective only against conduct because that conduct is religious.

### Application to Islam

A legislature could regulate genuinely harmful conduct through neutral law.

It could not constitutionally design a prohibition whose real object was suppressing Islam while allowing materially comparable secular conduct.

### Important book line

> The Constitution does not ask whether the majority finds a religious practice strange before deciding whether government must treat the religion neutrally.

---

# VI. FULTON / KENNEDY — GENERAL APPLICABILITY HAS TEETH

## 8. Fulton v. Philadelphia (2021)

Where government retains a system of individualized exceptions, refusal to extend an exception to religious exercise can trigger strict scrutiny.

Do not reduce *Fulton* to a universal rule that religious claimants always win. Its contractual and exception structure mattered.

## 9. Kennedy v. Bremerton School District (2022)

*Kennedy* reiterated that a government policy can fail neutrality/general applicability where religious conduct is specially burdened while comparable secular conduct is permitted.

It also rejected the idea that the Establishment Clause requires government to purge all visible private religious expression by public employees.

### Critical distinction

Protected private religious expression is not automatically government-sponsored religion.

### Book consequence

The constitutional solution is not a religion-free public square.

A Muslim public employee does not cease being Muslim at the workplace door any more than a Christian employee ceases being Christian.

The constitutional question is contextual: private expression, governmental speech, coercion, job duties, and treatment of comparable secular activity matter.

---

# VII. RFRA — STATUTORY PROTECTION ABOVE THE CONSTITUTIONAL FLOOR

## 10. Religious Freedom Restoration Act of 1993

Congress enacted RFRA after *Smith*.

RFRA provides that government may not substantially burden a person's exercise of religion, even through a generally applicable rule, unless application of that burden to the person furthers a compelling governmental interest through the least restrictive means.

### City of Boerne v. Flores (1997)

The Supreme Court held that Congress could not use its Fourteenth Amendment enforcement power to impose RFRA's standard generally upon the states in the manner Congress attempted.

### Modern practical rule

Federal RFRA remains critically important against the federal government.

It should not be described as a universal federal statute governing every state and local action.

States may enact their own RFRA-like protections.

Arkansas has done so.

### Safe formulation

> RFRA reflects a legislative decision to protect religious exercise more strongly than *Smith*'s constitutional baseline in areas where the statute validly applies.

---

# VIII. RLUIPA — LAND USE AND INSTITUTIONALIZED PERSONS

## 11. Religious Land Use and Institutionalized Persons Act of 2000

RLUIPA protects two areas especially important to this book:

1. religious land use;
2. religious exercise by institutionalized persons.

### Land use

Government may not impose certain substantial land-use burdens on religious exercise unless the statutory strict-scrutiny standard is met.

RLUIPA also contains equal-terms, nondiscrimination, and exclusion protections for religious assemblies and institutions.

### Why this matters to mosques

A mosque is not constitutionally or statutorily outside ordinary American religious-liberty architecture merely because neighbors associate Islam with Sharia.

A zoning authority cannot simply create a special anti-mosque rule while treating comparable religious or nonreligious assemblies differently.

The exact RLUIPA provision applicable depends on the facts and jurisdictional predicates.

### Institutionalized persons

Government may not substantially burden an institutionalized person's religious exercise unless the burden is the least restrictive means of furthering a compelling governmental interest.

That rule produced one of the project's central Arkansas cases.

# IX. HOLT v. HOBBS — THE ARKANSAS MUSLIM TEST CASE

## 12. Holt (2015)

Gregory Holt, also known as Abdul Maalik Muhammad, was an Arkansas inmate and devout Muslim who sought permission to maintain a one-half-inch beard for religious reasons.

Arkansas's grooming policy generally prohibited beards, with a medical exception for shorter beards.

Holt proposed the half-inch beard as a compromise even though he believed his religion called for a fuller beard.

The Supreme Court unanimously held that Arkansas's policy, as applied to Holt, violated RLUIPA.

### Why Arkansas lost

Arkansas identified legitimate prison-security interests, including contraband and identification.

But RLUIPA required more than asserting those interests in general terms.

The state had to justify burdening this particular claimant and show that less restrictive alternatives would not adequately achieve its objectives.

Searching the short beard and using photographs were among obvious alternatives Arkansas failed adequately to defeat.

### Crucial religious principle

RLUIPA's protection does not depend on the practice being compulsory or central to a religion, and Holt's belief did not have to be shared by every Muslim.

### Narrative significance

> Arkansas already had a Muslim religious-liberty test case. It reached the United States Supreme Court, and Arkansas lost unanimously.

### Important restraint

Do not say *Holt* held that Arkansas acted from anti-Muslim animus. The case was resolved under RLUIPA's substantial-burden/strict-scrutiny framework.

# X. ESTABLISHMENT — GOVERNMENT RELIGION IS A DIFFERENT QUESTION

## 13. The Establishment Clause

The First Amendment also prohibits laws respecting an establishment of religion.

Free Exercise and Establishment are related but distinct.

A policy may implicate one, the other, or both.

### Old shorthand to avoid

Do not make *Lemon v. Kurtzman* the current governing test.

The Supreme Court stated in *Kennedy* that it had abandoned the Lemon test and its endorsement-test offshoot.

### Current orientation

Modern Establishment Clause analysis emphasizes historical practices and understandings, with coercion remaining important in relevant contexts.

The exact boundary remains contested and fact-dependent.

### Safe formulation

> Modern Establishment Clause doctrine does not require government to erase religion from public life. It does continue to constrain governmental religious coercion and practices inconsistent with the historical meaning and traditions of the Establishment Clause.

---

# XI. COERCION

## 14. Government may not compel religious observance

The Supreme Court has repeatedly recognized that government cannot make religious observance compulsory or force citizens into formal religious exercise.

*Kennedy* distinguished the coach's private prayer from government-directed prayer or prayer imposed on a captive audience.

### Book's symmetry test

This principle becomes powerful when scripture is changed in the hypothetical.

If government cannot compel a Christian child to participate in Islamic prayer, the same constitutional problem arises when government compels a Muslim child to participate in Christian prayer.

### Important nuance

Not every exposure to religion, religious symbol, historical religious reference, legislative prayer, or private religious expression constitutes unconstitutional coercion or establishment under current doctrine.

---

# XII. RELIGIOUS STATUS AND PUBLIC BENEFITS

## 15. Trinity Lutheran / Espinoza / Carson line

Modern Free Exercise doctrine strongly restricts government from excluding otherwise eligible participants from generally available public benefits merely because of their religious status or, in the school-funding cases, religious use.

### Why this matters

“Separation of church and state” cannot safely be used in the book as though it means government must always exclude religious organizations from neutral public programs.

### Symmetry

A public benefit open to qualifying private organizations cannot simply be denied to a mosque because it is Muslim while being made available to comparable churches or secular institutions, subject to the governing program and constitutional context.

---

# XIII. RELIGIOUS TESTS AFTER INCORPORATION

## 16. Torcaso v. Watkins (1961)

Maryland required a declaration of belief in God for public office.

The Supreme Court invalidated the requirement under the First and Fourteenth Amendments.

### Significance

The modern constitutional rule is broader than Article VI's original federal-office text.

A state cannot require an officeholder to profess belief in God as a condition of office.

### Muslim-president connection

Article VI supplies the founding-era federal architecture.

*Torcaso* helps demonstrate the modern constitutional rejection of state religious qualification.

---

# XIV. RELIGIOUS POLITICAL PARTICIPATION

## 17. Religious motivation does not disqualify political speech

American constitutional law does not require citizens to strip religious reasoning from their political participation.

A Christian may vote from Christian conviction.

A Muslim may vote from Islamic conviction.

A Jew may vote from Jewish conviction.

An atheist may vote from secular moral conviction.

### Distinction the book must maintain

**Religiously motivated democratic advocacy** is not the same thing as **governmental establishment of religion**.

A Muslim advocating a policy because of Islamic belief is not, without more, imposing Sharia as sovereign law.

A Christian advocating a policy because of biblical belief is not, without more, establishing Christianity.

### The constitutional issue

Governmental action remains subject to constitutional limitations regardless of the religious or secular motivations of voters who supported it.

### Safe formulation

> The Constitution does not require citizens to become secular before entering politics. It constrains what government may constitutionally do once political power is exercised.

---

# XV. RELIGIOUS ARBITRATION, CONTRACTS, AND PRIVATE ORDERING

## 18. Religious norms can enter private legal arrangements without becoming sovereign law

Americans may voluntarily organize private conduct around religious norms in many contexts.

Examples can include contracts, religious marriage commitments, internal church governance, mediation, and arbitration.

But this does not create a parallel sovereign government.

### Important distinctions

A private religious tribunal does not acquire criminal jurisdiction merely because participants believe its rules divine.

A religious body cannot imprison, physically punish, or exercise governmental police power simply through private religious authority.

Civil enforcement of agreements remains subject to ordinary jurisdictional, contractual, procedural, statutory, and public-policy limits.

Courts also face First Amendment restrictions on resolving genuinely ecclesiastical questions.

### Book consequence

“Muslim arbitration exists” is not evidence that American courts have surrendered sovereignty to Sharia.

Likewise, Jewish beth din proceedings or Christian ecclesiastical tribunals do not automatically create rival governments.

### Research gate

Before publication, build a separate arbitration mini-matrix using current federal and Arkansas law if the manuscript treats religious arbitration in detail.

# XVI. WHAT HAPPENS WHEN RELIGIOUS BELIEF CONFLICTS WITH CIVIL LAW?

There is no single answer.

The following decision tree is more accurate.

## Question 1 — Is government regulating belief itself?

If yes, constitutional protection is at its strongest.

## Question 2 — Is the conduct being targeted because it is religious?

If yes, *Lukumi* and related doctrine may require strict scrutiny.

## Question 3 — Is the rule genuinely neutral and generally applicable?

If yes, *Smith* may permit the burden under the Free Exercise Clause, subject to exceptions and other constitutional doctrines.

## Question 4 — Does the government maintain comparable secular exceptions or individualized exemptions?

If yes, *Fulton* and later general-applicability doctrine may require more demanding scrutiny.

## Question 5 — Does RFRA apply?

If federal government action, examine RFRA.

## Question 6 — Does RLUIPA apply?

If land use or institutionalization, examine the statutory predicates and protections.

## Question 7 — Does a state RFRA or state constitution provide stronger protection?

In Arkansas, this is critical.

## Question 8 — Is another constitutional right implicated?

Speech, association, due process, equal protection, parental rights, or other protections may alter the analysis.

## Question 9 — Would accommodation itself violate another constitutional command?

Accommodation has limits as well.

# XVII. ARKANSAS CONSTITUTIONAL LAYER

## 19. Arkansas Constitution Article 2, Section 24

Arkansas protects a natural and indefeasible right to worship according to conscience and declares that human authority may not control or interfere with the right of conscience, while prohibiting legal preference among religious establishments, denominations, or modes of worship.

### Significance

Arkansas's text is rhetorically expansive.

The Arkansas Court of Appeals in *Thorne v. Arkansas Department of Human Services* described Section 24 as even more sweeping than the federal First Amendment in the context before it.

### Book consequence

Arkansas's own constitutional vocabulary makes the Muslim-neighbor test especially powerful.

---

# XVIII. ARKANSAS CONSCIENCE PROTECTION ACT / RFRA

## 20. Act 733 of 2023

Arkansas enacted the Conscience Protection Act and amended its Religious Freedom Restoration Act.

The statute employs a substantial-burden / compelling-interest / least-restrictive-means structure for covered governmental burdens on religious exercise.

It defines religious exercise broadly and contains remedies.

### Crucial textual fact for this project

The statute's definition of “religious organization” expressly includes a house of worship such as a:

- church;
- synagogue;
- shrine;
- **mosque**;
- temple.

### Why this matters

The inclusion is explicit, not inferred.

### Safe narrative line

> Arkansas did not accidentally leave Muslims inside its modern conscience law. The legislature wrote “mosque” into the definition.

### Important limitation

The presence of the word “mosque” does not resolve every possible dispute in favor of a Muslim claimant. It establishes inclusion within the statutory category; application still depends on the provision, facts, defenses, and constitutional limits.

# XIX. THE “CHANGE THE SCRIPTURE” TEST

This is a narrative heuristic, not a judicial test.

It should never be presented as controlling doctrine.

But it is extremely useful for exposing inconsistent political intuitions.

Ask:

- Would we accept the same rule if the Bible were replaced by the Qur'an?
- Would we accept the same rule if a church were replaced by a mosque?
- Would we accept the same compelled prayer if Christianity became Islam?
- Would we defend the same religious accommodation if the claimant belonged to a religion we disliked?

### What this test can reveal

Possible denominational preference.

Possible religious discrimination.

Possible confusion between private religious exercise and governmental religious authority.

### What it cannot prove

A constitutional violation automatically.

Actual doctrine still controls.

# XX. THE CONSTITUTIONAL SYMMETRY MODEL

The book's strongest constitutional synthesis can now be stated more precisely.

## The Muslim

May believe Islam is true.

May practice Islam subject to constitutional/statutory boundaries.

May seek accommodations where law provides them.

May organize a mosque.

May advocate policies from Islamic conviction.

May run for federal office without a religious test.

May voluntarily structure private conduct around Islamic norms within ordinary civil-law boundaries.

May not obtain governmental power to violate other people's constitutional rights merely by calling the rule Sharia.

## The Christian

The same architecture applies.

May believe Christianity is true.

May practice Christianity.

May seek accommodations.

May organize a church.

May advocate policies from Christian conviction.

May hold office.

May structure private conduct around Christian norms.

May not obtain governmental power to violate other people's constitutional rights merely by calling the rule biblical law.

## The former Muslim / atheist

May reject Islam.

May criticize Islam.

May reject Christianity.

May refuse worship.

May advocate secular policy.

May hold office without professing belief in God.

Government may not impose a religious profession as the price of civil status.

# XXI. WHERE THE BOOK'S SLOGANS NEED CORRECTION

## Unsafe slogan

“Religion is protected until it violates the law.”

### Why unsafe

Religious-liberty law can require exemptions from otherwise applicable laws.

### Better

> Religious conviction does not automatically override civil law, but neither does an ordinary law automatically end the religious-liberty inquiry.

---

## Unsafe slogan

“The Constitution completely separates church and state.”

### Why unsafe

Modern doctrine permits substantial religious expression and various accommodations in public life.

### Better

> The Constitution limits governmental establishment and protects religious exercise; it does not require religion to disappear from public life.

---

## Unsafe slogan

“Sharia is protected by the First Amendment.”

### Why unsafe

Too categorical. “Sharia” can refer to belief, worship, private ethics, contractual norms, jurisprudence, or governmental coercion.

### Better

> Muslim belief and religious exercise receive the same constitutional and statutory protections applicable to other religions, while conduct and governmental power remain subject to the same civil constitutional order.

---

## Unsafe slogan

“The Constitution forbids Sharia law.”

### Why unsafe

It depends what “Sharia law” means.

Prayer, fasting, halal observance, charitable obligations, religious marriage, voluntary religious advice, and private ethical commitments are not equivalent to sovereign criminal law.

### Better

> The Constitution does not prohibit Muslims from living by religious norms. It prevents any religious authority from becoming a source of governmental power unconstrained by constitutional rights and valid civil law.

---

## Unsafe slogan

“A Muslim can never put Sharia above the Constitution.”

### Why unsafe

As theology, a believer may regard God's law as morally supreme. The Constitution regulates governmental/legal authority, not the hierarchy inside someone's conscience.

### Better

> A Muslim may believe God's law morally supreme, just as a Christian may. But in American civil government, religious conviction does not by itself supply sovereign legal authority to override constitutional limits.

# XXIII. WHAT THIS MEANS FOR THE BOOK'S CENTRAL THESIS

The constitutional answer is neither:

> Sharia is harmless, therefore protect it.

nor:

> Sharia contains dangerous doctrines, therefore suppress Islam.

The American legal architecture asks different questions.

Who is acting?

Government or private citizen?

Belief or conduct?

Voluntary or coercive?

Neutral law or religious targeting?

Private agreement or sovereign punishment?

Federal or state government?

Constitutional floor or statutory accommodation?

Government speech or private religious expression?

Those distinctions are the machinery of religious liberty.

They allow the constitutional system to say two things simultaneously:

> A Muslim does not surrender religious conscience by becoming an American.

and

> Religious conscience does not grant any believer sovereign governmental authority over everyone else.

The same propositions apply to Christianity.

# XXVIII. PASS 23 VERDICT

**LEGAL ARCHITECTURE: PASS — READY FOR CHAPTER PACKET DEVELOPMENT, SUBJECT TO PRE-PUBLICATION LEGAL UPDATE AND SPECIALIST REVIEW.**

The book can now make a considerably more precise argument than “America protects Sharia.”

The stronger formulation is:

> American law protects Muslim belief and religious exercise through the same constitutional and statutory architecture that protects other faiths. That protection can include accommodations from civil rules. It does not transform religious authority into sovereign civil power, immunize harmful conduct merely because it is religiously motivated, or permit government to discriminate against a disfavored faith. The constitutional question is therefore not whether America accepts or rejects “Sharia” as one undifferentiated thing. The question is what kind of belief, conduct, private ordering, or governmental power is actually at issue.

That distinction is the legal wall the book has been looking for.

And it produces the reciprocal conclusion:

> The constitutional rule strong enough to protect a Christian from an Islamic government must also be strong enough to protect a Muslim from a Christian government.

The point is not that Christianity and Islam are the same.

The point is that civil liberty cannot depend on which one has the votes.
