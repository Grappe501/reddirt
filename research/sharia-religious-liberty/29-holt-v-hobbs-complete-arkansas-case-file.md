# Pass 29 — Holt v. Hobbs: Complete Arkansas Case File

## Executive finding

*Holt v. Hobbs*, 574 U.S. 352 (2015), is a central Arkansas religious-liberty case. Gregory Houston Holt, also known as Abdul Maalik Muhammad, was an Arkansas prisoner and a Muslim who believed his religion required him to grow a beard. Arkansas prison policy generally prohibited beards except for a quarter-inch medical exception. Although Holt believed he should not trim his beard at all, he proposed a compromise: a half-inch beard.

Arkansas refused. The Supreme Court unanimously held that applying the policy to prohibit Holt's half-inch religious beard violated the Religious Land Use and Institutionalized Persons Act of 2000 (RLUIPA).

The case did not hold that Islamic law superseded Arkansas law. It held that Arkansas was governed by an American federal statute requiring government to justify substantial burdens on institutionalized persons' religious exercise through a compelling-interest and least-restrictive-means test.

> Holt did not win because Sharia became the law of Arkansas. He won because American law protected his right to practice Islam.

## 1. Case identity

Supreme Court: *Holt, aka Muhammad v. Hobbs, Director, Arkansas Department of Correction, et al.*

Citation: 574 U.S. 352 (2015).

Docket: No. 13-6827.

Argued: October 7, 2014.

Decided: January 20, 2015.

Disposition: reversed and remanded.

Justice Samuel Alito wrote the opinion for a unanimous Court. Justice Ginsburg filed a concurrence joined by Justice Sotomayor; Justice Sotomayor also wrote separately.

## 2. Holt's claim

The Supreme Court described Holt as an Arkansas inmate and devout Muslim. He believed Islam required him to maintain a beard and that he should not trim it at all. Nevertheless, he asked for only a half-inch beard as a compromise.

That detail matters. The dispute reaching the Supreme Court was not about an unlimited beard. The requested accommodation was physically small and specifically defined.

Potential chapter opening:

> Half an inch. That was the distance between an Arkansas prison rule and a unanimous decision of the Supreme Court of the United States.

## 3. Arkansas's grooming rule

Arkansas generally prohibited prisoners from growing beards. It permitted prisoners with diagnosed skin conditions to maintain quarter-inch beards but provided no comparable religious exception for Holt's proposed half-inch beard.

The medical exception mattered as evidence when the Court evaluated whether Arkansas's religious restriction was truly the least restrictive means available.

## 4. Arkansas's security arguments

Arkansas defended the restriction primarily through two prison-security concerns:

1. prisoners might conceal contraband in beards; and
2. a prisoner could alter his appearance by shaving a beard and thereby complicate identification.

The Supreme Court did not declare prison security unimportant. The legal problem was that RLUIPA required Arkansas to demonstrate that imposing this burden on Holt was the least restrictive means of furthering compelling interests.

Invoking “security” did not itself complete that analysis.

## 5. The governing statute

RLUIPA provides heightened statutory protection for religious exercise in defined institutional settings. When a covered government imposes a substantial burden on an institutionalized person's religious exercise, even through a generally applicable rule, government must demonstrate that imposing the burden on that person furthers a compelling governmental interest and is the least restrictive means of doing so.

This is why the statement “religion is protected until it violates a law” is legally inadequate.

A generally applicable rule may itself be subject to a statutory religious-accommodation requirement.

RLUIPA also defines religious exercise broadly; a practice need not be compulsory or central to a faith before it can qualify.

## 6. Courts do not decide the correct Islam

The civil court's job was not to determine whether Holt had the best interpretation of Islamic beard doctrine. The legal inquiry concerned religious exercise, sincerity, burden, and government's justification.

This distinction matters far beyond Islam. American religious liberty would become unstable if government could protect only those practices judges regarded as theologically orthodox.

## 7. The lower courts

Holt sued in federal court after Arkansas denied his accommodation. At an evidentiary hearing, Arkansas witnesses presented the contraband and identification rationales.

A magistrate judge recommended dismissal. The lower-court analysis also emphasized that Holt remained able to engage in other forms of religious exercise, such as religious diet and observances.

The district court adopted the recommendation, and the Eighth Circuit affirmed.

The Supreme Court rejected the idea that government adequately answers a burden on one religious exercise merely by pointing to other religious exercises it still permits.

## 8. Supreme Court review

On March 3, 2014, the Supreme Court granted review limited to whether the Arkansas Department of Correction's grooming policy violated RLUIPA insofar as it prohibited Holt from growing a one-half-inch beard in accordance with his religious beliefs.

The narrow question is important. The Court was not deciding whether Islam was true, whether every religious accommodation must be granted, whether prisoners can disregard prison rules generally, or whether every grooming restriction is unlawful.

## 9. The United States supported Holt

The United States filed an amicus brief and participated in oral argument supporting Holt. The federal government's position was that Arkansas failed to demonstrate that banning the half-inch beard was the least restrictive means of advancing its asserted compelling interests.

This prevents a simplistic narrative of “Muslim prisoner versus America.” One part of American government defended Holt's religious-liberty claim against another government's policy.

## 10. Oral argument

The Court heard argument October 7, 2014. Douglas Laycock argued for Holt. Assistant to the Solicitor General Anthony A. Yang argued for the United States supporting Holt. Arkansas Deputy Attorney General David A. Curran argued for the state respondents.

The official transcript is especially valuable because the justices tested Arkansas's abstract security arguments against concrete alternatives: what could realistically be concealed in a half-inch beard, whether the beard could be searched, and whether identification concerns could be addressed through photographs.

All direct quotations used in the manuscript should be checked against the official transcript.

## 11. Respect is not unquestioning deference

Justice Alito's opinion recognized the expertise of prison officials but made clear that RLUIPA did not permit unquestioning deference.

That distinction is central:

> Expertise receives respect. It does not erase the government's statutory burden.

## 12. Contraband

Arkansas argued that beards could conceal contraband. The Court found the justification inadequate for Holt's proposed half-inch beard under the least-restrictive-means standard. Searching the beard was an obvious less restrictive alternative that Arkansas had not persuasively shown inadequate.

The legal point is not that contraband is trivial. It is that government claiming a restriction is the least restrictive means must seriously address workable alternatives.

## 13. Identification

Arkansas also argued that a prisoner could grow a beard and later shave it, altering his appearance. The Court identified an obvious alternative: maintain photographs showing the inmate both with and without the beard.

Again, the Court did not deny the importance of identification. It concluded that the record did not establish that prohibition was the least restrictive way to address the concern.

## 14. Other prison systems

The Court considered the fact that many other correctional systems permitted comparable religious beards. Their practices did not automatically bind Arkansas, but they weakened the assertion that Arkansas had no workable less restrictive alternative.

Arkansas could have attempted to demonstrate why its circumstances were materially different. The Court concluded the record did not persuasively do so.

## 15. The medical exception

Arkansas already tolerated quarter-inch beards for certain medical conditions. This complicated a categorical security rationale against short beards.

Do not misdescribe this as an Equal Protection holding. The case was decided under RLUIPA. The exception was relevant evidence in evaluating tailoring and alternatives.

## 16. Holding

The Supreme Court held that Arkansas's grooming policy violated RLUIPA insofar as it prevented Holt from growing his proposed half-inch beard in accordance with his religious beliefs. The Eighth Circuit judgment was reversed and the case remanded.

The result was unanimous.

## 17. Concurring opinions

Justice Ginsburg, joined by Justice Sotomayor, emphasized that accommodating Holt would not detrimentally affect others who did not share his belief. That observation is a concurrence, not the holding of the full Court.

Justice Sotomayor separately emphasized that appropriate deference to prison officials remained possible. Officials can satisfy RLUIPA when they actually demonstrate that a restriction imposed on a particular claimant is the least restrictive means of advancing a compelling interest.

Thus the case should not be summarized as judges claiming superior prison-management expertise.

## 18. What Holt did not hold

*Holt* did not hold that Sharia overrides Arkansas law, Muslims are generally exempt from prison rules, every prisoner religious request must be granted, security can never justify a religious burden, all grooming rules are invalid, or RLUIPA governs every governmental or private context.

It is principally a statutory RLUIPA decision, not a holding that the Free Exercise Clause independently produces the same result in every analogous case.

## 19. Constitutional floor versus statutory protection

This case illustrates a major Pass 23 distinction. The Constitution is not the only source of American religious-liberty protection. Legislatures can provide stronger protections than the constitutional floor.

RLUIPA supplies demanding protection in its covered land-use and institutionalized-person contexts. That is why neutral-law analysis under *Employment Division v. Smith* does not end a covered RLUIPA case.

## 20. Why Holt belongs in a book about Sharia

If Holt understood beard-growing as an Islamic obligation, it can reasonably be discussed as part of his lived Islamic normative practice. But the Supreme Court did not enforce Islamic law as sovereign law.

The structure was:

**Religious norm:** Holt believes Islam requires the beard.

**Civil rule:** Arkansas has a grooming policy.

**American law governing the collision:** RLUIPA.

**Judicial result:** Arkansas must accommodate Holt because Arkansas failed the American statutory test.

> Islam supplied the religious obligation. American law supplied the remedy.

## 21. Christian symmetry

The statutory architecture is not Muslim-specific. A Christian, Jewish, Sikh, Native religious practitioner or other claimant meeting RLUIPA's requirements receives the same legal framework.

That makes Holt useful to the book's symmetry test:

> The rule that protects the Muslim beard is the same rule that can protect the Christian conscience when the legal elements are met.

## 22. The anti-Sharia paradox

The phrase “no Sharia law in Arkansas” contains at least two very different possible propositions.

If it means Islamic doctrine possesses no independent sovereign civil authority over unwilling Arkansans, *Holt* does not contradict it.

If it means Arkansas may suppress a Muslim's religious practice merely because the practice is understood as Sharia, that proposition collides with American religious-liberty law.

This distinction should become explicit in the manuscript:

> “Sharia cannot replace the Constitution” and “Muslims may practice Islam” are not opposing ideas.

## 23. A definitional interview test

Ask:

> If a Muslim prisoner believes his faith requires a half-inch beard, do you consider that Sharia?

If yes, ask whether Arkansas should be permitted to prohibit it merely because it is Sharia.

If no, ask what the speaker means by Sharia.

The purpose is not a gotcha. It demonstrates why a single political word can collapse prayer, diet, dress, family ethics, contracts, criminal punishments and theories of government into one misleading category.

## 24. Later influence

DOJ materials have subsequently described *Holt* as requiring an individualized, context-specific and less deferential RLUIPA analysis. Later prisoner-religion litigation concerning grooming, diet, prayer caps and other accommodations has relied on the decision.

Any claim about downstream influence should be supported with specific later cases rather than saying Holt transformed all religious-liberty law.

## 25. Holt's later Arkansas litigation

DOJ materials from 2020 identify Gregory Holt as a plaintiff in later Arkansas RLUIPA litigation concerning religious materials and the statutory federal-funding coverage of correctional operations.

## 27. Claims safe for manuscript

With primary sourcing, the book may safely state that Holt was an Arkansas prisoner and devout Muslim; he believed his religion required a beard; he compromised by requesting a half-inch beard; Arkansas generally prohibited beards while allowing a quarter-inch medical exception; Arkansas invoked contraband and identification; the lower courts ruled against him; the United States supported him at the Supreme Court; the Supreme Court unanimously reversed; Justice Alito wrote the Court's opinion; and Arkansas failed RLUIPA's demanding statutory test on the record presented.

## 28. Claims to prohibit or heavily qualify

Do not write that the Supreme Court “ruled Sharia is protected by the Constitution,” “imposed Sharia on Arkansas,” made Muslims exempt from prison rules, held religious beliefs always trump law, held prison security non-compelling, or held that the First Amendment itself necessarily required the precise half-inch accommodation.

Do not attribute anti-Muslim motive to Arkansas officials without evidence establishing motive.

## Final finding

*Holt v. Hobbs* is a miniature of the book's central problem.

A Muslim has a religious rule. Arkansas has a civil rule. They collide.

The American system does not resolve the collision by deciding which religion should govern Arkansas. It applies American law to determine how far government may burden religious exercise.

Arkansas retains authority over its prisons. Islam does not become the state religion. Holt does not become sovereign. But the state is not sovereign over conscience either.

A federal statute establishes the boundary, and the Supreme Court unanimously concludes that Arkansas crossed it.

> Religious liberty protects the believer. Constitutional government protects everyone else. Neither requires the other to disappear.

For Gregory Holt, that boundary could be measured with a ruler.

It was half an inch.
