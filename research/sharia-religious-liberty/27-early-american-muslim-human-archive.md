# Pass 27 — Early American Muslim Human Archive

## Purpose

The founding chapters have so far dealt largely with Muslims as constitutional possibilities: the “Mahometan” in a religious-liberty argument, the hypothetical Muslim officeholder, the Muslim president imagined during ratification.

This pass changes the camera angle.

Muslims were not merely hypothetical people eighteenth-century Americans used to test the boundaries of religious liberty. Muslims were already here.

Many arrived through the Atlantic slave trade. Some were educated, literate, commercially experienced, legally trained, religiously learned, multilingual, or socially prominent before enslavement. Their surviving Arabic writings, portraits, property records, newspaper accounts and recollections make it impossible to treat Islam as a religion that suddenly appeared in America in the twentieth century.

The central narrative tension is therefore:

> America imagined the rights of the hypothetical Muslim while enslaving the real one.

This archive does not claim that every enslaved West African Muslim shared the same beliefs or legal traditions. It reconstructs identifiable individuals and grades the evidence separately for each.

---

# 1. Method

For each person distinguish:

- documented biographical fact;
- the person's own surviving words;
- contemporary descriptions;
- later recollection;
- specialist interpretation;
- disputed or legendary claims.

Do not romanticize literacy, elite status or Islamic education as making one enslaved person more deserving of liberty than another.

The point is not that these Muslims should have been free because some were scholars.

They should have been free because they were human beings.

Their education matters because it destroys the historical flattening that portrayed enslaved Africans as people without intellectual, legal, religious or political worlds of their own.

---

# 2. Ayuba Suleiman Diallo — Job ben Solomon

Ayuba Suleiman Diallo, known in English sources as Job ben Solomon, is one of the earliest extensively documented Muslims enslaved in British North America.

Born in West Africa into a prominent Muslim family, Diallo received an Islamic education and could read and write Arabic. In 1731 he was captured and transported through the Atlantic slave trade to Maryland.

His story became unusually well documented because of his literacy, social background and eventual contact with English patrons.

While enslaved in Maryland he attempted to preserve Islamic religious practice. A letter he wrote in Arabic helped reveal his background and eventually contributed to efforts that secured his freedom.

Diallo traveled to England and ultimately returned to West Africa.

His life predates the Declaration of Independence by decades.

## Why he matters

Islamic literacy and religious identity existed within British North America well before the American founding.

Potential line:

> Before Thomas Jefferson wrote the Declaration, a Muslim scholar had already been enslaved in Maryland, writing Arabic in a country that did not yet call itself the United States.

## Caution

Do not portray Diallo as representative of every enslaved African Muslim. His elite background and extraordinary documentation make him unusually visible.

---

# 3. Yarrow Mamout

Yarrow Mamout became one of the most visually recognizable early American Muslims because portraits of him survive.

Born in West Africa, he was brought to North America through slavery and eventually obtained his freedom. He lived for decades in Georgetown in the District of Columbia.

Evidence portrays him as a practicing Muslim and Arabic-literate African who, after emancipation, accumulated property and participated in Georgetown's economic life.

Charles Willson Peale painted Yarrow in 1819, leaving a remarkable visual record of an elderly African Muslim in the early American republic.

James Alexander Simpson also produced a portrait associated with Yarrow.

## Narrative importance

The portrait gives the reader something the constitutional debates cannot: a face.

When Americans at ratifying conventions imagined a “Mahometan,” men like Yarrow were not abstractions on another continent. Muslims of African origin were living within the developing United States.

## Important chronology

Yarrow's best-documented Georgetown life extends into the early nineteenth century, so do not falsely place every documented detail before 1788.

The stronger formulation is that his life spans the founding and early national periods.

---

# 4. Omar Ibn Said

Omar Ibn Said provides one of the most extraordinary surviving Muslim voices from American slavery.

Born in West Africa around 1770, Omar received substantial Islamic education before his capture and forced transportation to the United States in 1807.

He was enslaved in the Carolinas.

In 1831 he wrote an autobiography in Arabic.

The Library of Congress preserves the manuscript and describes it as the only known surviving autobiography of an enslaved person in the United States written in Arabic.

Omar begins his text with Qur'anic material, notably Surah al-Mulk.

That opening is enormously important, but it must not be overinterpreted without specialist analysis.

Scholars have debated how Omar's Qur'anic choices, Christian references and descriptions of his enslavers should be understood—including whether apparently deferential language contains coded criticism of slavery.

Those interpretations should be attributed to scholars rather than stated as self-evident fact.

## Narrative significance

Omar reverses the normal archive.

Instead of an American official describing a Muslim, the Muslim writes back.

Potential line:

> The founding generation debated whether a Muslim could hold office. A generation later, an enslaved Muslim in North Carolina was still writing his own story in Arabic.

---

# 5. Bilali Muhammad

Bilali Muhammad lived on Sapelo Island, Georgia, in the early nineteenth century and became one of the best-known Muslim figures associated with enslaved African communities in the American South.

He was literate in Arabic and produced a surviving Arabic manuscript commonly called the Bilali Document.

Scholars have connected the manuscript to Islamic jurisprudential/religious material, often specifically to the Maliki legal tradition.

This characterization should receive specialist verification before manuscript publication, especially if the book describes particular rules or identifies the exact source text behind Bilali's writing.

## Why Bilali matters

Bilali provides direct physical evidence that Islamic legal/religious learning did not remain on the other side of the Atlantic.

Arabic Islamic textual knowledge survived slavery and was reproduced in the American South.

That does **not** mean Islamic law governed Georgia.

It means a Muslim living under American slavery carried an Islamic intellectual tradition with him.

This distinction is central to the book:

> A person can carry a religious law inside his conscience without that religious law becoming the sovereign law of the state.

# 6. Other early Muslim lives

The archive should eventually expand beyond the four most famous names.

Research targets include:

- Salih Bilali;
- Lamine Kebe;
- Abd al-Rahman Ibrahima Sori;
- Muhammad Ali ben Said / Nicholas Said;
- Mahommah Gardo Baquaqua;
- additional Arabic-writing enslaved Africans identified through manuscript collections;
- Muslim communities and individuals associated with the Georgia Sea Islands;
- Muslim traces in Maryland, Virginia and the Carolinas;
- Muslim soldiers or sailors where evidence is reliable;
- early free Muslims where documentation survives.

Some of these lives fall later than the strict founding period. They remain relevant to the longer argument that Islam has deep roots in American history.

Each additional person requires an individual evidence dossier before inclusion.

---

# 7. How many Muslims were in early America?

The exact number is unknowable.

Modern scholarship and Library of Congress educational material have suggested that hundreds and possibly thousands of Muslims may have lived in North America around the Revolutionary/founding era, many of them enslaved Africans.

Population estimates should always be presented as estimates.

Never write a precise Muslim population number for 1776 as if a census recorded it.

## Safe formulation

> Historians cannot know the exact number, but Muslims were present in early America, many through the forced migration of the Atlantic slave trade, and surviving records identify individual Muslim lives with remarkable specificity.

---

# 8. Why the archive is biased

Survival of evidence is not survival of population.

The Muslims easiest for historians to identify are disproportionately those who:

- wrote Arabic;
- attracted elite white attention;
- appeared in newspapers;
- had unusual biographies;
- became free;
- owned property;
- produced manuscripts;
- sat for portraits;
- were remembered by descendants or enslavers.

Countless others may have left no identifiable record.

The absence of a written autobiography does not imply absence of Islamic identity.

Conversely, African origin alone cannot be treated as proof that a particular enslaved person was Muslim.

---

# 9. Islam and West African intellectual life

The Atlantic slave trade carried people from regions containing longstanding Muslim societies, Qur'anic schools, scholars, merchants, judges, political institutions and trans-Saharan intellectual networks.

Some captives therefore arrived in America with educational and religious backgrounds far more complex than the racial ideology of slavery acknowledged.

The book should resist the temptation to turn this into a simplistic “secret Islamic America.”

The historical significance is human and intellectual:

> Enslaved Africans did not arrive empty. They arrived with languages, memories, laws, prayers, arguments, families, histories and ideas.

Islam was one of those traditions.

---

# 10. Literacy as resistance and survival

Arabic literacy could function as continuity under conditions designed to sever identity.

Surviving writings demonstrate that some enslaved Muslims retained portions of Qur'anic learning, religious vocabulary and textual practice despite forced displacement.

But the book must be cautious with the word “resistance.”

Some writings may be reasonably interpreted as resistant acts; others may primarily reflect devotion, memory, communication, accommodation or survival.

Do not assign political intent to every Arabic manuscript.

---

# 11. Conversion and religious complexity

Several documented enslaved Muslims encountered Christianity in America.

Their later religious identities can be difficult to reconstruct.

Omar Ibn Said is the clearest warning against simple categories. His surviving writing contains Islamic and Christian language, and scholars disagree about how to understand his apparent Christianity, continued Islamic consciousness, accommodation to enslavers and possible coded meanings.

The book should not decide these questions more confidently than the evidence permits.

A person under slavery did not operate in conditions of ordinary religious choice.

That coercive context must remain visible.

---

# 12. The constitutional contradiction

This archive creates one of the book's deepest moral contradictions.

At the same historical moment Americans were developing increasingly expansive language about liberty of conscience, millions of enslaved people remained outside the practical protection of American liberty.

Some of them were Muslim.

Thus two histories run simultaneously:

**History A:** Americans debated whether a hypothetical Muslim could possess civil rights or hold federal office.

**History B:** actual Muslims were subjected to slavery and denied basic human freedom.

Neither history cancels the other.

The contradiction is the story.

Potential line:

> America imagined the rights of the hypothetical Muslim while enslaving the real one.

---

# 13. Why this does not invalidate founding religious liberty

The existence of slavery does not make the religious-liberty language meaningless.

Nor can religious-liberty language be used to make slavery disappear.

The historically honest conclusion is that American principles were often larger than the circle of people initially allowed to enjoy them.

That is not an excuse for exclusion.

It explains why later Americans could use founding principles against founding practices.

Potential line:

> The American idea could be larger than the Americans who first articulated it.

This should be written as interpretation, not as absolution.

---

# 14. Muslims were not imported as a constitutional theory

The presence of enslaved Muslims does not prove Islamic law influenced the Constitution.

That would repeat the causation error rejected throughout the project.

Their significance is different:

- Islam was physically present in America;
- Muslims were part of the human population to whom universal language could eventually be applied;
- Islamic religious practice existed under conditions of extreme coercion;
- the question of Muslim belonging is therefore older than modern immigration politics.

---

# 15. The “foreign religion” problem

Modern rhetoric frequently describes Islam as foreign to American history.

That proposition becomes difficult to sustain if “foreign” means historically absent from America.

Islam has been practiced on American soil for centuries.

However, avoid the opposite overstatement that Islam was a dominant or institutionally equivalent founding religion.

Christianity overwhelmingly dominated colonial and founding-era public culture.

Both propositions can be true:

> America was overwhelmingly Christian in culture, and Muslims were already here.

This is precisely the kind of historical complexity the book should preserve.

---

# 16. Black Muslim history must not begin with the Nation of Islam

Twentieth-century Black Muslim movements are enormously important, but African Muslim presence in North America predates them by centuries.

The early archive gives the Arkansas reporting project an important warning: when reconstructing Black Muslim history in Arkansas, do not assume Black Islam begins with twentieth-century organizations.

The national history is much older.

Whether a direct line can be drawn from enslaved African Muslims to later Black Muslim institutions is a separate historical question and should not be assumed without evidence.

# 18. Humanization rules

These people must not become props for the thesis.

Do not introduce Omar merely as “proof Muslims were here.”

Do not introduce Yarrow merely as “the Muslim in a portrait.”

Do not introduce Bilali merely as “evidence of Sharia.”

Do not introduce Ayuba merely as “the literate slave.”

Each should first appear as a person with a life before becoming evidence in an argument.

Narrative order:

1. person;
2. place;
3. life before enslavement where recoverable;
4. rupture/capture;
5. life in America;
6. religious/intellectual survival;
7. surviving artifact;
8. only then constitutional meaning.

---

# 19. Language rules

Prefer “enslaved person” to language that makes slavery an identity.

Use historical terms such as “Mahometan” only in quotations or when explaining period vocabulary.

Do not casually call all Arabic-writing Africans “Islamic scholars.”

Distinguish Qur'anic literacy, Arabic literacy, formal jurisprudential training and elite scholarly status.

Do not assume Arabic literacy proves identical levels of Islamic education.

# 20. Chapter 13 architecture — Muslims Who Were Already Here

## Opening

Do not open with a population statistic.

Open with a person.

Best candidates after full verification:

- Yarrow's face in Peale's portrait;
- Omar putting Arabic onto an American page;
- Ayuba's Arabic letter crossing the Atlantic world.

## Reveal 1

The reader has just spent chapters watching white Americans debate hypothetical Muslims.

Then say, in substance:

> There was one problem with treating the Muslim as hypothetical. Muslims were already here.

## Reveal 2

Introduce several lives rather than making one person representative.

## Reveal 3

Show intellectual/religious worlds carried across the Atlantic.

## Reveal 4

Bring in slavery.

Do not soften it.

## Ending

Transition into Chapter 14's explicit contradiction:

> The Constitution could imagine a Muslim in public office. American slavery could still own a Muslim's body.

That contradiction deserves its own chapter rather than being resolved in a paragraph.

# 21. Chapter 14 architecture — Hypothetical Muslim and Enslaved Muslim

Chapter 14 should put the two archives side by side.

On one side:

- Jefferson's Muslim civil-rights language;
- Lee's “Mahomitan” freedom;
- Washington's inclusion;
- Article VI;
- North Carolina's Muslim officeholder debate.

On the other:

- Ayuba;
- Yarrow;
- Omar;
- Bilali;
- the wider enslaved Muslim population.

Do not claim the founders personally knew these specific individuals unless evidence establishes it.

The juxtaposition is historical, not necessarily interpersonal.

Central question:

> What does universal liberty mean when the society speaking it refuses to recognize the humanity of people already within its reach?

This is not solely a Muslim question. It is an American question.

# 23. Claims safe for manuscript

With citations and appropriate qualification:

- Muslims lived in North America before the United States existed.
- some arrived through the Atlantic slave trade from Muslim regions of West Africa.
- identifiable enslaved Muslims retained Arabic literacy and Islamic religious knowledge.
- Ayuba Suleiman Diallo was enslaved in Maryland decades before independence.
- Yarrow Mamout lived through the founding/early national era and became a free Georgetown property owner.
- Omar Ibn Said wrote a surviving Arabic autobiography while enslaved in the United States.
- Bilali Muhammad left a surviving Arabic Islamic manuscript in Georgia.
- the exact Muslim population of founding-era America is unknowable.
- early American Muslims complicate claims that Islam is historically new to America.
- their existence does not prove Islamic influence on the Constitution.

# 26. Final finding

The Muslim in founding-era political argument was not purely imaginary.

Long before modern mosque controversies, immigration debates or congressional arguments about Islam, Muslim people had already been carried into North America, often in chains.

Some prayed here.

Some wrote Arabic here.

Some memorized Qur'anic passages here.

Some acquired freedom and property here.

Some died enslaved here.

The evidence does not make America secretly Islamic.

It makes the history of American Islam unmistakably American.

And it creates the contradiction the book cannot avoid:

> Americans were capable of imagining a Muslim holding federal office before they were willing to recognize the freedom of actual Muslims living among them.

The hypothetical Muslim tested the Constitution.

The enslaved Muslim tested the nation.
