# Research Pass 15 — The Founders’ Complete Islam File

## Purpose

This pass tests the project’s central religious-liberty thesis against the fullest founding-era record we can assemble. It deliberately includes material favorable to Muslims, hostile to Islam, diplomatically adversarial, stereotyped, theological, commercial, constitutional, and rhetorical.

The goal is not to prove that the founders admired Islam. The goal is to determine whether Muslim civil equality and religious liberty in the early republic depended upon admiration of Islam. The evidence strongly suggests it did not.

## Governing finding

The founders’ record on Islam is mixed in sentiment but much clearer in constitutional structure.

Founding-era Americans could distrust Muslim governments, describe Muslim societies through stereotypes, fight Barbary states, condemn religious justifications for piracy and enslavement, and still defend the civil rights of Muslims or accept constitutional rules that did not disqualify Muslims from citizenship or federal office.

This matters because it makes the religious-liberty argument harder to dismiss as sentimental multiculturalism.

**Religious liberty was not premised on believing Islam was benign. It was premised on refusing to make religious approval the condition of civil equality.**

## 1. Jefferson: Qur’an, Locke, religious liberty, and hostile diplomacy

### A. Jefferson owned a Qur’an while studying law

The Library of Congress confirms that Jefferson owned George Sale’s English translation of the Qur’an, apparently purchased in 1765 while he was studying law at William & Mary. The Library says it is likely he valued it in part as an example of Arabic law, as his legal textbooks suggested. His surviving copy contains no substantive annotations beyond ownership initials.

Evidence grade: **A — documented possession and timing.**

Do not claim the Qur’an directly supplied constitutional provisions.

### B. Jefferson’s 1776 Locke notes expressly include Muslims

Jefferson copied/summarized Locke’s toleration argument that neither pagan, “Mahamedan,” nor Jew should be excluded from civil rights because of religion.

Evidence grade: **A — direct textual evidence.**

This shows that Muslims were not outside Jefferson’s conceptual universe when he was formulating religious-liberty ideas in 1776.

### C. Virginia religious liberty was framed universally

Jefferson’s Bill for Establishing Religious Freedom states that religious opinion should not diminish, enlarge, or affect civil capacities. In his later autobiography, Jefferson recalled the defeat of an amendment inserting “Jesus Christ” into the bill’s preamble and interpreted that defeat as proof the protection included “the Jew and the Gentile, the Christian and Mahometan, the Hindoo, and infidel.”

Important caveat: editors of Founders Online note there is **no surviving legislative-journal record of this particular amendment**. The statement is therefore Jefferson’s later recollection, not independently verified journal evidence.

Evidence grade: **A for Jefferson’s recollection; B/C for the underlying legislative event because the journal record is missing.**

### D. Jefferson’s diplomacy with Muslim governments was not romantic

Jefferson and John Adams dealt directly with Barbary governments and their representatives. In their 1786 report of a conversation with Tripoli’s envoy, they recorded a justification of warfare and enslavement grounded in the envoy’s description of the laws of the Prophet and Qur’an.

That encounter was adversarial and disturbing from their perspective.

This is crucial: Jefferson’s Muslim-inclusive conception of civil rights did not require him to regard every Islamic political interpretation as peaceful or admirable.

**Strong line:** “Jefferson did not have to believe every Islamic interpretation was benign to believe Muslims had civil rights.”

## 2. George Washington: competence, conscience, and Muslim inclusion

### A. “Mahometans” at Mount Vernon

In a 1784 letter about hiring or acquiring skilled workers, Washington wrote that workers could be “Mahometans, Jews, or Christian of any Sect—or they may be Athiests,” so long as they were good workmen.

Critical context: the letter exists inside Washington’s participation in an unfree labor/slavery economy. It must not be romanticized.

Still, the religious criterion is explicit: theology was not the qualification.

Evidence grade: **A.**

### B. Virginia religious assessment

In 1785 Washington wrote George Mason about a proposed religious assessment. Washington was less absolutist than Madison, but he explicitly contemplated relief for “Jews, Mahomitans or otherwise.”

Evidence grade: **A.**

### C. Presidential religious-liberty doctrine

Washington later told Baptists, Quakers, and Jews that conscience and religious liberty were rights rather than favors, and that good citizenship—not theological conformity—was the legitimate civil standard.

The Newport letter to the Hebrew Congregation is especially important because it rejects mere toleration as the indulgence of one class toward another.

Evidence grade: **A.**

Inference: Muslims logically fit this broader doctrine because Washington had expressly named them elsewhere. That inference is strong but should still be labeled as inference rather than a direct Muslim-specific presidential statement.

## 3. James Madison: universal conscience without a Muslim-specific slogan

Pass 15 again found no clean Madison quotation equivalent to Jefferson’s explicit “Mahamedan” line in which Madison himself names Muslims while articulating the First Amendment principle.

That absence matters.

Do **not** write that Madison drafted the First Amendment “for Muslims.”

What Madison did do is more structurally important: he rejected toleration in favor of equal free exercise, opposed religious assessments, argued that government could not privilege Christianity without empowering it to privilege one Christian sect over another, and proposed constitutional language protecting the “full and equal rights of conscience.”

Evidence grade: **A for the universal rule; no direct Muslim-specific Madison quote established in this pass.**

**Defensible formulation:** “Madison built a rule that did not require government to decide which religion was worthy of civil equality.”

---

## 4. Richard Henry Lee: direct Muslim-inclusive liberty

In a November 26, 1784 letter to Madison, Richard Henry Lee wrote that “true freedom embraces the Mahomitan and the Gentoo as well as the Christian religion.”

This is direct, contemporaneous, founding-era evidence from a major revolutionary figure.

Evidence grade: **A.**

It is especially useful because it demonstrates that Muslim-inclusive religious liberty was not a Jefferson-only eccentricity.

---

## 5. Benjamin Rush: Islam as morally preferable to irreligion

Benjamin Rush, signer of the Declaration and major early republican thinker, wrote that he would rather see the opinions of Confucius or “Mahomed” taught to American youth than see them grow up without any system of religious principles.

Rush still expressly preferred Christianity and the New Testament.

That complexity matters.

He was not claiming Islam and Christianity were identical or equally true. He was saying Islam could function as a morally serious religion within his republican framework.

Evidence grade: **A.**

## 6. Benjamin Franklin: Islam as satire, not simply tolerance

Franklin’s final public writing, “On the Slave Trade” (1790), invented a Muslim North African speaker, Sidi Mehemet Ibrahim, defending the enslavement of Christians using arguments mirroring those made by American defenders of Black slavery.

Franklin’s target was American slavery.

But the satire depended on an American audience recognizing Barbary Muslim slaveholding as morally repellent.

This is important evidence against any simplistic claim that leading founders merely admired Islam.

Evidence grade: **A.**

Interpretive significance:

- Franklin used Muslim imagery as a rhetorical mirror.
- He assumed readers would recognize the hypocrisy of condemning Muslim enslavement of Christians while defending American enslavement of Africans.
- The text tells us about American images of Muslims as well as about slavery.

**Strong line:** “Franklin turned the Muslim slaveholder into a mirror and asked Americans whether they recognized themselves.”

## 7. John Adams: war, diplomacy, and constitutional coexistence

### A. Barbary conflict

Adams wrote in 1786 that the United States was at war with Morocco, Algiers, Tunis, and Tripoli and described the legal-diplomatic reality that nations without treaties could be treated as enemies by those states.

He and Jefferson regarded securing peace as strategically vital.

Evidence grade: **A.**

### B. Adams did not treat Muslim diplomatic conflict as proof Muslims lacked civil rights

The founding generation’s diplomatic experience included capture of ships, tribute demands, enslavement of sailors, religious rhetoric, and difficult negotiations.

That history makes later religious-neutrality language more—not less—important. Americans had actual reasons to fear particular Muslim governments and still did not write a federal Christian test into the Constitution.

### C. Treaty of Tripoli

The English text of the 1796/1797 Treaty of Tripoli contains the famous Article 11 statement that the U.S. government “is not, in any sense, founded on the Christian religion” and has no character of hostility toward the religion or tranquility of Muslims.

The Senate consented unanimously among senators present, and President John Adams ratified and proclaimed the treaty.

Evidence grade: **A for the American government’s adoption of the English text.**

Critical caveat: the surviving Arabic version does **not** contain a corresponding Article 11; the translation history is anomalous and unresolved. Therefore the treaty should not be used as simplistic proof that America was culturally non-Christian.

What it safely proves is narrower and important: the U.S. government formally adopted an English treaty text publicly disclaiming governmental religious hostility toward Muslims and describing the federal government as not founded on Christianity.

## 8. North Carolina ratification: the Muslim president was an explicit possibility

The 1788 North Carolina convention remains among the strongest sources in the entire project.

Opponents of Article VI warned that without a religious test, “pagans, deists, and Mahometans” could hold office.

James Iredell defended the principle of general religious liberty and argued that excluding sets of people on religious grounds would destroy the very principle of religious freedom.

William Lancaster warned that “Mahometans may take” the presidential chair.

Governor Samuel Johnston acknowledged that Muslims could be chosen if they acquired the public’s confidence and esteem through good conduct and virtue.

Evidence grade: **A.**

Core conclusion:

**The Muslim president was not a loophole discovered centuries later. The possibility was named during ratification.**

## 9. Massachusetts and other state-level evidence

Library of Congress research records Massachusetts officials asserting that the Massachusetts Constitution provided ample liberty of conscience to Deists, Muslims, Jews, and Christians.

The broader state record also shows the opposite pattern: many states retained explicitly Christian or Protestant religious tests for office even while the federal Constitution rejected such a test.

This distinction must remain central.

The founding era did not instantly create universal religious equality across every state.

It created a federal constitutional break from religious-test orthodoxy while state practices remained inconsistent and often discriminatory.

Evidence grade: **A/B depending the individual state source.**

Open research task: complete a state-by-state inventory of religious tests and explicit Muslim references before final publication.

## 10. Chesterfield County petition: ordinary citizens, not only elite founders

A 1785 Chesterfield County, Virginia petition argued that Jews, Muslims, and Christians of every denomination should enjoy religious liberty and warned against establishing Christianity by law.

This is important because it widens the record beyond famous founders.

Evidence grade: **A as reported in Library of Congress manuscript research.**

Strong implication: Muslim-inclusive liberty existed in public political discourse, not merely private elite philosophy.

## 11. Hostile and stereotyped descriptions must remain in the book

American diplomatic correspondence contains descriptions of Muslim North African societies that are dismissive, racialized, stereotyped, or openly contemptuous.

Examples include descriptions of Moors or Arabs as fierce, avaricious, ignorant, or contemptuous of Christians; reports that Algerian officials sought conversions; and references to rulers using religious law as justification or pretext in international affairs.

These cannot be omitted if the book claims to present the complete record.

Evidence grade: **A as evidence of what particular American observers said; not evidence that the stereotypes were accurate descriptions of Islam or Muslim societies generally.**

This distinction is essential.

## 12. The founders encountered real religious conflict with Muslim powers

The Barbary relationship was not imaginary Islamophobia.

American sailors were captured and enslaved.

Tribute was demanded.

Diplomats reported religiously inflected arguments for warfare.

American officials struggled with governments whose legal and political assumptions differed sharply from those of the new republic.

The correct historical conclusion is not that early American fear was baseless.

It is that **conflict with Muslim governments did not become a constitutional rule disqualifying Muslims as citizens.**

That is the more powerful point.

## 13. The crucial distinction: Muslim governments versus Muslim persons

Pass 15 reveals that founding-era Americans often distinguished, whether consistently or imperfectly, between:

1. a Muslim state or ruler acting as a geopolitical adversary;
2. Islamic theology or law as an object of criticism;
3. a Muslim individual’s civil rights inside the republic.

Modern Sharia rhetoric frequently collapses these categories.

A dispute with an Islamist government becomes suspicion of a Muslim neighbor.

A medieval juristic doctrine becomes evidence of an American citizen’s political intentions.

The founding record is often more sophisticated than that collapse.

## 14. What we did NOT find

This pass did not establish:

- that Jefferson derived constitutional provisions from the Qur’an;
- that Madison studied Sharia as a constitutional model;
- that Washington admired Islam as a theological system;
- that Franklin regarded Islam as equivalent to Christianity;
- that the founders unanimously welcomed Islam;
- that Muslim civil equality existed uniformly in all states;
- that the Constitution was secretly Islamic;
- that hostility to Muslim governments never existed;
- that every founder agreed on church-state separation in modern terms.

These negative findings are as important as the positive ones.

## 15. What the evidence DOES support

### A-grade conclusions

1. Jefferson owned a Qur’an during his law-study period.
2. Jefferson explicitly included Muslims in his 1776 religious-liberty notes.
3. Jefferson later described his Virginia statute as protecting Muslims among other faiths, with the legislative-amendment caveat.
4. Washington explicitly named Muslims in private and policy discussions without treating their faith as automatic civil disqualification.
5. Richard Henry Lee explicitly said true freedom embraces Muslims as well as Christians.
6. North Carolina ratifiers openly discussed Muslims holding federal office and the presidency.
7. Article VI remained religion-neutral despite that concern.
8. Benjamin Rush could describe Islamic moral teaching as preferable to irreligion while remaining explicitly Christian.
9. Franklin used a fictional Muslim slaveholder to expose American proslavery hypocrisy.
10. Early U.S. diplomacy involved serious and sometimes violent conflict with Muslim governments.
11. The American government adopted the English Treaty of Tripoli text containing explicit assurances of no religious hostility toward Muslims.
12. Muslim-inclusive religious-liberty language existed among ordinary citizens as well as famous founders.

## 16. The strongest thesis produced by Pass 15

The evidence does not show founders celebrating Islam.

It shows something more constitutionally significant.

**Some of the same Americans who knew Islam through war, captivity, slavery, piracy, polemic, diplomacy, religious disagreement, and stereotype still defended a civil order in which Muslim belief was not supposed to erase civil rights.**

That is the story.

Religious liberty is meaningful precisely because it does not require religious admiration.

## 19. Evidence discipline for final publication

Every final claim should distinguish among:

- founder’s own words;
- correspondent’s words received by a founder;
- later founder recollection;
- official diplomatic report;
- newspaper/pamphlet rhetoric;
- constitutional text;
- ratification debate;
- later scholarly interpretation.

Never attribute a correspondent’s prejudice to Washington, Madison, Jefferson, or Adams simply because the document appears in their papers.

Never turn diplomatic descriptions of Barbary states into descriptions of Islam generally.

Never use Jefferson’s Qur’an ownership as proof of borrowing.

Never use the Treaty of Tripoli without the Arabic-text caveat.

Never use Jefferson’s “Jesus Christ amendment” recollection without noting the absence of a journal record.

Never say the founders were unanimous.

## Final Pass 15 conclusion

**The founding generation did not solve Islam. It did something more important. It began constructing a constitutional order in which Islam did not have to be solved before a Muslim could possess civil rights.**

The most defensible formulation is therefore:

**The founders’ relationship with Islam was complicated, sometimes hostile, and often shaped by eighteenth-century prejudice. Yet Muslim inclusion appears repeatedly in the very debates that defined American religious liberty. That means the Muslim was not outside the constitutional experiment. The Muslim was one of its earliest tests.**
