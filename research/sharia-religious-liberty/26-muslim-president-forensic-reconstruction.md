# Pass 26 — The Muslim President: Forensic Reconstruction of the North Carolina Ratification Debate

## Purpose

This pass reconstructs the July 30, 1788 North Carolina ratifying-convention debate over Article VI's prohibition on religious tests.

The point is not to manufacture a founding-era consensus that Americans welcomed a Muslim president. They did not.

The point is more precise and more important:

> Americans explicitly recognized during ratification that the proposed federal Constitution contained no Christian religious test capable of excluding Muslims from federal office, including the presidency.

The record contains both alarm and defense. That is what makes it historically powerful.

The debate proves that Muslim eligibility was not an accidental loophole discovered centuries later. It was one of the consequences contemporaries actually discussed.

---

# 1. Historical setting

The North Carolina ratifying convention opened at Hillsborough on July 21, 1788.

By then the proposed Constitution had already secured the nine ratifications needed for implementation among ratifying states. Virginia had ratified on June 25, and New York narrowly ratified on July 26.

North Carolina therefore confronted a Constitution that was no longer merely theoretical. A new federal government was becoming a political reality.

North Carolina itself, however, still operated under its 1776 constitution, which contained an explicit religious qualification for state office. Article XXXII declared that no person who denied the being of God, the truth of the Protestant religion, or the divine authority of the Old or New Testaments could hold civil office of trust or profit in the state.

That state background matters enormously.

Delegates were not debating religious tests in the abstract.

They came from a constitutional system that knew exactly how to write one.

---

# 2. Article VI enters the room

On Wednesday, July 30, 1788, the convention reached the final clause of Article VI.

The federal text required federal and state officials to support the Constitution by oath or affirmation but then declared:

> “no religious Test shall ever be required as a Qualification to any Office or public Trust under the United States.”

This was structurally different from North Carolina's own religious qualification.

The debate that followed reveals that delegates understood the difference.

---

# 3. Henry Abbot states the fear

Henry Abbot opened the recorded discussion by presenting concerns circulating among constituents.

He worried about federal interference with religious liberty, including an imagined treaty that could impose Roman Catholicism. He then turned directly to the absence of religious tests.

Abbot said many regarded their exclusion as dangerous because, without such tests, “pagans, deists, and Mahometans” might obtain public offices and senators and representatives might all be pagans.

He also asked how officials would swear their oaths if no religious test existed, joking about pagan gods such as Jupiter and Juno.

## What Abbot proves

Abbot is evidence of fear, not Muslim inclusion as an ideal.

But his fear establishes an important constitutional inference:

**He understood Article VI to remove a religious barrier that otherwise could exclude Muslims from federal office.**

That inference was not supplied by a modern historian. It was articulated in the ratification debate itself.

### Claim grade

**A — Direct primary-source evidence that contemporaries believed the no-test clause could make Muslims eligible for federal office.**

Do not convert Abbot into a supporter of Muslim equality.

---

# 4. James Iredell answers the objection

James Iredell, one of North Carolina's leading Federalists and later a justice of the United States Supreme Court, responded at length.

His response is the constitutional core of the episode.

Iredell argued that religious tests had produced persecution and that people in power tended to presume their own religious correctness. He described the federal no-test provision as evidence of an intention to establish broad religious liberty.

Most importantly for this project, Iredell confronted the Muslim objection directly.

He acknowledged the concern that people with no religion, pagans, and “Mahometans” could enter office, then asked how any class of people could be excluded without surrendering the principle of religious freedom.

He warned that once government was permitted to discriminate among religious groups, the door to persecution was opened.

Iredell's argument was not that Islam was true.

It was not that Muslims would probably win office.

It was that **religious exclusion was itself the constitutional danger.**

## Safe synthesis

> Iredell did not answer the Muslim objection by saying Muslims could never hold office. He answered it by attacking the premise that government should exclude a religious class from office at all.

### Claim grade

**A — Direct defense of general religious liberty in response to Muslim eligibility concerns.**

---

# 5. Iredell's “universal religious liberty” language

Iredell ultimately described Article VI as calculated to secure “universal religious liberty” by “putting all sects on a level.”

Those words must be used carefully.

They do not mean that eighteenth-century American society actually treated every religion equally.

They do not erase state religious tests, social prejudice, slavery, or other exclusions.

But they do establish how one major ratification defender publicly interpreted the federal clause:

**the absence of a test was a protection against governmental religious hierarchy.**

For the manuscript, distinguish the constitutional aspiration from lived reality.

---

# 6. The oath discussion reveals another layer

Abbot pressed Iredell on how an oath could work without a Christian test.

Iredell responded that the form of an oath should bind the conscience of the person taking it. He discussed Jewish, Christian, and non-Christian oath forms and cited an English example involving a “Gentoo” witness allowed to swear according to his own religious practice.

This portion of the debate is useful because it shows that religious pluralism was being treated as a practical legal problem, not merely as abstract philosophy.

The question was:

If the government does not require every officeholder or witness to be Christian, how does it obtain a meaningful oath from people with different beliefs?

Iredell's answer was essentially conscience-based rather than denomination-based.

## Caveat

Iredell still spoke within eighteenth-century assumptions about belief in a Supreme Being and future rewards and punishments. Do not retroactively convert his position into modern constitutional doctrine concerning atheists.

---

# 7. Governor Samuel Johnston makes the decisive concession

Governor Samuel Johnston's intervention is among the strongest pieces of evidence in the entire book.

Johnston initially treated fears of a non-Christian president as highly improbable. He compared alarm about the pope becoming president with imagining a foreign king or the “Grand Turk” becoming president.

But then he confronted the actual constitutional possibility.

Johnston acknowledged that Jews, Muslims, pagans and others might be elected to high federal office.

He said a non-Christian president might emerge in two circumstances:

1. if American society ceased to be predominantly Christian; or
2. if such a person, **despite his religion**, acquired the confidence and esteem of the American people through good conduct and virtue.

This is extraordinarily significant.

Johnston did not argue that Article VI secretly contained a Christian qualification.

He did not claim a Muslim president would be constitutionally invalid.

He said the practical barrier was electoral confidence—not constitutional religious identity.

## Strong manuscript formulation

> Samuel Johnston thought a Muslim president unlikely. He did not think one unconstitutional.

Or:

> The founding-era answer was not “a Muslim can never be president.” Johnston's answer was: if a Muslim earned the confidence of the people, the Constitution would not stop them from choosing him.

### Claim grade

**A — Direct primary-source acknowledgment of the constitutional possibility of a Muslim president or other high federal officeholder.**

---

# 8. Johnston was not preaching modern pluralism

Johnston's remarks also contain evidence that must remain visible.

He hoped Christianity would continue to predominate and suggested that children of non-Christian immigrants might become Christians.

This is essential context.

He was defending a religiously neutral federal rule from within a society he expected and preferred to remain overwhelmingly Christian.

That distinction is one of the book's central themes:

> A deeply Christian political culture could still produce a federal constitutional rule that did not make Christianity the qualification for office.

This is stronger history than portraying Johnston as a twenty-first-century pluralist displaced into 1788.

---

# 9. David Caldwell presents the cultural counterargument

The Reverend David Caldwell argued that the absence of religious tests might invite Jews and pagans into the country and eventually endanger its character.

He considered Christianity the religion best suited to producing good citizens and thought the Constitution should not have created such an invitation to non-Christian immigrants.

Caldwell gives the manuscript a serious founding-era version of the “Christian America” objection.

He should not be mocked or omitted.

His position demonstrates the genuine choice being argued:

- should the federal Constitution reinforce the country's Christian religious character through political qualifications?
- or should public office remain legally open without a denominational test?

The Constitution as proposed chose the latter.

---

# 10. Samuel Spencer attacks tests as ineffective and persecutory

Samuel Spencer defended freedom of conscience and opposed establishing a particular religion.

He argued that religious tests punish conscientious people while failing to restrain unscrupulous people, who can simply swear insincerely.

His answer to fears about deists, atheists, and others was electoral rather than theological: would the people knowingly choose people of bad character?

Spencer said the absence of a religious test left religion on the foundation of its own validity rather than temporal authority.

This is important because it reveals a second pro-Article-VI argument:

religious tests are not merely oppressive; they are **bad filters**.

They exclude the sincere and can be evaded by the dishonest.

---

# 11. Richard Dobbs Spaight states equal eligibility most plainly

Richard Dobbs Spaight, a signer of the federal Constitution, entered the discussion after the other speakers.

His formulation may be the cleanest.

He argued that no sect was preferred and that no religious test was required. He then stated that people of equal capacity and integrity were equally eligible to office.

He also said he did not expect an infidel to be selected unless voters themselves shared that outlook.

Again the distinction is clear:

**the constitutional rule did not impose the religious barrier; political choice might.**

### Claim grade

**A — Direct ratification-era interpretation that office eligibility was not denominationally restricted by the federal Constitution.**

---

# 12. William Lancaster delivers the famous line

William Lancaster then gave the sentence that has become the centerpiece of modern discussion.

While considering presidential qualifications, Lancaster said he had not imagined the pope could occupy the president's chair. But because the government was being designed for generations not yet born, he could not know the distant future.

He warned that Catholics might occupy the chair and that “Mahometans may take it,” adding that he saw nothing in the Constitution preventing it.

## Critical interpretive rule

Lancaster was **not celebrating** this possibility.

He was arguing that the Constitution should contain a religious disqualification.

His statement is constitutionally important precisely because an opponent identified the consequence of Article VI and objected to it.

## Best manuscript line

> “Mahometans may take it” was not a promise. It was a warning.

Then:

> But warnings can be evidence. Lancaster had gone looking through the Constitution for the Christian barrier that would prevent a Muslim from taking the president's chair. He could not find one.

### Claim grade

**A — Direct evidence that a delegate understood the Constitution to permit a future Muslim president.**

Do not write that Lancaster supported a Muslim president.

---

# 13. What did “that chair” mean?

The context resolves the ambiguity.

Lancaster explicitly referred to “the qualifications necessary for a President” and then to the pope occupying “the President's chair.” His following sentence about Catholics and Muslims taking “that chair” therefore refers to the presidency.

This is not a modern extrapolation from a vague reference to public office.

The presidency is explicit in the immediate text.

---

# 14. The debate was larger than Lancaster

A weak version of the argument would build the entire claim around Lancaster's isolated sentence.

The full record is much stronger.

On the same day:

- Abbot said people feared Muslims could obtain federal office without a test;
- Iredell defended the no-test principle as religious liberty;
- Johnston explicitly addressed Muslims becoming president or holding other high office;
- Caldwell objected to constitutional openness toward non-Christians;
- Spencer attacked religious tests;
- Spaight defended equal eligibility absent a test;
- Lancaster warned explicitly that a Muslim might take the president's chair.

The evidentiary unit is therefore not one colorful quotation.

It is an extended constitutional argument.

---

# 15. The Muslim was functioning as a stress test

The debate did not center on a large Muslim voting bloc in North Carolina.

“Mahometan” functioned partly as an extreme hypothetical—a religious outsider far beyond the dominant Protestant world of many delegates.

That does not make the evidence less valuable.

It makes the constitutional role clearer.

The Muslim served as a boundary question:

> Does religious liberty still apply when the possible officeholder belongs to a religion most voters do not share and may distrust?

This is the historical origin of the project's recurring motif:

> **The Muslim is the test.**

Use this as interpretation, not as a quotation from the founders.

---

# 16. North Carolina's own constitution makes the debate sharper

North Carolina's 1776 constitution contained an explicit Protestant/biblical office qualification.

So when delegates complained that the federal Constitution allowed Muslims into office, they already possessed a model demonstrating how to stop that result.

The federal Constitution simply did not adopt such language.

This gives Pass 22 and Pass 26 a powerful combined conclusion:

> Americans knew how to write a Christian religious test. North Carolina had one. When its delegates examined the federal Constitution, they noticed that the federal document did not.

This avoids any argument from silence.

We have both the state comparator and the contemporaneous reaction to the federal omission.

---

# 17. Convention chronology after July 30

The Hillsborough convention did **not** ratify the Constitution immediately after this debate.

On August 1, 1788, the convention chose, by a large majority, neither to ratify nor reject the Constitution and instead called for a declaration of rights and amendments before ratification.

North Carolina remained outside the newly operating federal union for more than another year.

The second state convention met at Fayetteville in November 1789.

North Carolina finally ratified the Constitution on **November 21, 1789**, becoming the twelfth state.

The exact vote count varies slightly among later references because of reporting/transcription discrepancies; National Archives materials commonly give **194–77**. Use the official/documentary source selected during final fact-check rather than casually mixing 194, 195, 197, or 184 from secondary summaries.

## Manuscript correction

Never imply that the July 30 debate culminated in immediate North Carolina ratification.

It did not.

---

# 18. The federal government began without North Carolina

The federal government began operating in 1789 while North Carolina still had not ratified.

George Washington was inaugurated April 30, 1789.

North Carolina ratified only in November.

This matters narratively because the July 1788 debate was not a ceremonial conversation after an inevitable vote.

Delegates were genuinely deciding whether their state would join the new constitutional system.

---

# 19. What the debate proves

## A-grade conclusions

### 1. Article VI's no-religious-test rule was noticed and debated.

It was not an obscure technical clause whose implications went unnoticed.

### 2. Muslims were explicitly invoked as possible federal officeholders.

Multiple participants mentioned “Mahometans.”

### 3. The presidency was explicitly included.

Lancaster and Johnston both discussed non-Christian presidential eligibility.

### 4. At least some Federalist defenders rejected religious exclusion as inconsistent with religious liberty.

Iredell is the strongest example.

### 5. At least some defenders understood the federal Constitution to make religious identity legally irrelevant to office eligibility, leaving practical selection to voters.

Johnston and Spaight are especially strong.

### 6. Opponents understood that a religious test would be required if Muslims were to be categorically excluded.

Lancaster's complaint demonstrates this directly.

---

# 20. What the debate does not prove

It does **not** prove:

- that most founders wanted a Muslim president;
- that most North Carolinians favored Muslim political equality;
- that eighteenth-century Muslims enjoyed modern social equality;
- that anti-Muslim prejudice was absent;
- that Article VI alone created the entire modern law of religious equality;
- that all state religious tests immediately disappeared;
- that the Constitution was influenced by Sharia;
- that Muslims influenced the wording of Article VI;
- that Islam was politically popular in 1788;
- that the ratification conventions unanimously understood Article VI in precisely the same way.

These limitations strengthen the claim by keeping it narrow.

---

# 21. The strongest defensible historical proposition

The manuscript can safely say:

> Before the Constitution had finished being ratified by all thirteen states, Americans were already arguing explicitly over whether its ban on religious tests could permit a Muslim to become president. Opponents warned that it could. Defenders did not discover a hidden Christian qualification. They defended the absence of one.

That is the “holy shit” historical reveal.

It requires no exaggeration.

---

# 22. “The Constitution accidentally forgot Muslims” hypothesis

The North Carolina evidence seriously weakens any claim that Muslim eligibility resulted from an accidental drafting oversight nobody noticed.

We cannot prove the Philadelphia framers collectively sat down and specifically resolved “Muslims must be eligible for president” unless the Convention record supplies such evidence.

But we can prove that once the Constitution reached public ratification, the implication was articulated clearly.

After that articulation, North Carolina's Federalist defenders still defended Article VI.

And the federal text remained unchanged.

## Precise formulation

> Muslim eligibility was not necessarily a specifically enumerated drafting objective at Philadelphia. But it was an expressly recognized consequence of Article VI during ratification.

This is historically safer than saying:

> The framers deliberately designed the presidency for Muslims.

---

# 23. Article VI and modern presidential eligibility

Article VI still prohibits religious tests for federal office.

The Constitution's express presidential qualifications concern citizenship status at the founding, age, and residency—not religious affiliation.

Modern constitutional law further prevents government from imposing sectarian office qualifications.

Therefore the eighteenth-century hypothetical has a straightforward modern constitutional answer:

A Muslim is constitutionally eligible to serve as president if the person satisfies the Constitution's actual qualifications and wins the required electoral process.

This is not a special Muslim exception.

It is the consequence of the general rule.

---

# 24. The paradox of Christian expectation and religious neutrality

One of Pass 26's deepest findings is that several defenders expected America to remain Christian while still defending a federal rule that did not require Christianity for office.

This is essential to the book's treatment of “Christian America.”

The historical choice was not necessarily:

Christian society **or** religious freedom.

For people such as Johnston, the position could be:

Christian society **and** no federal religious test.

That makes the constitutional development more subtle—and potentially more persuasive to a conservative Christian reader.

Potential line:

> They could expect America to remain Christian without writing Christianity into the qualifications for President.

---

# 25. Why this is stronger than Jefferson's Qur'an

Pass 25 established that Jefferson's Qur'an proves intellectual contact but not constitutional borrowing.

Pass 26 gives us something much more direct.

We do not have to infer Muslim relevance from a book someone owned.

Here, the ratifiers themselves named Muslims while interpreting the Constitution.

That is why this evidence deserves greater evidentiary weight than speculative influence claims.

> The Qur'an on Jefferson's shelf is fascinating. The Muslim in the ratification debate is constitutional evidence.

---

# 26. Recommended Chapter 10 narrative structure

## Opening — do not reveal “Muslim president” immediately

Chapter 9 should end on the six words of Article VI: no religious Test.

Chapter 10 opens in Hillsborough.

North Carolina already has a Protestant office qualification.

The delegates begin asking what happens if the federal government does not.

## Movement 1 — Abbot

First let the anxiety build.

Pagans. Deists. Mahometans. Offices.

The reader understands the stakes before we tell them the answer.

## Movement 2 — Iredell

Let Iredell refuse the premise of exclusion.

This is the philosophical turn.

## Movement 3 — Johnston

Reveal the practical concession:

A Muslim could be chosen if virtue and public confidence overcame religious difference.

Do not rush past this.

## Movement 4 — Caldwell and Spencer

Show the argument was genuine.

Christian cultural preservation versus conscience and the ineffectiveness of tests.

## Movement 5 — Lancaster

Only now deliver the famous line.

“Mahometans may take it.”

Explain immediately that this was a warning.

## Movement 6 — the absence

Lancaster looked for something preventing it.

“I see nothing against it.”

Then place beside North Carolina's own Protestant test.

The contrast does the work.

## Movement 7 — refusal to ratify

Do not falsely end with triumph.

North Carolina does not ratify.

The story remains politically unresolved.

## Movement 8 — November 1789

Return briefly to Fayetteville and eventual ratification after the national Bill of Rights process has begun.

## Closing

Potential line:

> America did not discover in the twenty-first century that a Muslim could constitutionally become president. Americans were frightened by the possibility in 1788.

Then:

> The fear was spoken aloud. The Constitution survived it.

# 32. Final finding

Pass 26 materially strengthens the book's founding argument.

We no longer need to say merely that modern lawyers can read Article VI and conclude that Muslims are eligible for federal office.

We can say something historically richer:

> In 1788, while Americans were still deciding whether to ratify the Constitution, North Carolina delegates expressly raised the possibility that removing religious tests could allow Muslims into federal office and even the presidency. Some delegates feared that result. Defenders of the Constitution responded not by inventing a Christian qualification but by defending religious liberty, voter judgment, and equal eligibility under the no-test rule.

The founding generation was not unanimous about the Muslim.

That is precisely why this evidence matters.

The Muslim was placed at the edge of the imagined political community—and the delegates had to decide whether the Constitution's principle still reached him.

For Article VI, at the federal level, the text answered before the culture did:

**no religious test.**

The Muslim president was not a loophole nobody noticed.

They noticed.
