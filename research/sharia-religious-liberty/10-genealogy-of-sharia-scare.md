# Research Pass Ten — Genealogy of “Sharia” as an American Scare Word

## Core question
How did “Sharia” move in American political discourse from a term describing Islamic religious and legal tradition into a domestic-threat narrative centered on “creeping Sharia,” stealth jihad, taqiyya, Muslim Brotherhood infiltration, demographic conquest, and alleged replacement of the Constitution?

## Bottom-line finding
The modern U.S. “Sharia threat” narrative did not arise simply from neutral legal concern about conflicts between religious law and the Constitution. It was built and amplified in identifiable stages: post-9/11 national-security anxiety; the creation of a professional advocacy and think-tank ecosystem; the 2010 Park51/“Ground Zero mosque” controversy; the Oklahoma State Question 755 campaign; coordinated state-level “foreign law” legislation based in substantial part on a model act drafted by David Yerushalmi; media and grassroots amplification by figures and organizations including Frank Gaffney, the Center for Security Policy, Brigitte Gabriel/ACT for America, Pamela Geller, Robert Spencer, and others; and, by 2025–2026, explicit institutionalization through state executive action, a congressional Sharia-Free America Caucus, and House Judiciary hearings titled “Sharia-Free America.”

Legitimate constitutional concerns do exist: no religious tribunal can override state or federal law, deprive people of constitutional rights, enforce criminal penalties, or bind unwilling parties merely by invoking religion. But U.S. law already places those limits on every religious tradition. Catholic canon tribunals, Jewish battei din, Protestant disciplinary bodies, and voluntary Muslim mediation all operate only within the boundaries of civil law and voluntary religious association. The political scare narrative becomes misleading when it treats ordinary Muslim religious practice or voluntary dispute resolution as evidence of a parallel sovereign legal system poised to replace American law.

## 1. Post-9/11: the security frame
The September 11 attacks created the essential political environment in which Islam could increasingly be discussed not only as a religion but as a national-security category. Anti-Muslim activism existed earlier, but Georgetown’s Bridge Initiative describes 9/11 as opening a new protest cycle in which think tanks, policy advocates, and self-described terrorism experts rapidly expanded their influence.

This is the first major transformation: terrorism committed by Islamist extremists becomes generalized in some advocacy rhetoric into a theory about Islam itself, and then into a theory about Sharia as the strategic operating system of a broader political movement.

Evidence grade: B for the general post-9/11 causal environment; C where specific organizational motives are inferred.

## 2. From terrorism to “civilization jihad”
By the late 2000s and 2010, a network of activists and policy organizations had built a more elaborate claim: violent jihad was only one component of a larger struggle, while Sharia represented the governing objective. In this framework, mosques, religious accommodation, halal practice, Islamic finance, Muslim civil-rights organizations, interfaith work, and political participation could all be reinterpreted as stages of “stealth jihad” or Muslim Brotherhood penetration.

The Center for Security Policy’s 2010 report *Shariah: The Threat to America: An Exercise in Competitive Analysis — Report of Team B II* is a primary source for this ideological move. It treats Sharia not primarily as a diverse religious-legal tradition but as an overarching political threat to U.S. constitutional order. The report was produced by the Center for Security Policy and associated with Frank Gaffney and a group of former national-security officials and activists.

Later CSP work went further. Reporting on its 2015 “Secure Freedom Strategy” documented proposals including using “shariah-adherent” advocacy and practice as grounds for citizenship and immigration consequences and discouraging certain interfaith programs. These proposals illustrate how the scare framework could transform religious adherence itself into evidence of political disloyalty.

Evidence grade: A for what CSP publications said; D for treating those publications as representative of Muslim intentions.

## 3. The Park51 / “Ground Zero mosque” controversy, 2010
The proposed Park51 Islamic community center in Lower Manhattan was one of the decisive mainstreaming events. Opponents such as Pamela Geller and Robert Spencer reframed a proposed Islamic community center several blocks from the World Trade Center site as the “Ground Zero mosque,” a symbol of Muslim triumphalism and an alleged advance of Islamization.

The dispute moved anti-Sharia arguments from think tanks and specialist activist circles into national television, mass protest, electoral politics, and everyday public conversation. Protest imagery from the period prominently featured “SHARIA” and “NO SHARIA IN AMERICA” signs.

The significance for this project is rhetorical: a local zoning and religious-liberty controversy became evidence, for some activists, of a civilizational advance. The same interpretive template later appeared in mosque fights, Muslim housing/community projects, and allegations of “Sharia zones.”

Evidence grade: A for the controversy and rhetoric; C for broad causal claims about downstream political effects.

## 4. Oklahoma State Question 755: “Save Our State,” 2010
Oklahoma placed State Question 755 on the November 2, 2010 ballot. The proposed constitutional amendment directed Oklahoma courts not to consider Sharia law and certain forms of international law. More than 70 percent of voters approved it.

The Oklahoma Senate’s official legislative brief described Sharia as Islamic law and summarized the ballot measure. After passage, Muneer Awad challenged the amendment in federal court.

The U.S. Court of Appeals for the Tenth Circuit affirmed a preliminary injunction in *Awad v. Ziriax*, 670 F.3d 1111 (10th Cir. 2012). The court’s opinion records that the amendment singled out Sharia and that Awad argued it stigmatized his religion and could affect matters such as probate of a will containing references to Islamic law. The court treated the explicit denominational classification as presenting a serious Establishment Clause problem.

This is a critical constitutional moment for the book: voters attempted to write a specific disfavored religion’s legal tradition into the state constitution as a prohibited category, and federal courts blocked the amendment before it took effect.

Evidence grade: A.

## 5. The model-bill strategy: American Laws for American Courts
After Oklahoma’s explicit anti-Sharia language encountered constitutional problems, the movement increasingly used religion-neutral “foreign law” language.

Pew Research Center found that from 2010 through 2012 lawmakers in at least 32 states introduced bills restricting judicial consideration of foreign or religious law. Ninety-two bills were introduced; only 21 explicitly cited Sharia or another religious law, while many of the remaining bills resembled or reproduced a model measure called “American Laws for American Courts” (ALAC).

Pew identifies attorney David Yerushalmi as the drafter of ALAC. Yerushalmi’s own professional biography states that he drafted the act and describes it as designed to insulate state courts from constitutionally offensive foreign laws, including Sharia.

Six states enacted such measures during the 2010–2012 period: Arizona, Kansas, Louisiana, Oklahoma, South Dakota, and Tennessee. Oklahoma’s explicit Sharia measure was blocked; the others used broader language.

This is one of the strongest pieces of evidence that anti-Sharia politics was not merely spontaneous local concern. There was a replicable legal template, a network of advocates, and widespread multi-state introduction.

Evidence grade: A.

## 6. ACT for America and grassroots amplification
ACT for America, founded by Brigitte Gabriel in 2007, became one of the major grassroots organizations promoting the Sharia-threat narrative. Contemporary litigation records and reporting document ACT’s active support for Oklahoma’s amendment; an appellate amicus brief quotes Gabriel as saying the amendment would help prevent a takeover of Oklahoma by Islamic extremists seeking to undo America “from the inside out.”

ACT and allied organizations helped turn policy papers into local meetings, legislative contacts, chapter activity, public demonstrations, voter guides, and statehouse advocacy.

This matters because the narrative acquired a distribution chain:

Think tanks / writers -> model legal claims -> national media -> grassroots organizations -> state legislators -> ballot measures / statutes.

Evidence grade: A for documented advocacy; B for network interpretation.

## 7. The “taqiyya” mechanism
A central feature of the scare narrative is the claim that Muslims may lie to non-Muslims whenever doing so advances Islamization. This use of “taqiyya” transforms any Muslim denial into proof of guilt:

If a Muslim says he wants to replace the Constitution, he is dangerous.
If he says he does not, the denial can be dismissed as taqiyya.

That creates an unfalsifiable theory.

Historically, taqiyya is associated especially with concealment of religious belief under danger or persecution, with particularly important development in Shi’a history. Modern anti-Muslim rhetoric often converts this narrower historical doctrine into a universal license for strategic deception by Muslims.

The Guardian’s reporting on mosque opposition in Basking Ridge, New Jersey, documented precisely this usage: local anti-mosque material invoked taqiyya to cast doubt on a Muslim civic leader’s own explanation of his intentions.

Evidence grade: A for usage of the accusation; A/B for historical meaning depending on specialist source; E for the claim that ordinary Muslim political denials are presumptively deceptive.

## 8. The “creeping Sharia” phrase and political uptake
By 2011, presidential candidate Herman Cain publicly warned of a “creeping attempt” to ease Sharia and the Muslim faith into government. The Center for American Progress’s 2011 *Fear, Inc.* report preserved this rhetoric as an example of how previously specialist claims entered mainstream electoral politics.

That report argued that a relatively small, interconnected network of funders, activists, media figures, and organizations had amplified anti-Muslim claims. CAP identified Frank Gaffney, Daniel Pipes, David Yerushalmi, Robert Spencer, Steven Emerson, and others as central figures. Because CAP is an ideologically progressive institution, its “network” interpretation should not be treated as neutral fact without corroboration. However, many of the underlying relationships, publications, funding flows, and legislative campaigns it describes are independently verifiable.

Evidence grade: A for quotations and organizational connections; B/C for CAP’s larger interpretive frame.

## 9. What American law actually permits
The anti-Sharia movement often blurred two very different questions:

1. Can a religious tribunal or foreign legal norm override the Constitution or state/federal law?
2. Can religious Americans voluntarily organize aspects of their lives according to religious rules?

The answer to the first is no.
The answer to the second is generally yes, subject to ordinary civil-law limits.

Pew’s 2013 report on religious courts found that religious tribunals are ordinary in American life. Catholic diocesan tribunals handle thousands of marriage annulment matters. Orthodox Jews use battei din for divorces, contracts, and other disputes. Protestant denominations maintain internal disciplinary processes. Muslims may ask imams to mediate marital or other disputes. Civil courts do not permit any of these systems to exercise sovereign governmental authority, and religious decisions receive civil effect only where ordinary legal rules—such as contract, arbitration, property, or family law—permit it.

This is the book’s clean constitutional distinction:

Voluntary religious law is religious liberty.
Coercive religious sovereignty is unconstitutional.

The same rule applies to Sharia, halakhah, canon law, and Christian religious discipline.

Evidence grade: A.

## 10. Public anxiety in 2010–2011
Pew polling from 2010 found a conflicted American public. Fifty-one percent sided with objections to the Park51 project, while 62 percent said Muslims should have the same rights as other religious groups to build houses of worship in their communities. Thirty-five percent said Islam was more likely than other religions to encourage violence; 42 percent disagreed.

Pew’s 2011 survey of Muslim Americans found no evidence of growing alienation or support for extremism; Muslims in the U.S. overwhelmingly rejected extremism while also reporting pressure and concern over anti-Muslim sentiment.

This contrast is important. The political threat narrative expanded during a period when polling did not show American Muslims moving collectively toward separatism or extremism.

Evidence grade: A.

## 11. The movement did not disappear — it institutionalized again in 2025–2026
The contemporary revival is now undeniable and must be part of the final book rather than treated as a historical episode that ended after 2012.

### Texas, 2025
Governor Greg Abbott repeatedly invoked Sharia in official state communications concerning the East Plano Islamic Center and the proposed EPIC City development. In September 2025, his office announced the ceremonial signing of HB 4211 under the headline “Governor Abbott Signs Law Banning Sharia Compounds In Texas.” His office said the law targeted discriminatory or unfair housing arrangements and asserted that EPIC City sought to subject residents to Sharia law.

In November 2025 Abbott called for investigations into what he described as “Sharia courts” potentially masquerading as legal courts and emphasized that religious tribunals cannot displace Texas or federal law. He also designated CAIR and the Muslim Brotherhood as foreign terrorist/transnational criminal organizations under Texas state action and alleged an objective of imposing Sharia. These state claims were immediately disputed by Muslim civil-rights organizations and remain politically and legally contested.

Evidence grade: A for what Abbott’s office said and did; disputed for the underlying allegations.

### Congressional Sharia-Free America Caucus, 2025–2026
Representatives Keith Self and Chip Roy launched the Sharia-Free America Caucus in December 2025. Self’s office reported 26 members by January 15, 2026; 33 members by February 5; 60 by March 30; and 68 members from 25 states by June 29.

Official caucus rhetoric describes Sharia as incompatible with the Constitution, American liberty, and Western civilization and alleges a growing threat inside the United States.

Evidence grade: A.

### House Judiciary hearings, 2026
The House Judiciary Subcommittee on the Constitution and Limited Government held a February 10, 2026 hearing titled:

“Sharia-Free America: Why Political Islam & Sharia Law Are Incompatible with the U.S. Constitution.”

A second hearing followed on May 13, 2026 under the same title, “Part II.” Government Publishing Office and House committee records confirm both hearings and their witnesses.

The official committee description says the hearings examined alleged Sharia-based institutions, risks to civil liberties, and possible legislative reform. Critics, including Muslim organizations and some constitutional scholars and religious-liberty advocates, argued that the framing stigmatized ordinary Islamic religious practice and conflated private religious observance with governmental sovereignty.

Evidence grade: A for hearing existence, titles, witnesses, and official framing; competing perspectives must be presented fairly.

## 12. What is legitimate concern, and what becomes fearmongering?
A serious book cannot simply call every anti-Sharia argument bigotry. There are real constitutional red lines:

- No religious body can exercise criminal jurisdiction independent of the state.
- No religious tribunal can deprive a person of due process or statutory rights.
- No religious arbitration agreement can bind someone who did not validly consent.
- A court cannot enforce a contract or arbitral award that violates controlling law or public policy.
- Religious freedom does not authorize violence, coercion, forced marriage, unlawful discrimination, or other conduct prohibited by valid law.

Those propositions are ordinary American law and should be defended.

The scare framework becomes analytically weak when it moves from those real limits to unsupported collective suspicion, such as:

- ordinary observance of Sharia proves political subversion;
- Muslim denial of political conquest is itself proof because of taqiyya;
- mosque construction demonstrates territorial Islamization;
- halal practice, Islamic finance, or voluntary mediation are steps toward replacing the Constitution;
- Muslim civic participation is infiltration rather than citizenship;
- American Muslims should bear a special burden to prove they do not secretly reject constitutional government.

Those claims require evidence. Religious identity alone is not evidence.

## 13. Narrative chronology for the book
A clean narrative arc now exists:

**2001:** 9/11 creates an enormous national-security rupture.

**2001–2009:** specialist networks increasingly develop Islam-as-political-system and Sharia-as-totalitarian-framework arguments.

**2007:** ACT for America founded and expands grassroots infrastructure.

**2010:** Park51 controversy turns Islamization/Sharia rhetoric into mass politics.

**2010:** Center for Security Policy publishes *Shariah: The Threat to America*.

**2010:** Oklahoma voters approve SQ 755, explicitly barring Sharia from state courts.

**2010–2012:** model foreign-law bills spread through dozens of state legislatures.

**2012:** Tenth Circuit in *Awad v. Ziriax* keeps Oklahoma’s explicit Sharia ban blocked.

**2011–2015:** “creeping Sharia,” Muslim Brotherhood infiltration, taqiyya, and civilization-jihad narratives circulate in electoral politics and media; watchdog/progressive reports such as *Fear, Inc.* map the network.

**2015–2017:** anti-mosque and anti-refugee mobilization grows amid ISIS terrorism, refugee debates, and Trump-era Muslim-ban politics.

**2025:** Texas executive rhetoric and legislation revive explicit “Sharia” framing around Muslim institutions and developments.

**December 2025:** Sharia-Free America Caucus launched in Congress.

**February and May 2026:** House Judiciary holds “Sharia-Free America” hearings.

**June 2026:** caucus reports 68 members from 25 states.

This is no longer a fringe-only vocabulary. It has reached state executive offices and formal congressional institutions.

## 14. Most important analytical insight
The scare narrative depends on collapsing four distinct things into one word:

1. Sharia as personal religious ethics and worship.
2. Sharia as classical jurisprudence developed by Muslim scholars.
3. Voluntary religious mediation/arbitration in a secular legal system.
4. A sovereign theocratic state using religious law coercively.

Those are not the same thing.

Political rhetoric becomes powerful by allowing the listener to hear meaning #4 every time a Muslim means #1.

That should become a central explanatory device in the book.

## Final research conclusion
The modern American Sharia scare was not invented out of nothing: Islamist terrorism, theocratic states, coercive interpretations of Islamic law, and real constitutional conflicts provide material for legitimate debate. But the U.S. political movement that emerged after 9/11 constructed a much broader claim—that Sharia itself, ordinary Muslim observance, Muslim organizations, and Muslim civic growth constituted components of a covert domestic project to replace American constitutional order.

The evidence supports tracing that claim through named organizations, reports, model legislation, grassroots campaigns, media episodes, state ballot measures, and, by 2025–2026, official state and congressional institutions. What the evidence does not establish is the central conspiracy claim itself.

The constitutional answer remains remarkably straightforward: no religious law can override the Constitution, but every religion receives the protection of the Constitution. The government may prohibit coercion and unconstitutional conduct. It may not treat a Muslim’s religious identity as evidence that he secretly seeks to overthrow the legal order.
