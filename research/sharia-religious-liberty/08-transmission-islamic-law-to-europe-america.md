# Research Pass 08 — Transmission: Did Islamic Legal or Political Ideas Reach Europe and the American Founders?

## Research question

The prior pass established meaningful parallels between classical Islamic jurisprudence and constitutional ideas: consultation, rule-bound government, juristic independence, contracts, evidence, public welfare, property, justice, and restraints on rulers. This pass asks a much harder question: **can any of those ideas be documented as having traveled from Islamic civilization into European legal or political thought and then into the intellectual world of the American founders?**

The governing rule remains strict: **parallel is not proof of borrowing.** A plausible pathway is not the same thing as a documented chain of transmission.

## Executive finding

There is indisputable evidence that intellectual material moved from the Islamic world into medieval Latin Europe through translation centers and cross-cultural contact. Arabic-language philosophy, science, medicine, mathematics, and commentaries on Greek thought moved through places such as Toledo, Salerno, Sicily, and Antioch into European universities and scholarly traditions.

There is also indisputable evidence that Norman Sicily was a real contact zone in which Christian rulers governed large Muslim populations, used Arabic-speaking administrators and soldiers, issued documents in Arabic as well as Latin and Greek, and dealt with Islamic law in practice.

What is **not** yet established at an A-grade level is a direct chain showing that a specific American constitutional provision—Article VI, the First Amendment, separation of powers, judicial review, bicameralism, or similar—was adopted from Sharia or an Islamic legal institution.

The strongest disputed legal-transmission theory concerns English common law. John A. Makdisi argues that specific common-law institutions may have Islamic antecedents transmitted through Norman Sicily. His most famous comparisons involve the English action of debt and Islamic `aqd`, novel disseisin and `istihqaq`, and the jury and the Maliki `lafif`. He argues that the combination of structural similarities and historical opportunity makes direct borrowing plausible. This is serious scholarship, not fringe internet speculation, but it remains debated rather than settled consensus.

A related line of scholarship argues that the English trust may have been influenced by the Islamic `waqf`, an endowed property institution administered for designated beneficiaries or charitable purposes. The structural similarities are substantial and the Crusader/Franciscan transmission theory is historically possible. Other legal historians argue that similar social problems can produce similar institutions without direct borrowing. Therefore the trust–waqf connection is best graded as **C: plausible transmission / contested historical influence**, not A.

## 1. The Arabic-to-Latin translation movement is not speculative

The clearest transmission channel is intellectual history rather than constitutional law.

During the Abbasid period, Muslim scholars translated and developed Greek, Persian, and Indian knowledge. Centuries later, an Arabic-to-Latin translation movement moved many of those works—and Islamic commentaries and original works—into Latin Europe.

Major centers included Toledo, Salerno, Sicily, and Antioch. Works by or associated with al-Kindi, al-Farabi, Avicenna, al-Ghazali, Averroes, and others became known in Latin Europe. These were not merely copies of Greek texts preserved unchanged. European readers encountered Greek philosophy through layers of Islamic interpretation, commentary, criticism, and development.

**Evidence grade: A — direct, documented transmission.**

This proves that medieval Europe was not intellectually sealed off from Islamic civilization. It does **not**, by itself, prove that Islamic constitutional thought entered the U.S. Constitution.

## 2. Sicily was a genuine legal and administrative contact zone

Muslim rule in Sicily preceded the Norman conquest beginning in the eleventh century. Under Norman Christian rulers, large Arabic-speaking Muslim populations remained on the island for generations.

Modern Oxford scholarship documents Norman Sicily as multilingual and legally plural. Administrative and legal materials survive in Arabic, Greek, Judaeo-Arabic, and Latin. Muslims attempted to continue living under Islamic law even after Christian conquest. Norman rulers incorporated Muslim soldiers, farmers, scholars, and bureaucrats into the kingdom and depended on them in significant parts of state administration and military life.

Some scholarship argues that Norman political culture itself incorporated Byzantine and Islamic elements, including legal treatment of Muslim subjects.

This matters because Sicily supplies what any serious transmission theory needs: **proximity, contact, bilingual personnel, shared institutions, and actual legal interaction.**

But historical contact alone does not prove that a particular English legal institution was borrowed from Islamic law.

**Evidence grade: A for contact; C for specific institutional borrowing unless separately demonstrated.**

## 3. The common-law hypothesis

John A. Makdisi's 1999 North Carolina Law Review article, *The Islamic Origins of the Common Law*, argues that three early English common-law institutions resemble Islamic legal institutions so closely, and appeared in a period with sufficient opportunity for Norman-Sicilian transfer, that an Islamic source should be seriously considered.

His proposed parallels include:

- English action of debt ↔ Islamic `aqd`;
- English assize of novel disseisin ↔ Islamic `istihqaq`;
- English jury ↔ Maliki `lafif`.

Makdisi's argument is significant because he does not merely point to philosophical similarity. He attempts to establish functional and structural correspondence plus a historical pathway through Norman Sicily.

Later scholarship has both defended and criticized these claims. A 2022 study testing Makdisi's novel-disseisin theory against later common-law doctrine concluded that the possessory structure supports his proposed Islamic comparison. Other scholarship continues to treat the broader Islamic-common-law relationship as an unresolved historical debate.

Most important for this book: **this is a legitimate scholarly controversy, not settled fact.**

The safe formulation is:

> Some legal historians have argued that specific English common-law institutions may have Islamic antecedents transmitted through Norman Sicily. The hypothesis is serious and supported by structural comparisons and historical opportunity, but direct documentary proof remains incomplete and historians continue to disagree.

**Evidence grade: C.**

## 4. Waqf and the English trust

Another long-running argument concerns the Islamic `waqf` and the English trust or medieval `use`.

A waqf is an endowed property arrangement in which assets are dedicated for religious, charitable, family, or other designated purposes and administered for beneficiaries or an enduring objective.

Scholars have noted striking similarities between waqf structures and the English trust: separation of control and benefit, dedicated property, fiduciary administration, and the ability to preserve property for designated beneficiaries or purposes.

One theory proposes transmission through Franciscan friars returning from the eastern Mediterranean and Crusader world. A Tulane Law Review analysis described the historical proximity and structural similarities as strong enough that waqf influence should be taken seriously, while also noting competing Roman, Germanic, and indigenous explanations.

This is exactly the kind of claim that should remain carefully graded.

**Evidence grade: C — historically plausible and structurally strong, but not proven by a surviving transfer document.**

## 5. The Inns of Court / madrasa analogy

George Makdisi argued that the English Inns of Court had peculiar institutional similarities to Islamic legal education and professional guild structures. He explicitly acknowledged the central weakness: the surviving sources provide **no direct evidence** connecting the two systems.

That admission is important.

The similarities may be historically meaningful, but they cannot be published as proof of direct borrowing.

**Evidence grade: D/C border — significant parallel with a possible pathway, but no direct documentary chain.**

## 6. Contact did not flow in only one direction

The historical record also cautions against romantic narratives of a one-way Islamic transmission into Europe.

Norman Sicily shows overlapping Roman, Byzantine, Christian, and Islamic legal cultures. Oxford research on twelfth-century Sicilian litigation finds strong evidence of the reception of Roman `ius commune` jurisprudence as well. The region was a hybrid contact zone.

This is essential to the book's credibility.

Legal traditions interacted. They did not move as sealed packages labeled “Islamic,” “Christian,” or “Roman.”

A feature appearing in Norman Sicily might reflect Islamic law, Roman law, Byzantine practice, indigenous custom, royal innovation, or some combination.

## 7. Locke is an important founder-era bridge—but not a Sharia transmission bridge

John Locke matters enormously because Jefferson directly studied and summarized his arguments on toleration.

Locke's *Letter Concerning Toleration* explicitly states that neither pagan, Muslim (“Mahometan”), nor Jew should be excluded from the civil rights of the commonwealth merely because of religion.

Jefferson copied and summarized this proposition in his 1776 notes on Locke and then pushed beyond some of Locke's limitations.

This is a genuine, documented intellectual chain:

**Locke → Jefferson → Jefferson's religious-liberty thought.**

But it is not evidence of:

**Sharia → Locke → Jefferson.**

Locke discussed Muslims as subjects of toleration and as examples in his political argument. That does not establish that Islamic jurisprudence supplied Locke's theory of toleration.

**Evidence grade: A for Locke-to-Jefferson transmission; no established grade for Sharia-to-Locke transmission.**

## 8. Jefferson's Qur'an is direct contact, but still not proof of constitutional borrowing

The Library of Congress states that Jefferson appears to have purchased George Sale's English translation of the Qur'an in 1765 while studying law at William & Mary, and that legal study may have encouraged him to examine it as an example of Arabic law.

This is strong evidence that Jefferson encountered the Qur'an while training as a lawyer.

It is also significant that he later cataloged the work under religion and that the surviving volumes contain no substantive annotations showing which passages influenced him.

Therefore we can safely say:

> Jefferson encountered the Qur'an during his legal education and owned it for decades.

We cannot safely say:

> Jefferson read Qur'anic concepts of consultation or justice and then incorporated them into American constitutional law.

No surviving documentary chain currently proves that.

**Evidence grade: A for exposure; E for any specific claim of constitutional borrowing absent new evidence.**

## 9. Montesquieu, Grotius, and the Enlightenment require further narrow research

European Enlightenment thinkers were deeply aware of the Ottoman Empire, Persia, Islam, and comparative political systems. Montesquieu in particular used Islamic and Persian settings as vehicles for political comparison and criticism of European society.

However, general awareness of Muslim societies is not enough.

For the book to claim a transmission chain such as:

**Islamic constitutional concept → Montesquieu → Madison / Constitution**

we would need evidence that Montesquieu adopted the relevant principle from Islamic jurisprudence and that the founders adopted that principle through him.

That evidence has not yet been established.

Likewise, Grotius's engagement with non-European legal and religious traditions does not by itself establish that his natural-law theory derived from Islamic jurisprudence.

These should remain dedicated research subprojects rather than narrative claims.

## 10. The transmission claim we can already make confidently

We can now say three things with very different levels of certainty.

### A-grade statement

**Islamic civilization materially influenced medieval European intellectual life.** Arabic-to-Latin translation transmitted philosophy, science, medicine, mathematics, and Islamic commentaries into Europe, where they became part of university and scholarly culture.

### B/C-grade statement

**Muslim and Christian legal cultures interacted directly in places such as Norman Sicily.** That interaction created real opportunities for legal borrowing.

### C-grade statement

**Serious legal historians have proposed Islamic influences on particular English common-law institutions, including the trust, debt actions, possessory remedies, jury structures, and legal education. Some of these arguments are substantial, but direct documentary proof remains incomplete and the field is contested.**

### Unsupported statement we should reject

**“The U.S. Constitution was based on Sharia.”**

Current evidence does not establish this.

## 11. The better thesis

The failure to prove direct constitutional borrowing does not weaken the book. It actually improves it.

The more defensible historical story is:

> The Western legal tradition was never culturally hermetic. Medieval Europe received enormous bodies of knowledge through Arabic and Islamic civilization. Christian and Muslim legal systems interacted in Mediterranean contact zones. Some scholars see specific Islamic fingerprints in later common-law institutions, though the exact chains remain contested. By the eighteenth century, Jefferson could study both Locke's Muslim-inclusive theory of civil liberty and an English Qur'an while training as a lawyer. None of that proves that Philadelphia copied Sharia. It proves something more historically interesting: the intellectual world that produced America was far less isolated from Islam than modern political mythology suggests.

## 12. Narrative implications

Potential line:

**America did not emerge from a sealed Christian-European laboratory. The intellectual world that eventually produced it had already spent centuries exchanging ideas across Arabic, Muslim, Jewish, Greek, Roman, and Christian civilizations.**

Potential line:

**The question is not whether James Madison opened a book of Sharia in Philadelphia. We have no evidence that he did. The question is whether the West that educated Madison was as intellectually separate from Islam as modern mythology imagines. It wasn't.**

Potential line:

**Islamic influence on Europe is a historical fact. Islamic authorship of the U.S. Constitution is not. Between those two propositions lies the real story.**

Potential line:

**The honest historian must resist two temptations at once: pretending Islam contributed nothing to Western legal civilization, and pretending every resemblance proves America copied Sharia.**

## 13. Evidence ledger

### A — direct documented transmission
- Arabic-to-Latin translation movement brought Islamic and Arabic-language scholarship into Latin Europe.
- Norman Sicily contained Muslim populations, Arabic administration, multilingual legal documents, and active interaction between Christian government and Islamic law.
- Jefferson owned George Sale's Qur'an from roughly 1765 while studying law.
- Jefferson directly studied Locke's Muslim-inclusive toleration argument in 1776.

### B — strong historical connection
- Norman Sicily created real institutional pathways through which Islamic practices could potentially move into Christian European administration and law.
- Islamic intellectual materials became embedded in European university traditions through translated works and commentary.

### C — plausible but contested transmission
- Islamic influence on selected English common-law institutions under John Makdisi's theory.
- Waqf influence on the English trust/use.
- Islamic legal guild or educational influence on Inns of Court.

### D — meaningful parallel, causation unknown
- Rule-of-law traditions, consultation, juristic independence, and public-welfare reasoning appearing in both Islamic and later Western constitutional/legal traditions without a demonstrated transfer chain.

### E — do not publish as fact
- “The Constitution was copied from Sharia.”
- “Jefferson used Qur'an 42:38 to design Congress.”
- “Madison borrowed separation of powers from Islamic government.”
- “The First Amendment came from Islamic law.”
- Any specific founder-level Islamic borrowing absent a primary-source chain.

## Bottom line

Pass Eight does **not** prove that American constitutionalism derives from Sharia.

It does establish that the civilization producing American constitutionalism was part of a much older web of cross-cultural intellectual exchange in which Islamic civilization played a major role. It also establishes several serious but disputed theories of Islamic influence on the English common-law tradition inherited by the United States.

That is enough to dismantle the simplistic claim that Islam and “Western law” developed in completely separate universes.

The next research pass should build the **Bible–Qur'an mirror**, comparing difficult and humane passages across both scriptures with equal interpretive standards.
