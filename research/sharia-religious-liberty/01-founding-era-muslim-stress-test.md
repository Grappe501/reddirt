# Research Pass 01 — Muslims as a Founding-Era Stress Test for Religious Liberty

## Core finding
Muslims were not merely accidental modern beneficiaries of broad constitutional language. Founding-era Americans explicitly discussed “Mahometans” when debating civil rights, religious tests, eligibility for office, and the meaning of universal liberty of conscience.

## Jefferson and Locke, 1776
Thomas Jefferson’s notes on Locke include the proposition that neither pagan, Muslim (“Mahamedan”), nor Jew should be excluded from civil rights because of religion. Jefferson’s notes also distinguish civil interests from religious belief and reject coercive authority over faith.

Primary source: Founders Online, “VI. Notes on Locke and Shaftesbury, 11 October–9 December 1776.”
https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Jefferson/01-01-02-0222-0007

## Virginia religious freedom
Jefferson’s Bill for Establishing Religious Freedom argued that civil rights do not depend on religious opinions and that religious belief should neither diminish nor enlarge civil capacities.

Primary source: Founders Online, “A Bill for Establishing Religious Freedom, 18 June 1779.”
https://founders.archives.gov/documents/Jefferson/01-02-02-0132-0004-0082

Jefferson later recalled that an attempt to insert an explicit reference to Jesus Christ was rejected, and he interpreted that rejection as proof that the statute was meant to protect the Jew, Gentile, Christian, Muslim, Hindu, and infidel alike. This retrospective claim should be carefully attributed to Jefferson; the legislative journals do not independently preserve the proposed amendment in the form he later described.

## Article VI and the Muslim officeholder debate
The U.S. Constitution bars religious tests for federal office. During ratification, opponents expressly worried that this could permit non-Christians, including Muslims, to hold public office. In the North Carolina ratification convention, delegates raised the prospect that a “Mahometan” could someday become president or otherwise hold office.

This is critical evidence because the constitutional consequence was understood before the new government fully began. The absence of a Christian test was not merely a twenty-first-century judicial reinterpretation.

Useful source collection: University of Chicago, Founders’ Constitution, Article VI religious-test debates.
https://press-pubs.uchicago.edu/founders/documents/a6_3s26.html

## Broader founding-era evidence
Library of Congress research collects examples of founding-era Americans using Muslims, Jews, Hindus, deists, and other minorities as examples when explaining universal religious liberty. The evidence includes Richard Henry Lee, George Washington, state-level debate, and popular petitions.

Library of Congress: “Religion and the Founding of the American Republic / Tolerance and Religious Liberty.”
https://www.loc.gov/loc/lcib/0205/tolerance.html

## Interpretation
The defensible claim is not that every founder welcomed Islam or that eighteenth-century America was free of anti-Muslim prejudice. It was not. The stronger point is that Americans were debating the same boundary question that remains with us today: does liberty of conscience protect only familiar religions, or does it protect people whose religion the majority distrusts?

## Story value
Potential narrative line:

“America did not discover in the twenty-first century that Muslims were protected by the Constitution. Americans argued about whether Muslims could hold office before the Constitution had finished being ratified.”

The Muslim therefore becomes a recurring constitutional stress test. If a system protects only the majority’s faith, it is not religious neutrality; it is religious preference.

## Evidence grade
A/B for the existence of explicit founding-era Muslim references in religious-liberty and officeholding debates.

Do not turn this into the broader claim that the Constitution was drafted because of Islam or derived from Sharia. That requires separate evidence.
