# Research Pass 18 — Islamic Reform and Constitutionalism

## Purpose

This pass asks a question left open by the hard-case analysis in Pass 17: when Muslim thinkers themselves recognize tensions between inherited Islamic jurisprudence and modern constitutional democracy, what solutions have they proposed?

The answer is not one thing. Contemporary Muslim thought contains several distinct reform strategies, from constitutional democracy grounded in Islamic moral values, to maqasid-based reinterpretation, to pluralist democracy, to secular-state arguments that insist Sharia loses its religious legitimacy when enforced coercively by the state.

The central conclusion is therefore not that “Islam has solved democracy.” It is that there is a substantial, serious, internally Muslim literature arguing over how Islam should relate to democracy, human rights, citizenship, freedom of religion, women’s equality, and the modern state.

## Evidence discipline

Use the project’s standing grades:

- **A — Direct documented position**: the thinker or institution explicitly argues the proposition.
- **B — Strong documented connection**: the proposition is strongly supported by primary text or authoritative scholarly treatment.
- **C — Plausible interpretive development**: serious but not universally accepted reform reasoning.
- **D — Significant parallel**: useful comparison, no claim of consensus.
- **E — Unsupported or misleading generalization**: do not publish as a general statement about Islam or Muslims.

Do not convert any reform thinker into “what Islam teaches.” These are contested arguments within Islam.

## 1. Abdullahi Ahmed An-Na'im — Sharia needs a secular state to remain genuinely religious

### Core argument

An-Na'im’s *Islam and the Secular State* offers one of the clearest modern Muslim arguments for separating coercive state authority from religious normativity.

His position is radical in a useful way: Sharia should continue to guide Muslim moral and religious life, but the state should not enforce Sharia simply because it is religious law.

An-Na'im argues that coercive state enforcement undermines the religious character of Sharia because genuine religious observance requires voluntary moral commitment. He also argues that state laws should be justified by “civic reasons” accessible to citizens regardless of religion.

### Evidence grade

**A — Direct documented position.**

### Why it matters

This directly answers a central fear in the American debate:

A Muslim scholar can believe Sharia is divinely important while simultaneously arguing that the state must not impose it as religious law.

### Book-ready line

**“For An-Na'im, Sharia does not become more Islamic when government coerces it. It becomes less religious because obedience ceases to be voluntary.”**

### Constitutional relevance

This is exceptionally compatible with the American distinction developed throughout this project:

- religious belief is protected;
- religiously motivated political advocacy is protected;
- the state must justify coercive law in civil terms applicable to citizens generally;
- no sacred law automatically becomes civil law merely because believers regard it as divine.

### Important caveat

Do not say An-Na'im represents mainstream global Muslim opinion. His secular-state argument is influential but contested.

## 2. Khaled Abou El Fadl — constitutional democracy as an Islamic moral achievement

### Core argument

Khaled Abou El Fadl argues that constitutional democracy, especially one protecting basic individual rights, can be the form of government most capable of realizing central Islamic moral values.

His reasoning starts from the danger of human beings claiming to speak with God’s unquestionable authority. If no human interpreter is infallible, then concentrated political authority claiming divine certainty is dangerous.

Democracy, rights, consultation, accountability, and limits on power therefore become mechanisms for restraining human arrogance in God’s name.

### Evidence grade

**A — Direct documented position.**

### Key conceptual move

The problem is not divine sovereignty in the abstract. The problem is the human being who says:

“I know exactly what God commands, therefore you must obey me.”

Abou El Fadl’s response is constitutional restraint.

### Book-ready line

**“The danger is not that God is sovereign. The danger is the ruler who declares himself God’s authorized translator and then removes every human institution capable of telling him he is wrong.”**

### Why this matters to Pass 17

Pass 17 identified “Sharia supremacy” as one of the strongest legitimate anti-Sharia concerns. Abou El Fadl answers that concern from inside Islamic thought by emphasizing uncertainty, interpretive humility, pluralism, and constitutional rights.

### Caveat

Abou El Fadl does not erase tensions between classical jurisprudence and modern rights. His work is a reformist jurisprudential argument about how Islamic values should be interpreted under modern constitutional conditions.

## 3. Rached Ghannouchi — from Islamic democracy toward “Muslim democracy”

### Core argument

Rached Ghannouchi is especially important because his ideas moved from theory into constitution-making in Tunisia.

Oxford’s 2023 collection *On Muslim Democracy* documents the evolution of Ghannouchi and Ennahda from a more traditional “Islamic democracy” framework toward what they called “Muslim democracy.”

Ennahda helped participate in drafting Tunisia’s 2014 Constitution, a pluralist democratic constitution that did **not** give Sharia a special constitutional status in the legal system.

Ghannouchi defended this compromise as appropriate to a plural society whose constitution must belong to the whole country, not merely to the electoral majority.

### Evidence grade

**A — Direct documented position and political practice.**

### Major themes

Ghannouchi argues for:

- political pluralism;
- popular democracy;
- equal citizenship;
- political participation by women;
- eligibility of non-Muslims for public authority;
- religious freedom;
- consultation;
- constitutional rules broad enough for citizens of differing ideologies and religions.

### Particularly important finding

Oxford’s treatment states that Ennahda helped author a democratic constitutional order with **no special status for Islamic Sharia in the legal system**, followed by the movement’s 2016 declaration that it had left “political Islam” and embraced “Muslim democracy.”

### Book-ready line

**“One of the most important Islamist movements in the Arab world eventually helped write a constitution that did not make Sharia supreme law.”**

### Another

**“Ghannouchi’s constitutional lesson was not that the majority gets to religiously own the state. It was that a constitution must be livable by people who lost the election too.”**

### Caveat

Tunisia’s later democratic breakdown under President Kais Saied demonstrates that constitutional design alone does not guarantee democratic consolidation. Do not portray Tunisia as a completed success story.

## 4. Abdolkarim Soroush — pluralism, fallible religious knowledge, and human rights

### Core argument

Iranian philosopher Abdolkarim Soroush distinguishes religion itself from human religious knowledge.

God may be perfect; human interpretation is not.

Religious understanding is historical, developing, and plural. This opens intellectual space for democracy because no clerical or political faction can credibly claim permanent monopoly on God’s meaning.

Soroush has explicitly identified pluralism and human rights as pillars of democracy.

### Evidence grade

**A — Direct documented philosophical position.**

### Human-rights argument

Soroush argues that human rights cannot simply depend on the theological doctrine of whichever religion controls political power. The freedom to choose religion logically precedes meaningful religious belief.

### Book-ready line

**“If religious interpretation is human, then no government gets to turn one human interpretation into God’s final political decree.”**

### Significance

Soroush provides one of the strongest philosophical answers to the “who decides what Sharia means?” problem.

His answer is effectively:

No interpreter gets infallibility.

That means disagreement must remain politically possible.

## 5. Mohammad Hashim Kamali — Islamic constitutionalism without simply copying the modern nation-state backward

### Core argument

Kamali’s work stresses that modern writers often project contemporary nation-state concepts backward onto early Islam. He examines whether an Islamic polity must be theocratic, whether Islam is compatible with a qualified democracy, whether separation of powers can exist, and how religious freedom and pluralism fit into Islamic constitutional thought.

### Evidence grade

**A/B — Direct scholarly argument; specific institutional conclusions require careful reading in context.**

### Importance

Kamali is useful precisely because he is not simply arguing for Western secularism. He works within Islamic jurisprudential categories and asks how they can support constitutionalism, consultation, accountability, rights, and pluralism.

### Book-ready line

**“Modern Muslim constitutionalism is not one project. Some thinkers seek a secular state; others seek an Islamic constitutional order restrained by rights, consultation, and pluralism.”**

This distinction must remain visible throughout the book.

## 6. Jasser Auda — maqasid as a pathway from rules toward justice, freedom, rights, and welfare

### Core argument

Jasser Auda’s maqasid approach centers the higher purposes of Islamic law rather than treating every inherited ruling as an eternally frozen endpoint.

His systems approach argues that Islamic legal reasoning should be evaluated according to purposes including justice, freedom, rights, common good, and tolerance.

### Evidence grade

**A — Direct documented framework.**

### Why it matters

This is one of the most important reform mechanisms in contemporary Islamic jurisprudence.

Instead of asking only:

“What did a jurist in the tenth century rule?”

A maqasid-oriented reformer may ask:

“What was the underlying purpose of the rule, and how should that purpose be realized under radically different social conditions?”

### Book-ready line

**“The reform argument is not always ‘ignore the old law.’ Sometimes it is: recover the purpose the old law was trying to serve.”**

### Caveat

Traditionalists contest how far maqasid can legitimately override or reinterpret specific textual rules. Do not present maqasid as a universally accepted escape hatch.

## 7. Nader Hashemi — democracy may require Muslim societies to develop their own form of secularism

### Core argument

Hashemi argues that liberal democracy requires some form of secularism, but that secularism cannot simply be imported as an anti-religious ideology into societies where religion remains a central marker of identity.

Instead, democratic development may require internal religious debate and reinterpretation that produces an indigenous separation or differentiation between religious authority and state coercion.

### Evidence grade

**A — Direct documented political-theory argument.**

### Crucial distinction

Hashemi does not argue that democracy requires religion to disappear from public life.

He argues that democratic politics must find a way to prevent religious truth claims from becoming unchecked coercive political authority.

### Book-ready line

**“Secular government does not have to mean a godless society. It can mean a society where nobody gets coercive political authority merely by claiming to speak for God.”**

This is extremely useful for American Christian readers because it mirrors the book’s treatment of Christianity and Islam symmetrically.

## 8. Democracy does not require Muslims to become secular people

One of the strongest recurring themes across these thinkers is that there are at least two different meanings of secularism:

1. **philosophical secularism** — religion should disappear from public life or be regarded as false/private;
2. **institutional or political secularism** — coercive state authority must not be monopolized by one religious interpretation.

Many Muslim reformers reject the first while accepting some version of the second.

### Evidence grade

**B — Strong cross-source synthesis.**

### Book-ready line

**“The constitutional question is not whether Muslims must stop believing God governs the universe. Millions of American Christians believe that too. The question is whether any human institution gets unchecked political power by claiming exclusive authority to interpret God.”**

## 9. Popular sovereignty versus divine sovereignty

This is one of the hardest philosophical questions.

A democratic constitution says the people govern themselves.

Many religious traditions say ultimate sovereignty belongs to God.

Muslim constitutionalists have developed several responses:

- popular sovereignty can operate as a delegated or procedural authority beneath ultimate divine sovereignty;
- human legislation is legitimate where revelation leaves room for judgment;
- democracy can institutionalize shura and accountability;
- constitutional rights can prevent majorities from violating human dignity;
- because human interpretation is fallible, no cleric or ruler may claim absolute divine political authority.

### Evidence grade

**B/C — Serious family of arguments; no consensus.**

### Key comparison

This tension is not uniquely Islamic.

American Christianity has long contained people who say:

“God is sovereign.”

Yet American constitutional democracy operates through popular government.

Therefore “God is sovereign” by itself does not establish theocracy.

The constitutional question is what institutional consequences follow from that theology.

## 10. Religious freedom and apostasy

Pass 17 established apostasy as one of the strongest genuine conflicts between classical fiqh and modern freedom of conscience.

Modern Muslim reformers have taken several approaches:

- distinguish private religious conversion from political treason or wartime betrayal;
- argue that the Qur’an condemns apostasy spiritually without prescribing an earthly death penalty merely for changing belief;
- interpret classical penalties within their historical political context;
- argue that freedom of belief is required for genuine faith;
- reject state punishment for conversion as inconsistent with religious freedom.

### Evidence grade

**B — Strong documented reform current; classical consensus historically differed.**

### Book-ready line

**“The most credible Muslim reformers do not solve apostasy by pretending classical law never punished it. They solve it by arguing that the state should no longer do so.”**

This is a much more defensible formulation than claiming Islam has always recognized modern freedom to leave religion.

## 11. Women and equal citizenship

Contemporary Muslim reform arguments include:

- reading Qur’anic ethical equality as controlling over historically contingent patriarchal institutions;
- distinguishing immutable religious principles from medieval social arrangements;
- using maqasid to argue for dignity, welfare, justice, and equal participation;
- accepting women as political leaders;
- reconsidering classical family and guardianship structures;
- arguing that equal citizenship should not depend on sex or religion.

Ghannouchi explicitly argued for women’s political leadership and participation. Auda’s maqasid framework explicitly centers justice, freedom, rights, and human welfare.

### Evidence grade

**B — Strong contemporary reform tradition; no universal consensus.**

### Caveat

Do not imply that reform scholarship has erased discrimination in Muslim-majority legal systems. It has not.

---

## 12. Non-Muslims and equal citizenship

Classical dhimmi systems provided protected but unequal status.

Modern Muslim constitutionalists increasingly argue instead for citizenship independent of religion.

Ghannouchi’s work explicitly addresses equal citizenship and the permissibility of non-Muslims serving in positions of political authority.

An-Na'im grounds citizenship in secular constitutional equality rather than religious classification.

Soroush’s pluralism undermines monopoly claims by a single religious interpretation.

### Evidence grade

**A/B — Direct for individual thinkers; broader trend strongly documented but not universal.**

### Book-ready line

**“The modern reform move is from protected minority to equal citizen.”**

That is an important historical transition and should not be confused with the claim that classical Islamic law always recognized modern equality.

## 13. The modern state may itself be part of the problem

A subtle point appears repeatedly in serious scholarship: some practices labeled “Sharia rule” today are products not simply of medieval Islamic jurisprudence but of the modern centralized state.

Historically, jurists, courts, rulers, local communities, schools of law, custom, and multiple overlapping institutions shared legal authority.

The modern state centralizes legislation, policing, punishment, surveillance, and bureaucracy.

When that machinery claims a single official interpretation of Sharia, it can produce a form of religious coercion different in scale from much premodern legal pluralism.

### Evidence grade

**B/C — Important scholarly thesis requiring additional specialist sourcing in final draft.**

### Book-ready line

**“Sometimes the frightening part of ‘Islamic law’ is not only the medieval rule. It is the modern police state enforcing one interpretation with twentieth-century bureaucratic power.”**

This deserves a dedicated source pass before publication.

## 14. Reform is real — and resistance to reform is real

The book must avoid a new form of cherry-picking.

It would be misleading to line up An-Na'im, Abou El Fadl, Soroush, Ghannouchi, Auda, Kamali, and Hashemi and then announce:

“Islam supports liberal democracy.”

That would flatten internal disagreement just as badly as anti-Sharia activists flatten Islam into hudud and jihad.

Traditionalist and Islamist scholars continue to disagree over:

- whether Sharia should constitutionally bind legislation;
- apostasy;
- blasphemy;
- gender roles;
- family law;
- sexual morality;
- limits of popular sovereignty;
- eligibility of non-Muslims for specific offices;
- scope of secular political authority;
- how far maqasid reasoning may revise inherited fiqh.

### Core methodological rule

**Never replace the caricature “all Muslims want theocracy” with the caricature “all Muslims are liberal constitutionalists.”**

Both are false.

## 15. The emerging model: faith without theocracy

Across the reform literature, several recurring principles appear:

1. Faith remains morally serious.
2. Religious values may inspire political participation.
3. No human interpreter is infallible.
4. Political authority must be accountable.
5. Citizens require protected rights.
6. Religious pluralism must be politically possible.
7. Citizenship should increasingly be understood independently of creed.
8. Constitutional order should restrain both rulers and majorities.
9. Sharia may guide believers without automatically becoming coercive state law.
10. Democratic participation does not require believers to abandon theological convictions.

### Evidence grade

**B — Cross-source synthesis, not a universal Islamic doctrine.**

### Book-ready formulation

**“The emerging reformist answer is not Islam without faith. It is faith without monopoly on the state.”**

## 16. How this changes the anti-Sharia chapter

Pass 17 asked whether legitimate dangers exist in coercive interpretations of Sharia.

The answer was yes.

Pass 18 adds the necessary next sentence:

**Muslims themselves have spent generations arguing about those dangers and developing constitutional responses to them.**

That matters because anti-Sharia rhetoric often treats Islam as a frozen system incapable of internal legal change.

But Islamic intellectual history contains exactly what legal traditions normally contain:

- conservatives;
- reformers;
- textualists;
- contextualists;
- traditionalists;
- constitutionalists;
- liberals;
- revivalists;
- secular Muslims;
- Islamist democrats;
- critics of democracy;
- advocates of pluralism;
- opponents of pluralism.

In other words:

**a legal civilization arguing with itself.**

## 17. Strongest final conclusion from Pass 18

The most defensible conclusion is not:

> Islam and liberal democracy are automatically compatible.

Nor is it:

> Islam and liberal democracy are inherently incompatible.

The evidence supports something more historically serious:

> **Muslim thinkers have developed multiple intellectually substantial ways of reconciling religious commitment with constitutional democracy, human rights, plural citizenship, and limits on state enforcement of Sharia. Those arguments are contested, but they are real, longstanding, and increasingly important to modern Muslim political thought.**

### Book-ready line

**“Islam does not arrive in the modern world with one political answer. Muslims are still arguing over the answer.”**

And:

**“The existence of the argument is itself evidence against the claim that Sharia is a single political program waiting to be imposed.”**

## 18. The American connection

This pass strengthens the core American thesis dramatically.

American constitutionalism does not require a Muslim to settle every debate inside Islam before receiving religious liberty.

A Muslim can be:

- conservative;
- reformist;
- Sunni;
- Shi'a;
- secular;
- traditionalist;
- feminist;
- politically progressive;
- politically conservative;
- uncertain;
- observant;
- non-observant.

The Constitution protects the person while constraining coercive civil power.

That is precisely why constitutional religious liberty works.

### Core line

**“America does not need Islam to become liberal before Muslims become citizens. America needs the Constitution to remain liberal enough to protect Muslims while preventing any religion from becoming coercive sovereign law.”**

## Final thesis from Pass 18

**The deepest constitutional divide is not Islam versus America. It is coercive religious sovereignty versus protected human conscience. Muslim thinkers themselves have recognized that divide and have proposed sharply different ways to resolve it—from constitutional democracy grounded in Islamic morality, to maqasid reform, to pluralist citizenship, to a secular state that protects Sharia precisely by refusing to enforce it.**

That finding substantially strengthens the book’s religious-liberty thesis while preserving the hard truths established in Pass 17.
