# Research Pass 11 — What Sharia Means to American Muslims

## Governing question

What do American Muslims themselves mean when they say they follow Sharia, and how does that compare with the political claim that Sharia represents a competing sovereign legal system?

## Executive finding

The strongest empirical evidence located in this pass does **not** support the claim that ordinary North American Muslims understand Sharia primarily as a program for replacing U.S. law. The clearest interview-based study — Julie Macfarlane’s four-year qualitative research with 212 Muslims in the United States and Canada, including divorced men and women, imams, scholars, lawyers, therapists, social workers, and community leaders — found that participants overwhelmingly treated Sharia as a private religious, moral, family, and identity framework. Nearly all respondents used civil law alongside Islamic marriage/divorce processes, and none of the interviewees advocated that American courts directly impose Islamic law on Muslims or non-Muslims.

The most defensible formulation is therefore:

**For many American Muslims, Sharia functions less like a competing constitution and more like a religious rule of life — prayer, fasting, charity, diet, marriage, personal morality, financial ethics, family obligations, and the attempt to live faithfully before God.**

This is not universal. American Muslims are highly diverse, and there is no single American Muslim view of Sharia. Some Muslims are conservative, some progressive, some secular, some highly observant, some minimally observant, and many differ over gender, sexuality, family law, finance, politics, jurisprudence, and the proper role of religious authority. The key finding is not unanimity. It is that the political caricature of a unified Muslim population seeking a parallel sovereign legal system is unsupported by the best empirical research found in this pass.

## 1. The strongest empirical study: Julie Macfarlane

### Source
Julie Macfarlane, *Islamic Divorce in North America: A Shari’a Path in a Secular Society* (Oxford University Press, 2012), and the Institute for Social Policy and Understanding report *Shari’a Law: Coming to a Courthouse near You?: What Shari’a Really Means to American Muslims*.

Macfarlane’s study interviewed **212 individuals between 2006 and 2010**. The respondent pool included Muslim men and women who had experienced divorce, imams, religious scholars, lawyers, social workers, therapists, and community leaders.

### What the study found

- For most respondents, Sharia was understood primarily as a **private system of morality, identity, and religious practice**.
- The most common legal-religious context in which Sharia mattered was **marriage and divorce**.
- Almost all respondents who entered Islamic marriages also registered civil marriages.
- Almost all respondents seeking divorce used the civil legal system as well as a religious process.
- Islamic divorce had no independent coercive legal force in U.S. or Canadian courts.
- Respondents did not expect American courts to replace civil family law with Islamic law.
- Macfarlane reported that none of the interviewees — including imams, Muslim lawyers, and legal scholars — argued that U.S. courts should directly impose Sharia on Muslims or non-Muslims.

This is extremely important because it replaces speculation with actual interviews.

### Evidence grade
**A/B — strong empirical evidence regarding the interviewed North American Muslim population.**

Caution: qualitative interviews cannot be treated as a statistically representative national poll. The study is powerful evidence about lived meaning and practice, not proof that every American Muslim thinks identically.

## 2. The marriage/divorce example explains the difference between religious and civil law

One of the clearest analogies for American readers is marriage.

Many Muslims interviewed by Macfarlane regarded the **nikah**, the Islamic marriage contract/ceremony, as the religiously meaningful marriage. Yet nearly all also obtained the civil marriage license required by the state.

Likewise, many divorcing Muslims sought a religious divorce or religious recognition of the end of the marriage while also obtaining a civil divorce.

That means the individual was often married twice in two different senses:

1. recognized by the religious community;
2. recognized by the state.

And divorced in those same two senses.

American Christians already understand this distinction instinctively. A Catholic marriage may have a sacramental status under canon law that is distinct from civil marriage law. A Catholic annulment does not erase a state divorce decree, and a civil judge does not decide Catholic sacramental validity.

Likewise, an Orthodox Jewish get has religious meaning independent of the civil divorce decree.

The Muslim use of nikah and Islamic divorce belongs in this comparative religious-liberty framework.

### Key formulation

**Religious recognition and civil recognition are not competing sovereignties simply because they answer different questions.**

The state asks: Are you legally married?

The believer may separately ask: Is my marriage religiously valid before God?

## 3. What “following Sharia” can mean in ordinary life

Multiple Muslim educational and research sources describe everyday Sharia practice in categories such as:

- five daily prayers;
- fasting during Ramadan;
- charitable giving (zakat and other charity);
- halal dietary practice;
- avoiding alcohol;
- avoiding gambling;
- family obligations;
- honoring parents;
- marriage and divorce rituals;
- modest dress;
- personal sexual ethics;
- honesty in business;
- debt and contract ethics;
- avoidance of interest/usury in some interpretations;
- inheritance planning;
- care for neighbors and the poor;
- environmental stewardship;
- personal moral discipline.

The crucial rhetorical point is that these practices are usually invisible when politicians use the word “Sharia.”

A political audience may hear:

**Sharia = amputations, stoning, theocracy.**

An observant Muslim may hear:

**Sharia = how I pray, fast, give, eat, marry, conduct business, raise my family, and try to behave ethically.**

The same word is functioning at two radically different levels.

## 4. Sharia is not one bound statute book

This pass reinforces an earlier finding: “Sharia” is not equivalent to the Arkansas Code or United States Code.

A useful distinction:

- **Sharia** = the divine path or ideal of living according to God’s will.
- **Fiqh** = human jurisprudence — the interpretive effort by jurists to derive concrete rules.

Because fiqh is human interpretation, different schools and scholars can disagree.

This explains why American Muslims can say they follow Sharia while disagreeing intensely about what particular rules require.

That diversity matters because political rhetoric often treats “Sharia says” as though one legislature published a single Islamic code.

A better journalistic question is always:

**Which interpretation, which school, which scholar, which time period, and which issue?**

## 5. American Muslim religious practice resembles American religious practice more broadly

Pew Research Center’s surveys show that American Muslims are religiously serious but internally diverse.

In 2017:

- 65% said religion was very important in their lives;
- 42% reported praying all five daily salah;
- another 17% prayed at least some salah daily;
- a majority said Islam is open to more than one interpretation.

Pew’s earlier comparison found 57% of U.S. Muslims saying Islam could be understood in more than one way, compared with 37% saying there is one correct interpretation.

This is a crucial corrective to the idea of a single ideologically uniform American Muslim population.

The most recent ISPU American Muslim Poll located in this pass, fielded by NORC at the University of Chicago in 2025, similarly found American Muslims highly religious but civically integrated:

- 89% rated religion as important to daily life;
- 44% attended religious services weekly or more;
- 85% of legally eligible Muslims reported being registered to vote.

These facts do not prove attitudes toward Sharia specifically, but they establish the broader context: religious seriousness and participation in American civic institutions coexist.

## 6. Muslim and American are not experienced as mutually exclusive identities

Pew’s 2017 national survey found:

- 92% of U.S. Muslims said they were proud to be American;
- 97% said they were proud to be Muslim;
- 89% were both proud to be American and proud to be Muslim.

This finding is particularly useful because the Sharia-scare narrative often assumes a zero-sum identity:

**the more Muslim a person is, the less American the person must be.**

The survey evidence does not support that binary.

A believer can understand Islam as morally binding while understanding the Constitution as civilly binding.

That is not unusual in American religious life.

A devout Catholic can believe divine law is morally superior to human law while still obeying American civil law.

An Orthodox Jew can regard halakha as binding religiously while functioning within American law.

An observant Muslim can likewise regard Sharia as divinely authoritative for religious conscience without believing an imam possesses sovereign power over American courts.

## 7. The most important first-person framing found

One useful public first-person example came from Muslim physician and community leader Mushir Hassan in Wisconsin. Asked directly about Sharia, he said Muslims living in America respect the laws of the land and described Sharia as guidance for personal moral life rather than a program to supersede the Constitution.

This is not representative evidence by itself. It should therefore be used as illustration, not proof.

The empirical proof comes from Macfarlane and national survey work.

But this type of testimony suggests the interview scenes we should seek for the book.

## 8. A Muslim scholar formulation worth examining carefully

In a 2010 Pew forum, Tariq Ramadan argued that for Muslims living in countries where law guarantees equality, living faithfully within that legal order can itself be consistent with the objectives of Sharia. He rejected the framing of Western civil law and Sharia as two sealed competing systems.

Ramadan is a particular scholar and controversial public figure; his theory should not be attributed to all Muslims.

But it illustrates a broader jurisprudential possibility:

**Sharia can be understood as directing Muslims to seek justice and ethical faithfulness within the lawful constitutional order where they live rather than establishing a rival state.**

This idea deserves specialist legal-theological verification in later passes.

## 9. Important limit: American Muslims do not agree on everything

This book must avoid replacing anti-Muslim stereotyping with Muslim romanticism.

American Muslims differ over:

- gender roles;
- LGBTQ questions;
- abortion;
- interest-based finance;
- interfaith marriage;
- inheritance;
- religious authority;
- hijab;
- mosque governance;
- political parties;
- foreign policy;
- criminal jurisprudence;
- the proper role of classical fiqh;
- whether and how Islamic law should evolve in modernity.

Some American Muslims hold socially conservative views that progressive Americans will strongly reject.

Others are progressive.

Some seek traditional juristic guidance in many areas of life.

Others treat Islam primarily as spiritual identity.

Some barely practice.

That diversity is exactly the point.

**There is no single American Muslim mind behind the word Sharia.**

---

## 10. Important limit: foreign polling cannot be imported into American Muslims

Pew’s global surveys show substantial support for official Sharia law in numerous Muslim-majority countries, with enormous variation by country. Those findings are real and must not be hidden.

But they cannot simply be projected onto Muslims living in the United States.

Political culture, constitutional structure, minority/majority status, national history, religious institutions, migration, and lived experience are radically different.

The global evidence actually reinforces the larger methodological lesson:

**“Sharia” does not mean one identical political program across Muslim societies.**

Even among Muslim-majority countries, support and understandings vary sharply.

## 11. The strongest narrative juxtaposition for the book

Pass Ten documented politicians describing Sharia as an organized threat to American government.

Pass Eleven gives us the counter-scene.

Politician:

> They want Sharia.

Then ask an ordinary Muslim:

> What does Sharia mean in your life?

Potential answers we should investigate through original interviews:

- “It tells me to pray.”
- “It tells me to fast.”
- “It tells me how much charity I owe.”
- “It tells me not to cheat people in business.”
- “It affects what I eat.”
- “It tells me to honor my parents.”
- “It guides my marriage.”
- “It tells me I will answer to God for how I treat people.”

Do **not** put these words in anyone’s mouth. They are hypotheses generated from the existing empirical literature and should become interview questions.

The power of the eventual chapter will come from actual named human beings explaining their own faith.

## 12. Original interview plan for the book

Existing literature is not enough. We should conduct our own interviews.

### Interview categories

Seek approximately 12–20 interviews across diverse viewpoints:

1. Imam from a mainstream Arkansas mosque.
2. Muslim attorney.
3. Muslim mother raising children in Arkansas.
4. Muslim physician or healthcare professional.
5. Muslim military veteran.
6. Black American Muslim with multigenerational U.S. roots.
7. Immigrant Muslim from a Muslim-majority country.
8. American convert to Islam.
9. Conservative/traditional Muslim.
10. Progressive Muslim.
11. Muslim woman who wears hijab.
12. Muslim woman who does not wear hijab.
13. Young college-age Muslim.
14. Muslim small-business owner.
15. Muslim scholar of Islamic law.
16. Former Muslim or Muslim critic willing to discuss difficult aspects of Sharia.

The last category matters. The book should not only interview Muslims who validate our thesis.

### Core interview questions

Ask every participant:

1. When you hear the word **Sharia**, what does it mean to you personally?
2. What parts of Sharia affect an ordinary day in your life?
3. Does Sharia affect prayer, food, money, family, work, or charity for you?
4. Do you believe American courts should enforce Islamic religious law?
5. If Sharia and American law conflict, what do you believe a Muslim living in America should do?
6. Does following Sharia require establishing an Islamic government?
7. What do you think Americans misunderstand most about Sharia?
8. What parts of classical Islamic law make you uncomfortable or require reinterpretation today?
9. How do you understand Qur’an 2:256, “no compulsion in religion”? 
10. How do you understand classical apostasy law?
11. How do you understand hudud punishments?
12. What does religious liberty mean to you as an American Muslim?
13. Could America prohibit Christian religious practice in the name of preventing Christian nationalism? Why or why not?
14. What would you want a Christian neighbor in rural Arkansas to understand about your faith?
15. What does being **American** mean to you?
16. What does being **Muslim** mean to you?
17. Do those identities ever conflict?
18. Have you ever been accused of wanting to impose Sharia?
19. What did that accusation feel like?
20. What question about Islam do you wish someone would finally ask instead?

### Hard-question rule

Every interview should include hard questions.

We should not ask only friendly questions about prayer and charity.

Ask about:

- apostasy;
- blasphemy;
- hudud;
- women’s rights;
- LGBTQ rights;
- jihad;
- democracy;
- religious minorities;
- theocracy;
- whether classical doctrines should ever become state law.

That is how the chapter earns trust.

## 13. The constitutional insight

The empirical evidence from this pass points back toward Jefferson and Madison.

The Constitution does not require Muslims to secularize their consciences before receiving religious liberty.

It requires government to distinguish between **belief/religious practice** and **coercive unlawful conduct**.

A Muslim praying five times daily is exercising religion.

A Muslim fasting during Ramadan is exercising religion.

A Muslim choosing halal food is exercising religion.

A Muslim voluntarily structuring a permissible contract around religious principles may be exercising religion and contract rights.

A Muslim attempting to use violence or coercive governmental power against another person is subject to the same neutral civil law as everyone else.

The same analytical structure applies to Christians, Jews, Hindus, Sikhs, and others.

### Key formulation

**America does not require Muslims to stop following Sharia. America requires Sharia, Christianity, halakha, and every other religious authority to remain subject to the Constitution whenever religious practice becomes coercive civil power.**

## 14. Strong lines to preserve

**“The political argument begins with the sentence ‘They want Sharia.’ The journalistic response should be another sentence: ‘Then let’s ask them what they mean.’”**

**“For many American Muslims, Sharia is not what the government does to you. It is what faith asks of you.”**

**“The word Americans have been taught to hear as ‘Islamic government’ is often heard by Muslims as ‘how I am supposed to live.’”**

**“A Muslim can believe Sharia comes from God without believing an imam outranks the Constitution, just as a Christian can believe the Bible comes from God without believing a pastor outranks the Supreme Court.”**

**“Religious liberty becomes impossible if believing a divine command is itself treated as evidence of political disloyalty.”**

**“Before America decides what Sharia means to Muslims, America should probably ask some Muslims.”**

## 15. Evidence grading

### A/B — Strong empirical grounding
- Macfarlane’s 212-person qualitative interview study: Sharia primarily private/religious/family; civil law remained operative.
- Nearly all interviewees used civil marriage/divorce processes alongside religious processes.
- None advocated direct judicial imposition of Sharia through American courts.
- Pew national survey: overwhelming simultaneous pride in American and Muslim identities.
- Pew: American Muslims show substantial internal interpretive diversity.
- ISPU/NORC 2025: high religious importance coexists with high voter registration and civic participation.

### B/C — Strong explanatory material but institutionally affiliated
- ISPU Muslim educational material describing Sharia as prayer, fasting, charity, diet, finance, personal ethics, marriage, etc.
- Muslim public educators and community leaders describing Sharia as personal moral guidance.

### C — Illustrative individual testimony
- Individual Muslim physicians, imams, scholars, and activists explaining personal understandings of Sharia.

These should humanize, not establish population-wide claims.

### Unsupported and should not be claimed
- “No American Muslim wants political Sharia.”
- “All American Muslims interpret Sharia peacefully.”
- “American Muslims unanimously support secular government.”
- “Sharia never conflicts with modern constitutional values.”
- “Every classical Islamic doctrine is compatible with U.S. law.”

All are too broad.

## Bottom line

Pass Eleven fundamentally changes how the eventual Sharia chapter should be written.

Do not begin by defining Sharia for Muslims.

Begin by showing Americans what politicians say Sharia means.

Then ask Muslims.

Existing empirical research suggests the answer will often be surprisingly ordinary: faith, prayer, family, ethics, charity, marriage, food, money, and accountability to God.

Then ask the hard questions about coercion, punishment, gender, apostasy, and theocracy.

That structure prevents apologetics while also preventing caricature.

The most important conclusion from this pass is therefore:

**The constitutional question is not whether a Muslim follows Sharia. The constitutional question is whether anyone — Muslim, Christian, Jewish, or otherwise — is using religious authority to coerce another person through powers the Constitution does not permit.**
