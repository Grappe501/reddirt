# Architecture Stage 5 — Major Parts Architecture

## Purpose

Stages One through Four established the reader journey, governing questions, evidence map, and revelation order. Stage Five divides that journey into the major movements of the book.

This is **not yet a chapter outline**.

The goal is to decide where the book changes intellectual and emotional gears, what each major Part must accomplish, what question the reader carries into it, and what new question forces the reader into the next Part.

The architecture proposed here uses **seven major Parts plus a short prologue and epilogue**.

The seven-Part design is intentional. The book needs enough room to distinguish:

1. the fear;
2. the religious/legal complexity;
3. Christian America;
4. the founding-era Muslim constitutional test;
5. the modern Sharia scare;
6. Arkansas and living Muslims;
7. the constitutional resolution.

Collapsing these too aggressively would weaken the revelation order.

---

# PROLOGUE — THE WORD

## Job

Put the reader face-to-face with the word **Sharia** before explaining it.

The prologue should establish danger, uncertainty, and the narrator's investigative posture.

It should not resolve anything.

## Reader enters believing

Sharia is a foreign Islamic legal system threatening American law.

## The prologue asks

> What exactly are we afraid of when we say “Sharia”?

## What we may show

Modern American political warnings.

Images/rhetoric associated with Taliban rule, Iran, apostasy, blasphemy, stoning, jihad, women's status, religious government.

Potential Arkansas/local entry point if reporting later produces an extraordinary scene.

## What we deliberately do not reveal

Jefferson's Qur'an.

Article VI.

The Muslim-president debate.

Early American Muslims.

*Holt v. Hobbs*.

The final constitutional thesis.

## Exit

The narrator decides to investigate rather than reassure.

Possible conceptual exit:

> I expected to discover that the frightening stories about Sharia were mostly inventions. That was not what I found.

Then Part I begins.

# PART I — THE FEAR

## Function

Earn the skeptical reader's trust by taking the strongest anti-Sharia arguments seriously.

This Part must establish that the book is not apologetics.

## Governing question

> Are there things in Islamic law that should genuinely trouble an American constitutional democracy?

## Core evidence

Pass 17 — Steelmanning the Anti-Sharia Case.

Pass 12 — Hard Cases.

Supporting material from Passes 7, 9, 10, 11, and 18.

## Required subjects

Apostasy.

Blasphemy.

Hudud.

Gender and family law.

Dhimmi/religious-status systems.

Jihad and warfare jurisprudence.

Sharia supremacy clauses.

Modern states enforcing religious law.

International survey evidence showing genuine support for severe punishments in some societies.

## Narrative rule

Never begin a hard case with its reformist explanation.

Let the reader encounter the objection honestly first.

Then introduce complexity.

## Emotional movement

Fear → confirmation → unease → curiosity.

## Reader exits believing

Some historically authoritative Islamic doctrines would be unacceptable if imposed through American government.

But the reader now has a new problem:

> Does “Sharia” always mean those things?

That launches Part II.

# PART II — THE MIRROR

## Function

Break the assumption that Sharia is a single political code and establish how religious legal traditions actually work.

Then use the Bible–Qur'an mirror carefully to force interpretive symmetry.

## Governing questions

> What is Sharia?

> Who gets to decide what a religion means?

> How do religious communities live with ancient law they no longer want government to enforce?

## Core evidence

Pass 7 — Sharia and Constitutional Architecture.

Pass 9 — Bible–Qur'an Mirror.

Pass 11 — What Sharia Means to American Muslims.

Pass 18 — Islamic Reform and Constitutionalism.

Pass 14 — Constitutional Concordance, selectively.

## Major movements

### Sharia is larger than criminal law

Devotion, ethics, worship, family, contracts, finance, charity, jurisprudence, politics.

### Sharia versus fiqh

Divine ideal versus fallible human jurisprudence as a useful analytical distinction.

### Islam argues with itself

Schools, jurists, reformers, traditionalists, Islamists, constitutionalists, secular Muslims.

### The mirror

Biblical apostasy/idolatry, blasphemy, adultery, slavery, warfare, retaliation.

Not “Christianity is just as bad.”

Instead:

> Why does quoting an ancient biblical legal text not automatically tell us what a modern Christian wants the legislature to enact?

### Islamic reform

An-Na'im, Abou El Fadl, Soroush, Ghannouchi, Auda, Kamali, Hashemi and others.

The purpose is not to prove Islam has become liberal democracy.

It is to prove the political meaning of Islam is internally contested.

## Material to minimize

Long comparative-law excursions into shura/Congress, ijma/democracy, ijtihad/judicial review.

Those parallels may appear, but they must not distract from the book's actual argument.

## Emotional movement

Fear → complexity → recognition → intellectual humility.

## Reader exits asking

> Fine. Religions interpret themselves. But America was founded as a Christian country. Why should Islam receive equal constitutional standing?

Now Part III begins.

# PART III — CHRISTIAN AMERICA

## Function

Build the strongest historically responsible version of the Christian-America argument before challenging the constitutional conclusion often drawn from it.

This Part is essential. Without it, conservative Christian readers will correctly feel that the book evaded their strongest historical argument.

## Governing question

> Was America founded as a Christian nation?

## Core evidence

Pass 16 — Christian America Counterargument.

Supporting evidence from Washington, Madison, founding documents, state constitutions, Northwest Ordinance, public prayer and religious institutions.

## Major movements

### Yes, Christianity mattered enormously

Deep Christian culture.

Christian institutions.

Christian moral vocabulary.

Public religious practices.

Religious proclamations.

### States sometimes explicitly privileged Christianity

Massachusetts.

North Carolina.

Other relevant state tests/establishments as later expanded.

### The key drafting fact

Americans knew how to write Christian qualifications.

This cannot be treated as an abstract possibility.

They actually wrote them.

## Central turn

Now ask:

> If Americans knew how to require Christianity when they wanted to, what did the federal Constitution require?

## Emotional movement

Recognition → affirmation → tension.

## Reader exits

The Christian-America argument has been conceded in its strongest cultural form.

But cultural Christianity and federal constitutional Christianity have become separate questions.

The reader now needs to see Article VI.

That launches Part IV.

# PART IV — THE MUSLIM TEST

## Function

Deliver the book's central historical revelations.

This should probably become the longest or second-longest Part.

## Governing question

> Did the founding generation understand that the constitutional system they were building could include Muslims?

## Core evidence

## Major movement 1 — Article VI

No religious Test.

Let the text stand before interpreting it.

## Major movement 2 — They noticed

North Carolina 1788.

Abbot.

Iredell.

Johnston.

Lancaster.

The possibility of Muslims holding federal office, potentially the presidency.

This is the **central historical reveal of the entire book**.

> The Muslim president was not a loophole nobody noticed. He was a possibility somebody complained about.

## Major movement 3 — Jefferson

Explicit Mahamedan civil-rights language.

Virginia religious liberty.

Then the restraint:

No evidence that Jefferson used Islamic law to write the Constitution.

## Major movement 4 — The circle widens

Richard Henry Lee.

Washington.

Madison's conscience framework.

Massachusetts evidence.

Virginia petitions.

## Major movement 5 — Hostility belongs in the story too

Barbary conflict.

Stereotypes.

Religious prejudice.

Founders were not uniformly tolerant.

This produces the stronger principle:

> They did not have to like Islam to protect the Muslim.

## Major movement 6 — The Muslims already here

Ayuba.

Yarrow.

Omar.

Bilali.

Enslaved African Muslims.

## Moral turn

> America imagined the rights of the hypothetical Muslim while enslaving the real one.

This prevents triumphalist founding mythology.

## Emotional movement

Tension → surprise → discovery → contradiction → moral gravity.

## Reader exits believing

Muslim constitutional belonging is not a twenty-first-century invention.

But another mystery appears:

> If Muslims were part of the religious-liberty argument so early, how did “Sharia” become a modern domestic threat category?

# PART V — THE SCARE

## Function

Trace how real foreign conflict, terrorism, coercive Islamic legal systems, political activism, and domestic rhetoric became fused into the modern American category “Sharia threat.”

This Part should be investigative rather than accusatory.

## Governing question

> How did the Muslim move from a founding-era test of religious liberty to a modern political threat category?

## Core evidence

Pass 10 — Genealogy of the Sharia Scare.

Pass 17 — Anti-Sharia steelman.

Pass 12 — Hard cases.

Pass 11 — American Muslim lived Sharia.

Modern 2025–2026 material.

## Major movements

### 9/11

Acknowledge the reality of Islamist terrorism without euphemism.

### Foreign coercive systems

Show why Americans encountering Taliban, Iranian, Saudi, Pakistani or other laws might reasonably associate Sharia with coercion.

### The category expands

Park51.

Threat reports.

Oklahoma SQ755.

Model legislation.

Advocacy networks.

Taqiyya conspiracy rhetoric.

Congressional rhetoric.

### The conceptual collapse

Personal devotion.

Classical jurisprudence.

Religious arbitration.

Islamist politics.

Terrorism.

Theocracy.

All begin occupying the same rhetorical box.

## Critical fairness rule

Do not claim the entire movement was fabricated from nothing.

The book's stronger argument is:

> Real examples of coercive religious law became evidence used to cast suspicion on categories of people whose actual beliefs had not been established.

## Emotional movement

Recognition → alarm → analytical clarity.

## Reader exits asking

> If coercive Sharia really would be unconstitutional, doesn't American law already have a way to stop it without treating Muslims themselves as a threat?

That question launches Part VI.

# PART VI — ARKANSAS

This Part should contain some of the most vivid contemporary narrative writing in the book.

## Governing questions

> What do Arkansas Muslims themselves believe?

> What do Arkansas Christians fear?

## Core evidence

## Major movement 2 — Arkansas's religious-liberty identity

# PART VII — THE WALL

## Function

Resolve the investigation without pretending to resolve theology.

This is where the reader finally understands what the book has actually been about.

## Governing question

> Can the same constitutional rule protect the Christian, Muslim, former Muslim, Jew, believer and unbeliever without giving any of them religious sovereignty over the others?

## Evidence

Article VI.

First Amendment.

Founding conscience philosophy.

Modern religious-exercise doctrine.

Arkansas constitutional language.

*Holt*.

Interview evidence.

Hard-case evidence.

Christian-America evidence.

The entire book converges here.

## Major movement 1 — The symmetry test

Christian asks:

Do not make me live under Sharia.

Muslim asks:

Do not make me live under Christianity.

Former Muslim asks:

Do not let religious authority punish me for leaving.

Jew asks:

Do not make Christian or Muslim theology the condition of citizenship.

Atheist asks:

Do not make me practice any religion.

## Major movement 2 — What religious liberty protects

Conscience.

Belief.

Worship.

Speech.

Association.

Religious identity.

Lawful religious practice.

Political participation.

## Major movement 3 — What religious liberty does not automatically authorize

Violence.

Coercion.

Private criminal punishment.

Government religious supremacy.

Loss of other people's civil rights.

A religious institution replacing constitutional civil sovereignty.

## Major movement 4 — The jurisdiction insight

The state does not own conscience.

Religious authorities do not own civil sovereignty.

That is the wall.

## Major movement 5 — Return to the founders

Jefferson.

Madison.

Article VI.

The Muslim-president debate.

Washington.

But now the evidence means something different than when first encountered.

## Major movement 6 — The Muslim is the test

Only now fully explain the phrase.

> Religious liberty is only real when it protects the religion the majority fears.

The Muslim is not the only test.

Historically and narratively, the Muslim becomes our test because Americans explicitly used the Muslim as a boundary case when arguing over religious liberty, and modern politics again uses Islam as a boundary case.

## Emotional movement

Disagreement → symmetry → clarity → self-examination → conviction.

## Reader exits believing

Not:

“Islam is harmless.”

Not:

“Christianity is dangerous.”

Not:

“Sharia built America.”

But:

> I do not have to approve of my neighbor's religion to understand why his liberty and mine are constitutionally connected.

# EPILOGUE — WHO OWNS YOUR CONSCIENCE?

## Function

Reduce the entire investigation to the human question beneath the constitutional machinery.

Return to people rather than doctrine.

Potentially return to a person or scene introduced during the Arkansas reporting section.

The epilogue should be shorter, quieter, and less argumentative than Part VII.

## Final reciprocal test

To the Christian:

If Muslims became the overwhelming political majority tomorrow, what religious liberty would you demand?

To the Muslim:

If Christians became overwhelmingly powerful, what liberty would you demand?

To the former Muslim:

Who has the authority to compel you to remain?

To the unbeliever:

Who has the authority to make you worship?

Then:

> Who owns your conscience?

The constitutional answer should no longer need a lecture.

The reader should already know it.

# STRUCTURAL BALANCE

The Parts should not be equal in length.

A preliminary proportional model:

- Prologue — 2–3%
- Part I: The Fear — 11–13%
- Part II: The Mirror — 12–14%
- Part III: Christian America — 10–12%
- Part IV: The Muslim Test — 20–23%
- Part V: The Scare — 12–14%
- Part VI: Arkansas — 18–21%
- Part VII: The Wall — 9–11%
- Epilogue — 2–3%

These percentages are architectural guidance, not word-count mandates.

Part IV and Part VI deserve the most space because they contain the book's strongest historical evidence and original reporting respectively.

---

# THE BOOK'S THREE ACTS INSIDE THE SEVEN PARTS

Although the reader sees seven Parts, the deeper narrative can operate as three acts.

## ACT ONE — WHAT ARE WE AFRAID OF?

Prologue + Parts I–II.

The reader investigates Sharia itself.

## ACT TWO — WHO DID AMERICA DECIDE BELONGED?

Parts III–V.

The reader moves through Christian America, the founding Muslim test, and the modern reversal.

## ACT THREE — DOES THE PRINCIPLE STILL WORK?

Parts VI–VII + Epilogue.

Arkansas becomes the live test, followed by constitutional synthesis.

This gives the book both investigative granularity and a simple underlying dramatic structure.

Appears initially as an unexplained or partially explained idea; fully resolved in Part VII.

## “Who owns your conscience?”

The deepest hidden question. Use sparingly so it does not become sloganistic.

A recurring symmetry device when evaluating government religious preference.

A recurring question after Part II whenever political rhetoric uses the word without definition.

A possible motif around Article VI and the Muslim-officeholder debate.

# WHAT EACH PART MUST NOT BECOME

## Part I must not become

An anti-Islam polemic.

## Part II must not become

An Islamic-studies textbook or an apologetic defense of Islam.

## Part III must not become

An attack on Christianity.

## Part IV must not become

Founder worship or a “secret Muslim Constitution” conspiracy in reverse.

## Part V must not become

A claim that Islamist terrorism or coercive religious law is imaginary.

## Part VI must not become

A collection of friendly Muslim testimonials selected to prove the thesis.

## Part VII must not become

A civics lecture detached from the human beings whose liberty is at stake.

---

# STAGE FIVE GATE

Before Stage Six begins, the architecture should satisfy these conditions:

1. The book can be described in seven major movements without reference to chapter titles.
2. Every Part has one clear job.
3. Every Part answers a governing question and creates the next one.
4. The central historical reveal remains in Part IV.
5. Original Arkansas reporting receives enough narrative weight to change the book rather than merely decorate it.
6. The Christian-America case is heard before the federal constitutional distinction is made.
7. The anti-Sharia case is heard before the religious-liberty defense is completed.
8. The final resolution protects the Muslim, Christian, former Muslim, and unbeliever under the same principle.
9. No Part depends on proving direct Sharia influence on the Constitution.
10. The reader reaches the final constitutional answer through discovery rather than being told the conclusion at the beginning.

If these gates hold, Stage Six can build the **chapter architecture inside each Part**: chapter function, opening scene, governing question, evidence package, counterargument, major reveal, interview dependency, and transition—without yet drafting the manuscript itself.
