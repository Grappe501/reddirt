# Pass 28 — Arkansas Muslim History Archive

## Purpose

This pass reconstructs the history of Muslim life in Arkansas before the book's interview stage.

The reporting rule is important: interviews should deepen, challenge and humanize a history we have already attempted to reconstruct. We should not walk into a mosque and ask current congregants to manufacture the state's history from memory for us.

The emerging Arkansas story is not one immigration story. It contains at least two major twentieth-century streams that later overlap and diversify:

1. Black American Muslim institution-building in Little Rock, including a Nation of Islam congregation established by 1969; and
2. immigrant and international-student Muslim communities connected to Arkansas universities, medicine and later corporate/economic migration.

The state then develops multiple local Muslim communities rather than one centralized “Arkansas Islam.”

This archive is provisional until local primary records, newspapers, organizational archives and oral histories are fully examined.

---

# 1. The first known Arkansas mosque

The Encyclopedia of Arkansas identifies the earliest known mosque in Arkansas as a congregation established in **1969 at 10th and Schiller in Little Rock**.

Its first minister is identified as **Roy X**.

The congregation primarily served African-American members of the Nation of Islam.

The institution later became associated with the **Islamic Center for Human Excellence**, also known as **New Africa**, and moved to Wright Avenue.

## Historical significance

Arkansas Muslim history therefore should not be narrated as though Islam arrived only with physicians, engineers or international university students from the Middle East and South Asia.

A documented Black Muslim institution existed in Little Rock by 1969.

That places organized Muslim religious life inside the same city whose modern civic identity is inseparable from the Black freedom struggle surrounding Central High School.

We should investigate, rather than assume, what direct relationships existed between Black Muslim organizing and Arkansas civil-rights organizations.

## Claim grade

**A/B** for 1969 mosque, location, Roy X and primarily African-American Nation of Islam congregation, based on Encyclopedia of Arkansas's synthesis and cited Arkansas newspaper sources. Upgrade to A after direct newspaper/archive verification.

---

# 2. Black Muslim Arkansas must remain a separate historical stream

The Nation of Islam is not interchangeable with Sunni Islam, and later institutional transitions must be reconstructed carefully.

The book should not flatten:

- Nation of Islam theology;
- Black Sunni communities;
- immigrant Sunni communities;
- Shia communities;
- Sufi-oriented institutions;
- reform/progressive Muslims;
- unaffiliated Muslims.

Likewise, “Black Muslim” cannot be treated as synonymous with “Nation of Islam.”

The 1969 institution matters because it demonstrates that Black Arkansans were building Muslim religious community before the better-documented immigrant institutional expansion of the 1980s and 1990s.

## Reporting target

Locate elders or archival witnesses who can reconstruct:

- the 10th and Schiller congregation;
- Roy X;
- its relationship to national Nation of Islam structures;
- transition into later forms of Muslim identity/institution;
- movement to Wright Avenue;
- the origin and meaning of “New Africa”;
- women's roles;
- youth education;
- neighborhood relationships;
- interaction with churches and civil-rights organizations.

---

# 3. University migration changes the map

The Encyclopedia of Arkansas reports that significant numbers of Muslims began arriving in Arkansas during the **1960s**, many through university-related exchange and educational programs.

Students came from the Middle East, India, Bangladesh and other regions.

The University of Arkansas at Fayetteville, Arkansas State University in Jonesboro and the University of Arkansas at Little Rock became important nodes of Muslim student life.

This means Arkansas's Muslim geography developed partly through higher education rather than solely through conventional immigrant neighborhood settlement.

## Historical pattern

A recurring institutional sequence appears to be:

**student/temporary population → informal prayer → Muslim student association/community organization → permanent families/professionals → mosque/institution.**

That pattern should be tested city by city.

---

# 4. Central Arkansas before ICLR

The institutional history of the Islamic Center of Little Rock does not begin in 1992.

Earlier community history needs reconstruction.

Prior research for this project identified a Central Arkansas Muslim community by the late 1970s, including approximately twenty African-American Muslim families associated with **Masjid Ameen Zakariah**, alongside immigrant Muslim families in Little Rock, North Little Rock and Jacksonville.

Home-based Qur'an study and informal gatherings reportedly occurred around 1980.

A Muslim Association structure involving Arkansas/Pine Bluff also appears in the early history.

These details are important but should be upgraded through primary/local organizational documentation and interviews before final publication.

## Why Jacksonville matters

Jacksonville should not disappear from the archive merely because the best-known mosque was eventually built in Little Rock.

Military, professional, immigrant and metropolitan movement may have linked Little Rock, North Little Rock and Jacksonville Muslim families.

This is a specific oral-history target.

---

# 5. The Second Presbyterian Church story

One of the most promising Arkansas religious-liberty stories concerns early Muslim-Christian interfaith relationships in Little Rock.

Prior project research identified an interfaith dialogue among Jews, Christians and Muslims sponsored through **Second Presbyterian Church**, with the church reportedly making Sunday-school rooms available without charge for Muslim meetings beginning around **1985**.

This episode is potentially extraordinary for the book.

But it must be reconstructed from primary evidence before being narrated confidently.

## Why it matters

If verified, this is not an abstract theological argument.

Christians with their own sanctuary effectively said to Muslim neighbors:

> You need somewhere to meet? Use ours.

That is religious liberty expressed socially rather than judicially.

It also complicates any narrative that assumes Arkansas Christian-Muslim relations have always been antagonistic.

## Required verification

Seek:

- Second Presbyterian minutes;
- newsletters;
- interfaith committee records;
- pastors/staff from the period;
- Muslim participants;
- photographs;
- local newspaper coverage;
- dates and names;
- whether rooms were used for prayer, study, organizational meetings or all three.

Do not publish the full anecdote as settled until this verification is complete.

# 6. University of Arkansas at Little Rock

UA Little Rock became an important institutional environment for Central Arkansas Muslims.

International students, including students from Saudi Arabia and other Middle Eastern countries, contributed to the growth of Muslim prayer and community networks during the 1980s and later.

Prior project research indicates Jummah/prayer rooms or campus meeting spaces were used before the Islamic Center of Little Rock obtained a permanent facility.

UA Little Rock is also important in the present because its Little Rock Congregations Study has documented local Muslim congregations, interfaith activity and lived religious practice.

## Archive targets

- Muslim Student Association records;
- student newspapers;
- international-student office records;
- room-reservation records where preserved;
- photographs;
- faculty/staff memories;
- Saudi/international training program records;
- early Jummah locations.

# 7. Islamic Center of Little Rock — formal incorporation

The Encyclopedia of Arkansas identifies the **Islamic Center of Little Rock (ICLR)** as founded in **1992** by Hashim Ghori, Ahmed Golzar, M. Hamid Hussain, Abdul Hamid Patel, Ali U. Shaikh and Ali Akbar.

The founders were originally from India and had moved to Arkansas around 1978.

The initial center occupied a roughly **900-square-foot house on Anna Street near UA Little Rock**.

As the Muslim population grew, the center acquired adjacent property and began construction of a larger building in **May 1994**.

The new center officially opened **January 13, 1996**.

The Encyclopedia reports approximately sixty members at that point.

## Growth driver

A major factor was the arrival and retention of Muslim physicians through residency and fellowship programs at the University of Arkansas for Medical Sciences.

This is a significant Arkansas immigration pattern: temporary professional training became permanent community formation when doctors and families remained in the state.

## Claim grade

**A/B**, pending direct organizational incorporation/property/archive verification.

---

# 8. ICLR becomes more than a prayer hall

UA Little Rock's Little Rock Congregations Study describes ICLR as a religious, educational, social and cultural institution rather than merely a place for prayer.

Its campus includes or has included a mosque, community facilities, Sunday school and **Huda Academy**.

The Encyclopedia reports that Huda Academy opened in **2001** across from the center and offered elementary education along with Arabic and Qur'an instruction.

ICLR has also engaged in:

- interfaith events;
- food distribution;
- charitable activity;
- disaster relief;
- public open houses.

A 2018 UA Little Rock profile documented food-bank activity and aid collected for victims of Hurricane Harvey.

## Narrative importance

This gives Chapter 21 a way to move beyond theology.

When the book asks what Sharia or Islam looks like on an ordinary Arkansas week, some of the answer may be worship—but some may also be school pickup, food boxes, disaster supplies, children's classes and conversations with Christian neighbors.

Interviews must determine what current members themselves consider religiously motivated rather than imposing the label “Sharia” on charitable activity from outside.

---

# 9. Jonesboro — 1983

The **Islamic Center of Jonesboro** was founded on **August 17, 1983**, according to the Encyclopedia of Arkansas.

The center has had a relationship with the Muslim Students Association at Arkansas State University.

A separate Encyclopedia immigration history states that Arkansas State University and the University of Arkansas participated with the Saudi government in a customs-official training program during the 1980s and that Saudi support helped construct a Jonesboro mosque for students, which later served a broader community.

## Research problem

We need to reconcile precisely:

- the August 17, 1983 founding date;
- the Saudi-sponsored construction chronology;
- student organization chronology;
- when the institution transitioned from primarily student-serving to wider local congregation.

This should become a local archive mini-project rather than a footnote.

---

# 10. Northwest Arkansas — 1994 onward

Northwest Arkansas Muslim growth reflects a different economic story.

The Encyclopedia connects Muslim migration to the corporate and industrial growth surrounding **Walmart, Tyson Foods and J.B. Hunt**, along with the broader internationalization of Benton and Washington counties.

The **Islamic Center of Northwest Arkansas** began in **1994 in Fayetteville**.

The **Bentonville Islamic Center** was established in **2006**.

This makes Northwest Arkansas useful for the book because Muslim Arkansas did not develop through a single pathway:

- Little Rock includes Black Muslim institution-building, universities, medicine and immigration;
- Jonesboro includes university/international student networks;
- Northwest Arkansas includes universities plus global corporate/economic migration.

## Caution

Do not reduce Northwest Arkansas Muslims to corporate professionals. The region's Muslim population is internally diverse and should be reported accordingly.

---

# 11. Pine Bluff

Pine Bluff appears in multiple strands of the Arkansas Muslim record, including earlier Muslim Association activity and later mosque/community presence.

Because Pine Bluff is historically central to Black Arkansas life and contains the University of Arkansas at Pine Bluff, it deserves substantially more research than existing statewide summaries provide.

## Questions

- Was early Pine Bluff Muslim organization primarily Black American, immigrant/international student, or mixed?
- What was the relationship to Little Rock's Black Muslim institutions?
- What was the relationship to UAPB?
- What organizations preceded the current mosque/community?
- Were there prison-ministry connections?

Do not fill these gaps by inference.

---

# 12. Fort Smith

Fort Smith developed a Muslim congregation serving western Arkansas.

Its history becomes nationally relevant because of a **2016 mosque vandalism case**.

The Encyclopedia of Arkansas reports that three men vandalized a Fort Smith mosque in October 2016 during a political environment containing openly anti-Muslim rhetoric; arrests followed in February 2017.

The exact offense, defendants, damage, prosecution, sentencing, restitution and subsequent community response should be reconstructed from court and local records before final manuscript use.

## Narrative significance

The episode gives the book a way to distinguish disagreement with Islam from attacks on Muslims' equal civil right to worship.

It also potentially provides a story of community response, forgiveness, reconciliation or continued trauma—but those dimensions must come from reporting, not assumption.

---

# 13. September 11, 2001 and Arkansas Muslims

Arkansas Muslim communities publicly condemned the September 11 attacks and increased outreach intended to explain their beliefs to neighbors.

The Encyclopedia records both condemnation of terrorism and continuing anti-Muslim sentiment in Arkansas afterward.

This is a major historical hinge for Part V and Part VI.

The book needs Arkansas-specific oral histories answering:

- What changed on September 12?
- Did children experience bullying?
- Did women wearing hijab change routines?
- Did mosques receive threats?
- Did Christian neighbors offer support?
- Did law enforcement relationships change?
- Did public invitations/open houses increase?
- Did Muslims feel pressure to continually condemn acts they had nothing to do with?

Do not presume the answers.

---

# 14. Madina Institute — 2016

The **Madina Institute** opened in western Little Rock in **2016**, founded by **Sophia Said**.

The Encyclopedia describes it as the first Arkansas mosque founded by a woman.

UA Little Rock's Congregations Study has documented its community life, including Ramadan observances, interfaith engagement and service activity.

A 2019 account describes an iftar where families, children, guests, prayer and food occupied the same evening—exactly the kind of ordinary lived religion the book needs to understand before making abstractions about “Sharia.”

## Important nuance

Madina Institute should not be used as representative of every Arkansas mosque. UA Little Rock reporting has described practices there as comparatively progressive in some respects.

That diversity is useful evidence in itself.

---

# 15. Security outside worship

A 2024 UA Little Rock Congregations Study account described police/security presence outside a Little Rock mosque during Ramadan amid heightened concern about anti-Muslim threats following the October 7, 2023 attacks and subsequent war.

The scene has strong narrative potential:

Inside: families, dates, prayer, guests, children and food.

Outside: armed security protecting the congregation's ability to worship.

But it must not be turned into melodrama.

The legal meaning is straightforward:

The state does not endorse Islam by protecting Muslims from violence. It protects citizens exercising religious liberty.

Potential line:

> Religious liberty sometimes looks like a constitutional clause. Sometimes it looks like a police car sitting outside while a family breaks its fast.

# 16. Arkansas Muslim geography

By the twenty-first century, Islamic institutions existed in multiple Arkansas population centers.

Documented nodes include:

- Little Rock/Central Arkansas;
- Pine Bluff;
- Jonesboro;
- Fayetteville;
- Bentonville/Northwest Arkansas;
- Fort Smith.

The Encyclopedia notes that many major Arkansas cities with colleges or universities developed Muslim communities/mosques.

## Book implication

There is no single Arkansas Muslim experience.

A Little Rock physician, a Black Muslim elder, a Jonesboro student, a Bentonville engineer, a Fort Smith business owner and a rural Muslim family may inhabit very different social worlds.

The interview sample must preserve that difference.

---

# 17. Arkansas Muslims and race

One of the most important reporting mistakes would be to treat “Muslim” as a race.

Arkansas Muslim communities include or have included:

- Black Americans;
- South Asians;
- Arabs/Middle Eastern immigrants and descendants;
- Africans;
- white converts;
- other converts;
- international students;
- second- and third-generation Americans;
- multiracial families.

The early Little Rock Nation of Islam story and later immigrant-institution story should therefore not be collapsed into one demographic narrative.

This also allows the book to explore whether racial divisions that characterize broader Arkansas society appear inside Muslim communities themselves.

That is an interview question, not a conclusion.

---

# 18. Arkansas Muslims and class/profession

Medicine and universities played major roles in Central Arkansas Muslim institution-building, while corporate globalization played an important role in Northwest Arkansas.

That can create an archive biased toward highly educated professionals.

The reporting sample must deliberately seek people outside elite professional networks:

- hourly workers;
- small-business owners;
- tradespeople;
- students;
- homemakers;
- retirees;
- rural Muslims;
- working-class Black Muslims;
- people with limited institutional involvement.

Otherwise the book will mistake the easiest people to interview for the community itself.

---

# 19. Interfaith Arkansas

Arkansas provides evidence not only of religious conflict but of religious cooperation.

Research targets include:

- Second Presbyterian Church and early Muslim meetings;
- mosque open houses;
- Jewish-Christian-Muslim dialogue;
- university interfaith programs;
- Unity in the Community initiatives;
- shared charitable work;
- clergy responses after attacks or vandalism;
- Christian defense of Muslim religious liberty;
- Muslim defense of other minorities' religious liberty.

The book should look specifically for **reciprocity**.

The strongest evidence is not merely Christians protecting Muslims. It is communities applying the same principle when another group is vulnerable.

---

# 20. Arkansas constitutional context

Pass 13 established the legal framework that makes this social history especially important.

Arkansas Constitution Article 2, Section 24 declares a natural and indefeasible right of worship according to conscience and says no human authority may control or interfere with the right of conscience or legally prefer one religious establishment, denomination or mode of worship over another.

Article 2, Section 25 directs protection of religious denominations in peaceable public worship.

Arkansas's modern statutory religious-liberty framework expressly uses the word **“mosque”** in defining protected religious organizations.

Therefore Arkansas Muslim history is not peripheral to the state's religious-liberty history.

Muslims are among the people to whom Arkansas's broad constitutional language necessarily applies.

---

# 21. Holt v. Hobbs belongs inside this history

The Arkansas Muslim story eventually reaches the United States Supreme Court through **Gregory Holt, also known as Abdul Maalik Muhammad**.

His request to maintain a half-inch beard for religious reasons became *Holt v. Hobbs*, resulting in a unanimous 2015 Supreme Court ruling against Arkansas under RLUIPA.

Pass 29 will reconstruct the case in full.

For this archive, the important point is historical:

Arkansas Muslim religious exercise was not merely a matter occurring inside mosques. It also existed inside Arkansas prisons, where institutional power and individual conscience collided directly.

---

# 22. The prison story needs its own Arkansas research stream

The history of Islam in Arkansas correctional institutions may be substantially larger than *Holt*.

Research targets:

- Muslim prison chaplaincy;
- Nation of Islam and Sunni prison populations;
- Ramadan accommodations;
- halal dietary disputes;
- prayer restrictions;
- grooming policies;
- Qur'an access;
- litigation before and after Holt;
- conversion in prison;
- post-release religious communities.

Do not assume prison Islam mirrors mosque Islam outside prison.

---

# 23. The “Sharia” question in Arkansas history

The archive currently contains no evidence that Arkansas Muslim institutions have operated as rival sovereign governments.

That does not mean every Arkansas Muslim has identical political or theological views.

The interviews should ask directly.

Historically, the visible institutional record instead includes:

- congregational worship;
- religious education;
- student organizations;
- family/community life;
- charity;
- interfaith activity;
- religious accommodation disputes;
- responses to discrimination and violence.

The book must not label all of those activities “Sharia” unless participants themselves explain the connection.

# 28. Questions the archive must allow interviews to change

The reporting must be capable of disproving or revising these preliminary hypotheses:

- Was 1969 actually the first Arkansas mosque, or merely the earliest currently documented?
- Was the 10th/Schiller congregation institutionally continuous with today's New Africa?
- How did Nation of Islam members transition, if at all, after national changes following Elijah Muhammad's death?
- Was Masjid Ameen Zakariah the central late-1970s Muslim institution described in current recollections?
- Did Black and immigrant Muslims worship together or mostly separately?
- What exactly did Second Presbyterian provide in 1985?
- Who initiated that relationship—the church, Muslim community or interfaith network?
- Did university prayer networks precede formal mosques in each city?
- How important were UAMS physicians compared with other professions?
- How did 9/11 actually alter daily Arkansas Muslim life?
- How much internal theological/political disagreement exists among Arkansas mosques?
- How do former Muslims remember these communities?

If interviews contradict the archive, revise the archive.

# 33. Final finding

Arkansas Muslim history is older, more local and more internally diverse than the modern political argument suggests.

It includes Black Arkansans building Muslim institutions in Little Rock by 1969.

It includes international students finding places to pray.

It includes physicians who came for medical training and stayed to raise families.

It includes Christians and Muslims experimenting with interfaith relationships.

It includes mosques in Jonesboro, Fayetteville, Bentonville, Fort Smith, Pine Bluff and Little Rock.

It includes vandalism and security fears.

It includes charity, schools, children, Ramadan meals and ordinary congregational life.

And it includes an Arkansas prisoner whose request for half an inch of beard forced the United States Supreme Court to tell Arkansas, unanimously, that religious liberty protected him too.

The Arkansas section of this book therefore does not need to import a hypothetical Muslim.

The Muslim is already here.

The historical question is no longer whether Arkansas's religious-liberty language *could* include Muslims.

The state has already had decades to show us what happens when it must.
