# Research Pass Five — The Founding-Era “Muslim President” Debate

## Executive finding

The strongest surviving evidence shows that Americans explicitly understood before ratification that Article VI’s ban on religious tests could permit Muslims to hold federal office, including the presidency. Opponents raised this as a danger. Defenders did not answer by claiming Muslims would be legally barred. They defended the constitutional principle that religious identity should not be used as a qualification for office.

The most important North Carolina ratification debate occurred on July 30, 1788.

## Constitutional text

Article VI, Clause 3 provides that no religious test shall ever be required as a qualification to any office or public trust under the United States.

Evidence grade: A — direct constitutional text.

## Henry Abbot’s objection

Henry Abbot warned that because no religious test was required, “pagans, deists, and Mahometans might obtain offices among us.” He presented this as a serious objection to the Constitution and asked defenders to explain how religious liberty would be protected without such tests.

Evidence grade: A — recorded ratification convention debate.

## James Iredell’s answer

James Iredell did not deny that Muslims could become officeholders. He argued that excluding any class of people on religious grounds would abandon the very principle of religious freedom and would open the door to persecution. He described Article VI as one of the strongest proofs that the framers intended “general religious liberty in America.” He later said the provision secured universal religious liberty by putting all sects on a level.

He also explicitly addressed the objection that “pagans and Mahometans may be admitted into offices” and asked how any group could be excluded without taking away the principle of religious freedom.

Evidence grade: A — recorded ratification convention debate.

## William Lancaster and the presidency

William Lancaster made the presidential issue unmistakable. Speaking about the future of the nation, he said: “Papists may occupy that chair, and Mahometans may take it. I see nothing against it.”

His point was hostile to the absence of a religious test: he believed the Constitution should contain a disqualification. But his statement is extremely important because it shows that delegates understood the presidency itself to be open, as a matter of constitutional text, to Catholics and Muslims.

Evidence grade: A — recorded ratification convention debate.

## Governor Samuel Johnston’s answer

Governor Samuel Johnston acknowledged that Jews, Muslims, pagans, and others might be elected to high federal office. He said a Muslim could become president in two broad circumstances: if the religious complexion of the country itself changed, or if a person of a different religion earned the confidence and esteem of the American people through good conduct and virtue.

This is one of the most important pieces of evidence in the entire project because Johnston did not claim a Muslim presidency was unconstitutional. He argued that political selection, not religious disqualification, would decide the question.

Evidence grade: A — recorded ratification convention debate.

## Richard Dobbs Spaight

Spaight argued that no sect was preferred, every person had a right to worship according to conscience, no test was required, and people of equal capacity and integrity were equally eligible to office. He rejected the idea that Congress had power to establish religious tribunals or otherwise intervene in religion.

Evidence grade: A — recorded ratification convention debate.

## Broader context

Article VI was controversial because many states still imposed religious qualifications for office. The federal Constitution departed from that tradition by prohibiting religious tests for federal office. Ratification debates in multiple states reflected anxiety that non-Christians, including deists, atheists, Catholics, Jews, and Muslims, could therefore hold federal power.

Evidence grade: A/B — constitutional text plus multiple ratification records and later constitutional scholarship.

## Narrative significance

The most defensible formulation is not: “The founders wanted a Muslim president.”

The defensible formulation is stronger historically:

**Americans explicitly warned in 1788 that the Constitution could allow a Muslim to become president. The Constitution was defended and ultimately ratified without adding a Christian test.**

This means modern Americans did not discover only recently that Muslims are constitutionally eligible for federal office. That possibility was openly discussed during ratification.

The critical exchange is:

1. Opponents: Without a religious test, Muslims may hold office and even the presidency.
2. Defenders: Yes, the structure permits people of different faiths to hold office; excluding them would compromise religious liberty.
3. Constitutional result: Article VI remained unchanged.

## Important caution

North Carolina did not ratify the Constitution at the July–August 1788 Hillsborough Convention. It initially declined to ratify and later ratified at Fayetteville on November 21, 1789, after the new federal government had begun operating and amendments had been proposed. Therefore the narrative should not falsely say North Carolina itself ratified immediately after this debate.

The broader national point remains valid: Article VI was already part of the Constitution being ratified by the states, and the Muslim-officeholder objection was known and debated.

## Key lines for later narrative

“America did not discover in the twenty-first century that a Muslim could constitutionally become president. Americans argued about it in 1788.”

“The objection was not hidden: opponents warned that Muslims could take the presidential chair. The constitutional answer was not to add a Christian test.”

“The Muslim president was not a loophole nobody noticed. He was a possibility people named out loud.”

“Article VI did something astonishing for its time: it made a citizen’s religion irrelevant as a federal qualification for office.”
