# Pass 25 — Thomas Jefferson's Qur'an: Forensic Evidence File

## Purpose

This pass isolates one of the most rhetorically powerful—and therefore most dangerous—pieces of evidence in the project: Thomas Jefferson's ownership of an English translation of the Qur'an.

The goal is not to prove that Jefferson admired Islam, derived American constitutionalism from Islamic law, or used the Qur'an to design the Constitution. The surviving evidence does not establish those propositions.

The goal is to determine, as precisely as the record allows:

1. what Qur'an Jefferson owned;
2. when he acquired it;
3. what he was studying at the time;
4. what the edition contained;
5. what evidence exists that he read or annotated it;
6. what Jefferson later wrote about Muslims and religious liberty;
7. what his diplomatic encounters with Muslim governments show;
8. what they do not show;
9. how modern writers have transformed ownership of the Qur'an into claims of constitutional influence;
10. what claims the book can safely make.

The forensic rule is simple:

> Possession is evidence of possession. Reading is evidence of reading only when independently supportable. Exposure is not borrowing. Resemblance is not influence.

---

# 1. The artifact

Thomas Jefferson owned a two-volume English translation of the Qur'an by George Sale, commonly titled *The Koran, Commonly Called the Alcoran of Mohammed*, first published in London in 1734.

The Library of Congress preserves Jefferson's copy as part of the Jefferson library collection.

The copy has become symbolically important in modern American political life, including its use in congressional swearing-in ceremonies by Muslim members of Congress.

That modern symbolic use must be distinguished from evidence about Jefferson's eighteenth-century purposes.

## Claim grade

**A — Direct documentary/artifact evidence:** Jefferson owned Sale's Qur'an.

---

# 2. Acquisition date

Library of Congress scholarship places Jefferson's purchase of the Qur'an in **1765**, when Jefferson was a young law student.

This chronology matters.

Jefferson was not yet drafting the Declaration of Independence. He was not a delegate to the Constitutional Convention. The United States did not yet exist.

He was engaged in an intensive program of legal and intellectual study.

The timing therefore supports a modest but meaningful proposition:

> Jefferson encountered the Qur'an as a serious book while he was studying law and political thought as a young man.

It does **not** prove:

> Jefferson studied the Qur'an in order to borrow Islamic legal principles for American government.

That second proposition requires evidence that has not been located.

## Claim grade

**A/B:** acquisition during Jefferson's legal-study period is strongly documented.

**E:** specific constitutional borrowing based solely on purchase timing.

---

# 3. George Sale's translation

Sale's 1734 English translation was an important eighteenth-century European presentation of the Qur'an.

It was not merely Qur'anic text stripped of context. Sale supplied a substantial preliminary discourse discussing Arabia, Muhammad, Islamic beliefs, religious institutions, legal customs, sects, and related material.

This matters because Jefferson's artifact potentially exposed a reader not only to translated Qur'anic passages but to an eighteenth-century European account of Islam as a religious and legal civilization.

However, the existence of that material in Jefferson's books does not establish which portions he read carefully, accepted, rejected, remembered, or used.

## Safe formulation

> Jefferson's edition gave him access to considerably more than isolated Qur'anic verses. Sale's translation presented Islam within an extended eighteenth-century discussion of Islamic religion, history, institutions and law.

## Unsafe formulation

> Jefferson studied Islamic jurisprudence through Sale and incorporated it into American constitutional design.

No evidentiary bridge currently supports that sentence.

---

# 4. Jefferson's annotations

The surviving Jefferson copy does **not** provide the kind of extensive substantive marginalia that would allow us to reconstruct a Jeffersonian reading of the Qur'an.

Library of Congress descriptions have noted ownership markings/initials associated with Jefferson, but the artifact does not furnish a developed set of Jefferson annotations demonstrating that he highlighted constitutional concepts, religious-liberty verses, criminal law, consultation, or other Islamic doctrines later reflected in American government.

This negative evidence is extremely important.

If the book is going to feature Jefferson's Qur'an prominently, readers must be told that we cannot open Jefferson's copy and find a trail of notes saying:

- religious liberty;
- separation of powers;
- due process;
- consultation;
- equality;
- use this in Virginia;
- use this in the Constitution.

That trail does not exist in the surviving evidence.

## Book rule

Never imply that Jefferson's Qur'an contains annotations proving Islamic constitutional influence unless new primary evidence is discovered.

---

# 5. Jefferson was not the author of the Constitution

This correction must accompany any Jefferson/Qur'an constitutional discussion.

Jefferson did **not** attend the 1787 Constitutional Convention.

He was serving as the United States minister in France.

He corresponded with American political leaders and later became deeply important to American constitutional development, but saying “Jefferson wrote the Constitution” is simply false.

Likewise, even if evidence someday demonstrated that Jefferson had borrowed an idea from Islamic law, another evidentiary step would still be necessary to show that the idea entered the federal Constitution through him.

## Forbidden claim

> Jefferson used his Qur'an to write the Constitution.

This fails both the documentary and chronological tests.

---

# 6. The stronger Jefferson evidence is not the Qur'an

One of the most important discoveries in the project is that Jefferson's strongest relevance to Muslims and American religious liberty does **not** depend on his Qur'an at all.

In his 1776 notes drawing from John Locke and Anthony Ashley Cooper, Earl of Shaftesbury, Jefferson recorded the proposition that civil rights should not be denied because a person was pagan, “Mahamedan,” or Jewish.

The significance is direct.

Muslims were explicitly within the conceptual universe of Jefferson's thinking about civil religious liberty.

This evidence is much stronger than an inference drawn from a book on his shelf.

## Narrative consequence

The chapter should not say:

> Jefferson owned a Qur'an, therefore he believed Muslims deserved civil rights.

It can instead establish independently:

> Jefferson explicitly recorded an argument that Muslims should not be excluded from civil rights because of religion.

Only after that should the Qur'an appear.

This reversal makes the argument both stronger and more honest.

---

# 7. Locke as the documented intellectual bridge

Jefferson's 1776 notes provide a direct documentary bridge to Locke's religious-liberty reasoning.

Locke's arguments about toleration famously discussed Muslims (“Mahometans”) within the problem of civil religious liberty.

This gives the project an A-grade intellectual connection:

**Lockean religious-liberty thought → Jefferson's documented notes concerning Muslim civil rights.**

It does **not** give us:

**Islamic law → Locke → Jefferson → Constitution.**

That longer chain would require independent evidence at each link.

Pass 8 already established the proper transmission rule: plausible intellectual contact must never be silently promoted into documented borrowing.

---

# 8. Virginia religious liberty

Jefferson's Virginia religious-liberty work provides another independent evidentiary foundation.

His *Bill for Establishing Religious Freedom* argued that religious opinions should neither diminish nor enlarge a person's civil capacities.

The statute was enacted in 1786 through Madison's legislative efforts while Jefferson was abroad.

Jefferson later recalled that an effort to insert “Jesus Christ” into the bill's preamble was rejected, and he interpreted that rejection as evidence that the protection was intended to include the Jew, Gentile, Christian, “Mahometan,” Hindu, and infidel.

## Critical caveat

The Jesus-Christ amendment episode survives as Jefferson's later recollection. The project has not located a legislative journal entry independently documenting the proposed amendment in the form Jefferson remembered it.

Therefore write:

> Jefferson later recalled...

Do not write:

> The Virginia legislative journal proves...

unless additional primary documentation is located.

---

# 9. The Virginia principle is jurisdictional

Jefferson's mature religious-liberty reasoning is more useful to this book than speculation about Islamic influence.

The principle can be described as jurisdictional:

Civil government possesses authority over civil matters. It does not acquire ownership of the human mind merely because a majority has religious convictions.

This produces one of the book's recurring distinctions:

> Toleration says the majority will permit your religion.
>
> Religious liberty asks why the majority believes it owned your conscience in the first place.

Jefferson's relevance to the Muslim is therefore not that Jefferson became a student of Islamic theology.

It is that his conception of civil religious liberty became broad enough to contemplate a person whose theology he did not share.

---

# 10. Jefferson's later encounters with Muslim governments

Jefferson's relationship with the Islamic world was not confined to books.

As an American diplomat and later national leader, he confronted the Barbary powers of North Africa.

In 1786 Jefferson and John Adams met with Sidi Haji Abdrahaman, the Tripolitan ambassador in London, seeking to understand the basis of conflict and the demands being made upon American shipping.

Jefferson and Adams reported the ambassador as grounding Tripoli's claims in religious authority associated with the Qur'an and the Prophet, including claims concerning war against nations that did not acknowledge Muslim authority and the enslavement of captives.

This is important evidence—but it must be handled correctly.

It is evidence of:

- Jefferson's direct diplomatic encounter with a representative of a Muslim government;
- American exposure to religious justifications for Barbary warfare;
- a real eighteenth-century conflict involving Muslim political powers;
- Jefferson's willingness to confront coercion and piracy.

It is not neutral evidence of what “Islam teaches” universally.

It is Jefferson and Adams's report of what one diplomatic representative said in a particular geopolitical conflict.

---

# 11. Jefferson did not need to think Islam harmless

This is one of the strongest conceptual findings.

The evidence allows Jefferson simultaneously to:

- encounter Islam intellectually;
- support Muslim civil rights as a matter of religious liberty;
- confront Muslim governments militarily/diplomatically;
- hear disturbing religious justifications for violence or enslavement;
- reject religious coercion.

Those positions are not contradictory.

They expose a modern false choice.

A person does not have to decide that every Islamic doctrine or Muslim government is benign before recognizing the civil rights of an individual Muslim.

Potential book line:

> Jefferson did not have to believe every interpretation of Islam was harmless before believing that a Muslim could possess civil rights.

This line is much more defensible than “Jefferson loved Islam.”

---

# 12. Barbary conflict versus Muslim citizenship

The early republic fought or negotiated with Muslim-majority governments without translating foreign conflict into a federal religious disqualification for Muslims.

This distinction becomes especially powerful when placed beside the North Carolina ratification debate.

Americans could know that conflict with Muslim governments existed and still debate Muslims as potential federal officeholders under Article VI.

Potential formulation:

> The early republic could fight a Muslim government without declaring war on Muslim citizenship.

This is a structural claim, not a claim that early Americans were free of anti-Muslim prejudice.

---

# 13. Jefferson's Qur'an and the Declaration of Independence

There is no reliable evidence presently identified demonstrating that Jefferson borrowed the Declaration's natural-rights language from the Qur'an.

Jefferson's intellectual environment included Locke, classical authors, Enlightenment thinkers, English legal traditions, colonial political experience, and numerous other documented sources.

Similarities between Qur'anic justice language and American natural-rights rhetoric are comparative parallels unless a documentary bridge is established.

## Forbidden inference

Qur'an contains justice/equality concept + Jefferson owned Qur'an + Declaration contains equality = Qur'an influenced Declaration.

That is not historical proof.

---

# 14. Jefferson's Qur'an and the Virginia Statute

Likewise, the fact that Jefferson owned a Qur'an before drafting his religious-liberty legislation does not establish that Qur'anic religious-liberty passages caused or shaped the statute.

There are direct documented Western intellectual sources for Jefferson's religious-liberty reasoning.

A Qur'anic parallel may be intellectually interesting.

It remains a parallel unless documentary evidence connects the two.

---

# 15. Jefferson's Qur'an and the Constitution

This is the weakest popular claim.

Problems:

1. Jefferson did not attend the Constitutional Convention.
2. No surviving Jefferson annotation demonstrates constitutional borrowing from the Qur'an.
3. No identified Convention record shows delegates citing Jefferson's Qur'an as a constitutional source.
4. No identified Article VI drafting record establishes Islamic-law parentage.
5. Article VI can be explained through the American struggle over religious tests without positing Qur'anic influence.
6. The North Carolina Muslim-office debate demonstrates Muslim applicability, not Islamic authorship.

## Claim grade

**E — Do not publish as historical influence without new evidence.**

---

# 16. The genuinely remarkable story

Removing the unsupported influence claim does not weaken the story.

It improves it.

The remarkable facts are these:

- a future author of the Declaration bought a Qur'an as a young law student;
- he lived in an intellectual world in which Islam was a subject of law, religion, empire, commerce, diplomacy and controversy;
- he explicitly recorded Muslim civil-rights reasoning in 1776;
- he authored one of America's foundational religious-liberty texts;
- he later remembered its universality as encompassing Muslims;
- he confronted Muslim governments as an American diplomat and statesman;
- his political world could condemn Barbary coercion without requiring a Christian identity test for federal citizenship or office.

That story requires no secret Islamic Constitution.

---

# 17. Modern symbolic life of Jefferson's Qur'an

Jefferson's Qur'an later acquired a modern American symbolic role because Muslim public officials have used the Library of Congress copy or a facsimile/related ceremonial arrangement in congressional oath ceremonies.

The most famous modern association is with Representative Keith Ellison, the first Muslim elected to the United States Congress, who used Jefferson's Qur'an in a private ceremonial reenactment associated with his swearing-in in 2007.

Important constitutional clarification:

Members of Congress take the official oath in the constitutionally prescribed institutional process; the later photo/ceremonial reenactment with a chosen religious text is not what legally creates the officeholder's authority.

The symbolism was nevertheless extraordinary:

A Qur'an owned by the man who wrote the Declaration became an object used by a Muslim American entering federal office under a Constitution that prohibits religious tests.

This is legitimate narrative symbolism.

It is not proof that Jefferson bought the Qur'an in anticipation of Muslim members of Congress.

---

# 18. Why the artifact became politically contested

Modern controversy surrounding Muslim officeholders has sometimes revived precisely the founding-era anxiety documented in the North Carolina debates: whether a Muslim can fully belong within American public authority.

Jefferson's Qur'an became symbolically useful because it disrupts the idea that Islam suddenly arrived in America's political consciousness in the twenty-first century.

But symbolism should not be asked to carry more historical weight than it can bear.

The artifact proves Jefferson possessed the Qur'an.

The founding-era documentary record separately proves Muslims were contemplated in debates about civil religious liberty and public office.

The combination is powerful because the two facts are independently true—not because one proves the other.

---

# 19. Modern claim audit

## Claim: “Jefferson owned a Qur'an.”

**Grade A.** Safe.

## Claim: “Jefferson bought his Qur'an while studying law.”

**Grade A/B.** Safe with proper sourcing and chronology.

## Claim: “Jefferson studied Islamic law.”

**Grade C at best without more specific evidence.** His edition exposed him to discussion of Islamic institutions/law, and his law-study context is suggestive, but “studied Islamic law” can imply more sustained engagement than the surviving record proves.

Prefer:

> Jefferson acquired a Qur'an containing extensive contextual material about Islam while engaged in legal study.

## Claim: “Jefferson admired Islam.”

**Unsupported as a broad claim.** His record is mixed and should not be reduced to admiration or hostility.

## Claim: “Jefferson believed Muslims had civil rights.”

**Grade A**, when grounded in his documented notes/religious-liberty reasoning and properly contextualized.

## Claim: “Jefferson's Qur'an influenced the Declaration.”

**Grade E unless new documentary evidence emerges.**

## Claim: “Jefferson's Qur'an influenced the Constitution.”

**Grade E.** Jefferson was not at the Convention; no documentary bridge established.

## Claim: “Jefferson used Sharia to build America.”

**Grade E.** Do not publish.

## Claim: “Islam was within Jefferson's intellectual and political world.”

**Grade A/B.** Strong.

## Claim: “Jefferson could support Muslim civil liberty while opposing Muslim governments' coercive conduct.”

**Grade A/B as synthesis of documented episodes.** Strong if carefully written.

# 20. Forensic source hierarchy for Chapter 11

When drafting Chapter 11, evidence should appear in this order of authority:

### Tier 1 — Jefferson's own writings

- 1776 notes involving Locke/Shaftesbury and Muslim civil rights;
- Virginia religious-liberty texts;
- correspondence;
- diplomatic reports;
- later autobiographical recollection, clearly labeled as recollection.

### Tier 2 — Surviving Jefferson artifact/catalog evidence

- Jefferson's Sale Qur'an;
- ownership/provenance records;
- Library of Congress bibliographic and curatorial documentation.

### Tier 3 — Contemporary governmental/diplomatic records

- Barbary correspondence and reports;
- treaty material;
- congressional/state records where relevant.

### Tier 4 — Modern specialist scholarship

Use to interpret context, not replace primary evidence.

### Tier 5 — Modern popular claims

Use primarily as objects to investigate, not as evidence of eighteenth-century fact.

# 21. Chapter 11 narrative architecture

The strongest chapter sequence is now clear.

## Scene 1 — Do not begin with the Qur'an

Begin with Jefferson writing/recording the startling 1776 idea that a “Mahamedan” should not be excluded from civil rights because of religion.

The reader expects Jefferson's Muslim relevance to come from a mysterious Qur'an.

Instead it comes first from an explicit civil-rights proposition.

## Scene 2 — Ask where this came from

Show Locke and the documented intellectual lineage.

Do not manufacture an Islamic lineage.

## Scene 3 — Then reveal the Qur'an

Jefferson had already purchased Sale's Qur'an as a young law student.

Now the artifact becomes intriguing rather than load-bearing.

## Scene 4 — Examine it like evidence

What edition? What did it contain? What did Jefferson mark? What did he not mark?

Tell the reader openly:

> We went looking for the receipt—the note in the margin, the letter, the citation that would let us say Jefferson borrowed American religious liberty from Islam. We did not find it.

That failed hypothesis builds trust.

## Scene 5 — Virginia

Move to Jefferson's actual religious-liberty work.

The evidence becomes stronger again.

## Scene 6 — Barbary contradiction

Jefferson later confronts Muslim governments and disturbing religious claims.

The chapter refuses hagiography.

## Scene 7 — The conclusion

The point was never that Jefferson had to admire Islam.

The point was that civil liberty could encompass a Muslim without Jefferson first certifying Islam as harmless or true.

# 22. Potential high-value lines

These are interpretive/narrative lines, not quotations from Jefferson.

> Jefferson's Qur'an is real. The secret Islamic Constitution is not.

> The most important evidence connecting Jefferson to Muslim religious liberty is not the Qur'an on his shelf. It is the Muslim in his sentence.

> We went looking for the receipt. We found the book. We found the intellectual contact. We found explicit Muslim civil-rights language. What we did not find was evidence that Jefferson copied Islamic law into the Constitution.

> Jefferson did not have to believe Islam was true before believing that a Muslim could possess civil rights.

> Religious liberty becomes meaningful precisely when theological agreement runs out.

> A book on Jefferson's shelf cannot prove constitutional borrowing. A Muslim explicitly included in a civil-rights argument proves something different—and more important.

> The early republic could fight a Muslim government without declaring war on Muslim citizenship.

# 23. Adverse evidence that must remain in the chapter

The chapter loses credibility if it omits:

- Jefferson's Barbary conflict;
- Jefferson/Adams's report of the Tripolitan ambassador's religious justification;
- hostile or stereotyped eighteenth-century language where relevant;
- the absence of substantive Qur'an annotations demonstrating borrowing;
- Jefferson's absence from the Constitutional Convention;
- the direct Locke/European intellectual lineage;
- uncertainty surrounding precisely how deeply Jefferson read Sale;
- the later-recollection status of the Jesus-Christ amendment story.

The adverse evidence is not a problem to solve.

It is part of the story.

---

# 24. Claims prohibited in manuscript drafting

Unless new primary evidence is discovered, do not write:

- Jefferson wrote the Constitution.
- Jefferson used the Qur'an to write the Constitution.
- Jefferson based the First Amendment on the Qur'an.
- Jefferson based the Virginia Statute on Qur'an 2:256.
- Jefferson learned separation of church and state from Islam.
- Jefferson was an admirer of Islam, without heavy qualification.
- Jefferson believed Sharia should influence American civil law.
- Jefferson's Qur'an contains annotations proving constitutional borrowing.
- the founders secretly used Islamic law.
- Jefferson's possession of a Qur'an proves America was founded on Sharia.

---

# 25. Claims safe for manuscript drafting

With appropriate citations/context:

- Jefferson owned George Sale's English translation of the Qur'an.
- He acquired it as a young man during his period of legal study.
- Sale's edition contained substantial contextual discussion of Islam.
- the surviving copy does not provide a documentary trail proving constitutional borrowing.
- Jefferson explicitly recorded Muslim-inclusive civil-rights reasoning in 1776.
- Jefferson's Virginia religious-liberty project sought to separate religious opinion from civil capacity.
- Jefferson later recalled the Virginia statute's universality as encompassing Muslims among other believers/nonbelievers.
- Jefferson encountered Muslim governments directly through American diplomacy.
- the Barbary conflicts demonstrate that support for Muslim civil liberty did not require believing every Muslim political actor or interpretation harmless.
- Jefferson was in France during the Constitutional Convention.
- Muslim eligibility for federal office is better established through Article VI and ratification evidence than through Jefferson's Qur'an.

# 27. Final finding

The forensic examination changes the rhetorical center of the Jefferson story.

The tempting version is:

> Thomas Jefferson owned a Qur'an, and maybe America secretly borrowed from it.

The evidence supports a more consequential story:

> Thomas Jefferson lived in a world where Islam was intellectually present, owned and encountered the Qur'an as a young law student, explicitly contemplated Muslims within civil religious liberty, helped articulate a sweeping jurisdictional protection for conscience, and later confronted Muslim governments without converting geopolitical conflict into a principle of Muslim civil exclusion.

The Qur'an matters.

But it matters as evidence that Islam was inside Jefferson's intellectual world—not as a magic key proving that Sharia authored American constitutionalism.

The strongest evidence is not the Muslim book Jefferson owned.

It is the Muslim citizen his theory of liberty could encompass.
