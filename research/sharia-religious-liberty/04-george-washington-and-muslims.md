# Research Pass Four: George Washington and Muslims

## Core finding
George Washington supplies unusually direct evidence that Muslims were not outside his conception of American religious liberty. In private correspondence he explicitly named “Mahometans” alongside Jews, Christians, and atheists when discussing whom he was willing to employ. In the Virginia religious-assessment debate he again named Jews and “Mahomitans” when considering relief from a proposed Christian religious tax. As president, he articulated a broader doctrine that liberty of conscience and citizenship were inherent rights, not favors granted by a religious majority.

## 1. Washington explicitly included Muslims in ordinary civic and economic life
On March 24, 1784, Washington wrote to Tench Tilghman seeking skilled workers. He said he would not confine the search by nationality or creed: if they were good workers, they might come from Asia, Africa, or Europe; they might be “Mahometans, Jews, or Christian of any Sect—or they may be Athiests.”

This is significant because it is not an abstract theological statement. Washington is describing who may work for him and what qualifications matter. His stated criteria are competence and character, not religion.

Primary source: George Washington to Tench Tilghman, 24 March 1784, Founders Online, National Archives.

## 2. Washington explicitly contemplated Muslims in Virginia’s religious-assessment debate
On October 3, 1785, Washington wrote to George Mason about the proposed Virginia religious assessment. He said no man’s sentiments were more opposed to restraint upon religious principles than his own. While he was not as doctrinally opposed to the assessment as Madison, he wrote that relief should be available to those who declared themselves Jews, “Mahomitans,” or otherwise.

This matters because Muslims appear by name inside the practical policy debate over whether public support of Christianity should burden non-Christians.

Primary source: George Washington to George Mason, 3 October 1785, Founders Online, National Archives.

## 3. Washington’s religious-liberty doctrine rejected mere toleration
As president, Washington repeatedly described religious liberty as a right rather than an indulgence.

In his 1790 response to the Hebrew Congregation at Newport, he declared that all possess alike liberty of conscience and immunities of citizenship, and rejected the idea that religious minorities exercise rights only through the indulgence of a dominant class. He wrote that the government of the United States gives “to bigotry no sanction, to persecution no assistance.”

This is one of the clearest presidential statements of the difference between toleration and equal religious citizenship.

Primary sources:
- George Washington to the Hebrew Congregation in Newport, Rhode Island, 1790, Founders Online.
- Library of Congress, Today in History, August 17.

## 4. Washington’s standard for citizenship was conduct, not theology
In May 1789, writing to the United Baptist Churches of Virginia, Washington said he would never have signed the Constitution if he believed it endangered religious rights. He stated that every man, so long as he conducted himself as a good citizen, ought to be protected in worship according to the dictates of conscience.

In October 1789, writing to the Society of Quakers, he similarly stated that government exists in part to protect the persons and consciences of men from oppression, and that while people fulfill their social duties, society can properly demand no more from them regarding religion.

These statements make conduct the civil test, not creed.

Primary sources:
- George Washington to the United Baptist Churches of Virginia, May 1789, Founders Online.
- George Washington to the Society of Quakers, 13 October 1789, Founders Online.

## 5. What we can responsibly claim
Strong claim: Washington explicitly treated Muslims as people who could participate in ordinary American economic life and as a religious category that should not be forced to subsidize Christianity without relief.

Strong claim: Washington’s presidential theory of religious liberty treated liberty of conscience and citizenship as rights rather than permissions granted by a Christian majority.

Moderate inference: Muslims logically fit inside Washington’s broader presidential doctrine because he expressly named Muslims in earlier religious-liberty contexts and later framed citizenship by conduct rather than theology.

Do not overclaim: there is not yet evidence here that Washington developed a systematic theory of Islam or Sharia, or that Islamic law influenced his political philosophy.

## 6. Narrative significance for the book
Washington is powerful because he broadens the story beyond Jefferson and Madison. The reader can no longer dismiss Muslim inclusion as the eccentricity of one founder.

We now have:
- Jefferson explicitly including “Mahamedans” in civil-rights reasoning.
- Madison building equal rights of conscience into Virginia and federal constitutional machinery.
- Washington explicitly naming Muslims in employment and religious-assessment contexts, then as president defining religious liberty as an inherent right of citizenship.

A useful narrative line:

“Washington did not ask whether a worker was Christian before asking whether he was competent. He did not imagine religious liberty as a favor Christians extended to outsiders. By the time he became president, he was saying the opposite: conscience was a right, citizenship was equal, and bigotry had no legitimate sanction from the government.”

## 7. Key interpretive line
Religious liberty under Washington was not: “Christians may graciously permit Muslims to live among them.”

It was closer to: “Government has no business making religious identity the test of whether a peaceful citizen belongs.”
