# Pass 22 — Founding-Era Religious Test Atlas, 1776–1791

## Purpose

This pass tests one of the book's most important historical propositions:

> Americans in the founding era knew how to write explicit religious qualifications when they wanted them.

The question is not whether every state imposed the same rule. They did not. The founding period contained a wide spectrum: formal Protestant establishments, Christian or Protestant office tests, broad conscience clauses, Christian-only equal-protection language, exclusions of clergy from office, and relatively religion-neutral systems.

That variation matters. It means the federal Constitution's Article VI was drafted in a political world where multiple religious-qualification models were already visible.

This atlas focuses on the thirteen original states plus Vermont, which operated under its own constitution before joining the Union in 1791.

The relevant categories are:

- religious establishment;
- compelled support/public religious funding;
- free exercise/conscience protection;
- religious test for civil office;
- religious qualification for legislators/governor;
- clergy disqualification;
- religious qualification for voting;
- major changes between 1776 and 1791.

---

# Executive finding

The evidence strongly supports the narrow claim:

> Founding-era Americans unmistakably knew how to put religious qualifications into constitutional text.

They did so in Delaware, Pennsylvania, Maryland, North Carolina, South Carolina, Georgia, Massachusetts, New Hampshire, Vermont, and New Jersey in differing forms. Connecticut maintained a Congregational establishment through statute and its colonial charter system. By contrast, Virginia, New York, and Rhode Island offered substantially broader conscience/free-exercise frameworks without comparable Christian office tests in their principal governing texts of the period.

Several states changed during the founding era itself. Georgia dropped its Protestant legislative qualification in 1789. South Carolina's 1790 constitution ended its explicit Protestant establishment and religious voting/office tests. Pennsylvania's 1790 constitution replaced its 1776 scripture-acknowledgment test with a broader theistic qualification. This demonstrates that religious qualification was an active constitutional design question, not an invisible cultural assumption.

The federal Constitution then said:

> “no religious Test shall ever be required as a Qualification to any Office or public Trust under the United States.”

The safest historical conclusion is not that all founders intended modern religious equality. It is that the federal no-test rule was adopted against a background in which explicit religious tests were familiar, operational, and constitutionally expressible.

# 1. NEW HAMPSHIRE

## 1776

New Hampshire's temporary 1776 constitution was principally a wartime governmental framework and contained no developed bill of rights or explicit religious office-test regime.

## 1784 Constitution

New Hampshire's 1784 constitution combined strong conscience language with explicit Protestant political qualifications.

Its Bill of Rights treated rights of conscience as unalienable and protected worship according to conscience. At the same time, it authorized support for “public protestant teachers of piety, religion and morality” and declared that Christian denominations behaving peacefully would receive equal legal protection.

The constitution required that:

- senators be “of the protestant religion”;
- members of the House be “of the protestant religion”;
- the President/Governor be “of the Protestant religion.”

### Classification

- Free exercise/conscience: **Yes**
- Public Protestant support: **Yes**
- Protestant office qualification: **Yes**
- Full religion-neutral civil equality: **No**

### Book significance

New Hampshire is one of the clearest examples that strong conscience language and explicit Protestant political qualification could coexist in the same constitution.

---

# 2. MASSACHUSETTS

## 1780 Constitution

Massachusetts created one of the founding era's strongest examples of public Protestant establishment combined with protections for individual conscience.

Article II protected worship according to conscience, subject to public peace.

Article III authorized towns and religious societies to provide financially for public worship and required support for “public Protestant teachers of piety, religion and morality” where not otherwise voluntarily provided.

The original constitution also required major officeholders and legislators to make a declaration that they believed the Christian religion and were firmly persuaded of its truth.

The religious declaration applied to the governor, lieutenant governor, councillors, senators, and representatives.

### Classification

- Free exercise/conscience: **Yes**
- Public Protestant establishment/support: **Yes**
- Christian office declaration: **Yes**
- Religion-neutral federal-style no-test rule: **No**

### Book significance

Massachusetts is especially important because it demolishes any suggestion that founding-era constitution writers did not know how to state Christianity explicitly as a qualification. They did exactly that.

---

# 3. RHODE ISLAND

## Governing framework

Rhode Island continued under its 1663 royal charter long after independence rather than adopting a new state constitution during the founding period.

The charter was unusually broad for its era, promising that persons would not be molested for differences in religious opinion so long as they did not disturb civil peace and describing the colony as a “lively experiment” in civil government with religious liberty.

Rhode Island's 1790 ratification declaration stated that religion could be directed only by reason and conviction, not force or violence, and that all men possessed an equal, natural, unalienable right to free exercise; it also opposed legal preference or establishment of one religious sect over another.

### Classification

- Broad conscience/free exercise: **Yes**
- Formal established denomination during the relevant state constitutional period: **No comparable founding-state establishment**
- Christian/Protestant office test in principal governing framework: **No comparable test identified**

### Book significance

Rhode Island proves that a religion-neutral liberty model existed before Article VI. The federal framers were not choosing between explicit Christian tests and nothing; they knew broad-liberty models existed too.

---

# 4. CONNECTICUT

## Governing framework

Connecticut continued operating under its 1662 colonial charter rather than adopting a new state constitution until 1818.

Its religious establishment was substantially statutory and institutional rather than contained in a new revolutionary-era constitution.

Congregationalism remained the legally established religion through the founding period. Connecticut history sources describe the system as requiring support for the Congregational establishment, though dissenters gained increasing exemptions.

In 1784 the state enacted an Act for Securing the Rights of Conscience extending important protections to persons “professing the Christian religion” of differing denominations. This broadened Christian denominational liberty but did not create full non-Christian equality. Historical Connecticut sources note that non-Christians could still be taxed to support the established church and that Congregational establishment persisted until the 1818 constitution.

Older Connecticut law also contained disabilities tied to denial of God, the Trinity, or scriptural authority.

### Classification

- Congregational establishment: **Yes**
- Christian dissent exemptions/protections: **Increasing**
- Full religion-neutral equality: **No**
- New revolutionary constitution with explicit test: **No; old charter continued**

### Book significance

Connecticut warns against studying only constitutional text. Religious establishment could also exist through inherited charters and statute.

---

# 5. NEW YORK

## 1777 Constitution

New York's constitution contained unusually broad language for the era.

Article XXXVIII guaranteed “the free exercise and enjoyment of religious profession and worship, without discrimination or preference” to “all mankind,” subject to public peace and safety.

The constitution also expressly abrogated inherited laws that could be construed to establish or maintain a particular Christian denomination.

However, New York barred ministers of the gospel or priests of any denomination from civil or military office while serving in their religious function.

### Classification

- Broad free exercise: **Yes**
- Denominational establishment rejected: **Yes**
- Christian/Protestant office test for lay officeholders: **No comparable test**
- Clergy exclusion: **Yes**

### Book significance

New York provides a model where religious liberty was broad but church/state separation was pursued partly by excluding clergy from office rather than by excluding non-Christians.

---

# 6. NEW JERSEY

## 1776 Constitution

New Jersey protected worship according to conscience and prohibited establishment of one religious sect in preference to another.

But its political equality language was explicitly Protestant.

Article XIX provided that no **Protestant** inhabitant should be denied civil rights merely because of religious principles and that persons professing belief in a Protestant sect could be elected to offices of profit or trust or the legislature.

### Classification

- Worship/conscience protection: **Yes**
- Single-sect establishment: **No**
- Protestant civil-office eligibility language: **Yes**
- Full non-Christian political equality: **No**

### Book significance

New Jersey is a clean example of the distinction between freedom of worship and equality of political eligibility. Those concepts were not automatically identical in 1776.

---

# 7. PENNSYLVANIA

## 1776 Constitution

Pennsylvania's Declaration of Rights broadly protected conscience and stated that a person acknowledging the existence of God should not be deprived of civil rights because of religious belief.

But the frame of government imposed a religious test on civil officers/magistrates: officeholders were required to acknowledge the Old and New Testament scriptures as divinely inspired.

The constitution then declared that no further religious test would be required.

That phrase is important: “no further” test still meant a test existed.

## 1790 Constitution

Pennsylvania replaced the explicit scriptural-inspiration test with a more inclusive theistic qualification.

Article IX protected conscience, prohibited legal preference among religious establishments or modes of worship, and provided that no person acknowledging God and a future state of rewards and punishments could be disqualified from office because of religious sentiments.

This removed the explicit Christian-scriptural barrier and opened office eligibility more broadly, including to Jews and potentially Muslims who satisfied the theistic condition.

### Classification, 1776

- Strong conscience protection: **Yes**
- Scriptural religious test: **Yes**

### Classification, 1790

- Strong conscience protection: **Yes**
- Explicit Christian scripture test: **Removed**
- Theistic qualification: **Yes**

### Book significance

Pennsylvania shows constitutional evolution inside the founding period itself. It moved from a distinctly Christian scriptural test toward a broader theistic standard by 1790.

---

# 8. DELAWARE

## 1776 Constitution

Delaware offers one of the most explicit Trinitarian office tests of the founding era.

Article 22 required members of either legislative house and persons appointed to offices or places of trust to profess faith in:

- God the Father;
- Jesus Christ his only Son;
- the Holy Ghost;
- one God;
- divine inspiration of the Old and New Testaments.

The same constitution prohibited establishment of one religious sect in preference to another and excluded clergy from civil office while engaged in pastoral functions.

### Classification

- Nonpreferential establishment language: **Yes**
- Explicit Trinitarian Christian office test: **Yes — very strong**
- Clergy exclusion: **Yes**

### Change after the atlas period

Delaware's 1792 constitution dropped the religious test. That falls just outside this pass's 1791 endpoint but is useful evidence of the broader founding-era movement.

### Book significance

If anyone claims Christian identity was merely an assumed cultural background that constitution writers did not need to specify, Delaware is decisive counterevidence. Its drafters specified Christianity in extraordinarily detailed doctrinal terms.

---

# 9. MARYLAND

## 1776 Declaration of Rights

Maryland Article XXXV stated that no qualification for office of trust or profit should be required beyond the prescribed oath and **a declaration of belief in the Christian religion**.

Maryland also allowed the legislature to lay a general and equal tax for support of the Christian religion under specified conditions in its religious provisions.

### Classification

- Christian office test: **Yes**
- Potential public support for Christianity: **Yes**
- Full religious equality: **No**

### Book significance

Maryland's language is straightforward and constitutionally explicit. It is another strong proof that “Christian qualification” was a readily available drafting option.

---

# 10. VIRGINIA

## 1776 Declaration of Rights

Virginia's Section XVI declared that religion can be directed only by reason and conviction, not force or violence, and that **all men** are equally entitled to the free exercise of religion according to conscience.

The text ends by invoking a mutual duty of Christian forbearance, love, and charity, but it does not make Christianity a civil-office qualification.

Virginia then moved further through the political struggle over religious assessments and Jefferson's Statute for Religious Freedom, enacted in 1786 through Madison's legislative leadership.

The Virginia model is important precisely because its broad conscience language developed inside a deeply Christian society without requiring Christian identity for civil rights in the same way Delaware, Maryland, North Carolina, or Massachusetts did.

### Classification

- Broad free exercise: **Yes**
- Christian/Protestant office test in principal 1776 rights framework: **No**
- Established Anglican legacy: **Being dismantled through revolutionary-era reforms**

### Book significance

Virginia is one of the strongest bridges from toleration toward universal conscience. It demonstrates that religion-neutral constitutional language was not a nineteenth- or twentieth-century invention.

---

# 11. NORTH CAROLINA

## 1776 Constitution

North Carolina combined broad worship language with a severe religious office disability.

The Declaration of Rights said all men possessed a natural and unalienable right to worship Almighty God according to conscience.

But Article XXXII made persons ineligible for civil office if they:

- denied the being of God;
- denied the truth of the Protestant religion;
- denied the divine authority of the Old or New Testaments;
- or held religious principles deemed incompatible with the freedom and safety of the state.

Article XXXIV prohibited establishment of one religious church or denomination in preference to another and protected voluntary worship/support.

### Classification

- Conscience/worship: **Yes**
- Preferred single church establishment: **No**
- Protestant office test: **Yes — strong**

### Book significance

North Carolina is indispensable because its state constitution expressly restricted office on Protestant grounds while its 1788 federal ratification debate confronted the fact that Article VI contained no comparable federal test.

That is the cleanest state/federal contrast in the project.

---

# 12. SOUTH CAROLINA

## 1778 Constitution

South Carolina established the **Christian Protestant religion** as the established religion of the state.

It gave equal religious and civil privileges to peaceful Christian Protestant denominations and established requirements for recognized Protestant churches, including affirmation that Christianity was the true religion and the Old and New Testaments were divinely inspired.

The constitution also imposed Protestant religious qualifications on political participation, including a Protestant requirement for House eligibility and religious qualifications for voters involving belief in God and future rewards/punishments.

## 1790 Constitution

South Carolina changed dramatically.

The 1790 constitution declared free exercise and enjoyment of religious profession and worship “without discrimination or preference” for “all mankind.” It removed the earlier Protestant establishment and the earlier religious voting/office tests. It continued to exclude active ministers/public preachers from specified political offices while exercising pastoral functions.

### Classification, 1778

- Protestant establishment: **Yes — explicit**
- Protestant office eligibility: **Yes**
- Religious voting qualification: **Yes**

### Classification, 1790

- Protestant establishment: **Removed**
- Religion-neutral free exercise: **Yes**
- Religious voting/lay office test: **Removed**
- Clergy exclusion: **Retained**

### Book significance

South Carolina demonstrates constitutional change in real time. Between 1778 and 1790 it moved from explicit Protestant establishment to a broad “all mankind” free-exercise clause.

This is powerful evidence that the founding period was an argument over religious citizenship, not a fixed consensus.

---

# 13. GEORGIA

## 1777 Constitution

Georgia required members of the House of Assembly to be **of the Protestant religion**.

At the same time, Article LVI declared that all persons should have free exercise of religion, subject to peace and safety, and should not be compelled to support teachers outside their own profession.

Clergy were excluded from seats in the legislature.

## 1789 Constitution

Georgia's 1789 revision eliminated the Protestant legislative qualification. The revised constitution broadly protected free exercise and prohibited compulsory support of another religious profession.

### Classification, 1777

- Free exercise: **Yes**
- Protestant legislative test: **Yes**
- Clergy exclusion: **Yes**

### Classification, 1789

- Protestant office test: **Removed**
- Free exercise: **Retained/broadened**

### Book significance

Georgia, like Pennsylvania and South Carolina, shows that state religious qualification was being actively reconsidered during exactly the years in which the federal Constitution was drafted and ratified.

---

# 14. VERMONT

Vermont was not one of the original thirteen states but operated under constitutions before entering the Union in 1791.

## 1777 Constitution

Vermont strongly protected conscience and declared that civil authority should not control free religious worship.

But it also restricted the strongest civil-rights guarantee to persons professing Protestant religion and required civil officers/magistrates to profess:

- belief in one God;
- future reward and punishment;
- divine inspiration of Old and New Testaments;
- the Protestant religion.

## 1786 Constitution

The religious test remained substantially intact.

### Classification

- Conscience protection: **Yes**
- Protestant office test: **Yes**

### Book significance

Vermont is another example of liberty language coexisting with Protestant qualification.

---

# 15. FEDERAL CONTRAST — ARTICLE VI

Against this state-level background, the federal Constitution required federal and state officers to swear or affirm support for the Constitution but then added:

> “no religious Test shall ever be required as a Qualification to any Office or public Trust under the United States.”

This clause did **not** abolish state religious tests for state office. It was a federal rule.

That distinction is essential.

The clause therefore should not be described as instantly creating complete religious equality throughout America.

But neither should it be described as an accidental silence.

By 1787 Americans had many visible constitutional options before them:

- Trinitarian test — Delaware;
- Christian test — Maryland/Massachusetts;
- Protestant test — North Carolina/Georgia/New Hampshire/Vermont/New Jersey political eligibility;
- Protestant establishment — South Carolina;
- theistic test — later Pennsylvania;
- broad free exercise without comparable lay religious test — New York/Virginia/Rhode Island.

The federal text selected **none** of the Christian or Protestant qualification formulas.

---

# 16. THE NORTH CAROLINA CONTROL CASE

North Carolina is the ideal proof against the argument that Article VI's religious breadth is merely a modern reinterpretation.

The state's own 1776 constitution explicitly barred people who denied Protestant truth/scriptural authority from civil office.

Twelve years later, North Carolina's ratification convention discussed the proposed federal Constitution, where Article VI contained no corresponding religious test.

Speakers then explicitly discussed the possibility that Jews, pagans, and “Mahometans” could hold federal office. One opponent warned that “Mahometans may take” the presidential chair; defenders responded by defending general religious liberty and the people's ability to choose whom they trusted.

The correct conclusion is narrow but powerful:

> At least some Americans in 1788 understood that Article VI's no-test rule could permit Muslim federal officeholding, and they argued about that consequence explicitly.

This is far stronger than saying modern courts later discovered Muslim inclusion hidden in eighteenth-century language.

# 17. THE FOUNDING-ERA SPECTRUM

A useful narrative spectrum rather than a binary “Christian/not Christian” classification:

## Explicit Protestant/Christian establishment or qualification

- Delaware — Trinitarian office declaration.
- Maryland — Christian office declaration.
- Massachusetts — Christian office declaration; public Protestant teachers.
- New Hampshire — Protestant governor/senator/representative qualifications; Protestant public teachers.
- North Carolina — Protestant/scriptural civil-office test.
- South Carolina 1778 — Protestant establishment and political qualifications.
- Georgia 1777 — Protestant legislative qualification.
- Vermont — Protestant office test.
- New Jersey — Protestant political-eligibility protections.
- Pennsylvania 1776 — Old/New Testament divine-inspiration office test.

## Establishment through inherited/statutory structure

- Connecticut — Congregational establishment.

## Broader religion-neutral constitutional direction

- Rhode Island — longstanding broad conscience model.
- Virginia — all-men free exercise; disestablishment trajectory.
- New York — free exercise “to all mankind,” no denominational preference, clergy exclusion.

## Rapid liberalization inside the founding era

- Georgia 1789 — Protestant test removed.
- Pennsylvania 1790 — scripture test replaced by broad theistic qualification.
- South Carolina 1790 — Protestant establishment and religious political qualifications removed.

This spectrum is more historically honest than asking whether “the states” had religious tests as though they acted uniformly.

# 18. WHAT THIS DOES TO THE CHRISTIAN-AMERICA ARGUMENT

Pass 22 strongly **supports** several elements of the Christian-America case.

The evidence makes it impossible to claim that Christianity was merely private or irrelevant to founding-era governance.

Several states legally privileged Christianity or Protestantism.

Some funded Protestant religious instruction.

Some required Christian belief for public office.

Some restricted political eligibility to Protestants.

This belongs plainly in the book.

But this evidence also creates a problem for the claim that the federal Constitution should simply be read as implicitly Christian because the culture was Christian.

Why?

Because founding-era Americans repeatedly demonstrated that when they wanted religious qualification, they knew how to write it explicitly.

The question therefore becomes not:

> Were the founders Christians?

but:

> Why did the federal Constitution use a no-religious-test rule when explicit Christian and Protestant tests were readily available constitutional technology?

That is a much more historically serious question.

---

# 19. WHAT THIS DOES NOT PROVE

Pass 22 does **not** prove:

- every federal framer personally supported Muslim equality;
- Article VI was drafted specifically because of Muslims;
- the states became religion-neutral in 1787;
- Christianity ceased to shape public institutions;
- Muslims enjoyed modern social equality;
- Jews, Catholics, atheists, or Muslims encountered no informal discrimination;
- the First Amendment originally applied to state governments as it does after incorporation;
- federal no-test language abolished state tests.

Avoid every one of these overstatements.

# 20. CLAIM GRADES

## Claim: Founding-era Americans knew how to constitutionally require Christianity/Protestantism for office.

**Grade: A.**

Multiple state constitutions contain direct text.

## Claim: Religious tests were universal among the states.

**Grade: E.**

False.

## Claim: Strong conscience clauses automatically meant religiously neutral officeholding.

**Grade: E.**

New Hampshire, Pennsylvania, North Carolina, Vermont, and others prove otherwise.

## Claim: The federal Article VI no-test rule differed materially from many contemporary state systems.

**Grade: A.**

## Claim: Article VI intentionally protected Muslims specifically.

**Grade: C/E depending wording.**

No Muslim-specific drafting evidence identified. Muslims are documented in ratification discussion as a known implication.

## Claim: Americans understood during ratification that Muslims could potentially hold federal office under Article VI.

**Grade: A for the North Carolina debate.**

## Claim: The founding era saw movement away from some explicit Protestant/Christian tests.

**Grade: A/B.**

Georgia 1789, Pennsylvania 1790, and South Carolina 1790 supply strong examples.

# 21. CHAPTER 8 PACKAGE — “THEY KNEW HOW TO WRITE A RELIGIOUS TEST”

This pass substantially completes the factual purpose of Chapter 8.

The chapter should not list every state mechanically.

Use a small number of escalating examples:

### Delaware

The doctrinally explicit test: Father, Son, Holy Ghost, divinely inspired scriptures.

### Maryland

The concise formula: declaration of belief in the Christian religion.

### North Carolina

The Protestant and Old/New Testament civil-office exclusion.

### Massachusetts

Christian office declaration plus public Protestant support.

Then tell the reader:

These were not obscure theories. These were constitutional clauses.

Only after the reader understands that should Chapter 9 reveal Article VI.

Potential chapter pivot:

> The men who wrote America's early constitutions did not lack the vocabulary to make public office Christian. They had already done it repeatedly.

Then:

> In Philadelphia in 1787, the federal Constitution chose a different sentence.

# 24. PASS 22 VERDICT

Pass 22 survives adversarial review at the architecture level.

The evidence establishes something more precise than “America was secular” or “America was Christian.”

The founding states built **multiple constitutional relationships between religion and government at the same time**.

Some established Protestantism.

Some required Christian belief.

Some required Protestant identity.

Some protected conscience but restricted office.

Some began removing tests.

Some moved toward broad religious neutrality.

Against that living constitutional laboratory, the federal Article VI prohibition on religious tests becomes much more historically significant.

The strongest safe line for the manuscript is:

> The founders did not omit a Christian religious test because eighteenth-century Americans did not know how to write one. State constitutions show that they knew exactly how. The federal Constitution chose not to use one.

And the strongest bridge into the Muslim-president chapter remains:

> The next question is whether anyone noticed what that omission meant.

They did.
