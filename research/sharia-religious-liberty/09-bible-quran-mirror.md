# Research Pass 09 — The Bible–Qur’an Mirror

## Governing question

What happens if the same interpretive standards are applied to difficult biblical and Qur’anic texts?

This pass is not an attempt to prove that the Bible and Qur’an are the same, nor that all Christian and Islamic legal traditions developed identically. The purpose is methodological: modern polemics often treat harsh Qur’anic texts as transparent statements of what all Muslims must believe today while treating harsh biblical texts as historically bounded, covenantal, figurative, superseded, or otherwise contextualized. A fair comparison requires text, context, jurisprudence, historical application, and modern interpretation on both sides.

## Central finding

Both scriptures contain severe passages involving warfare, retaliation, sexual conduct, religious offense, outsiders, and slavery. Both also contain strong traditions of mercy, charity, neighbor-love, justice, restraint, forgiveness, and care for vulnerable people. The Bible contains explicit ancient civil penalties for idolatry, blasphemy, and adultery, as well as rules governing slavery and warfare. The Qur’an contains corporal penalties and warfare commands, but also explicit restrictions on aggression, language against religious compulsion, obligations of justice even toward enemies, protection for noncombatants/peaceful outsiders in core texts, and strong encouragement of charity and manumission.

The strongest rhetorical conclusion is not “Islam is peaceful and Christianity is violent” or its reverse. It is:

> If Christians are entitled to read their hardest texts through history, covenant, genre, theology, legal development, and moral interpretation, Muslims are entitled to the same intellectual courtesy.

## 1. Apostasy / abandoning the faith

### Biblical material

Deuteronomy 13 commands death for a prophet or dreamer who entices Israel to follow other gods. The text frames this as rebellion against Israel’s covenantal God. Deuteronomy 17 likewise provides that a man or woman who worships other gods may be stoned after investigation and the testimony of two or three witnesses.

These are not vague hostile verses; they are legal penalties embedded in the Torah’s covenantal civil order.

### Islamic material

The Qur’an contains repeated condemnation of unbelief and apostasy, but Qur’an 2:256 states that there is to be no compulsion in religion. A significant body of modern scholarship emphasizes that the Qur’an itself does not prescribe an earthly death penalty simply for changing one’s religion. At the same time, the majority of classical Muslim jurists developed a death penalty for apostasy, drawing primarily from hadith and legal doctrine. Modern Muslim scholars disagree over whether those classical rulings addressed mere private change of belief or apostasy understood in historical contexts as political treason, rebellion, or hostile defection.

### Grade

- Bible contains explicit legal death penalties for idolatrous defection: **A — direct primary text**.
- Classical Islamic jurisprudence generally prescribed death for apostasy: **A/B — well documented jurisprudential fact**.
- Qur’an itself lacks a simple verse saying “execute anyone merely for leaving Islam”: **A/B — strong textual/scholarly conclusion, but interpretive disputes remain about surrounding texts and hadith**.

### Narrative use

A modern American Christian can read Deuteronomy 13 without believing the U.S. government should execute a Baptist who becomes Hindu. That interpretive move is normal. The book should ask why a Muslim’s relationship to classical apostasy doctrine is so often treated as incapable of comparable historical or jurisprudential development.

## 2. Religious coercion

Qur’an 2:256 states that there is no compulsion in religion. Classical tafsir, including Ibn Kathir, reads the verse as forbidding forced conversion to Islam in the basic sense.

This does not erase every coercive practice in Muslim history, every discriminatory rule in classical fiqh, or later debates over apostasy. It does establish that the Qur’an contains an explicit anti-compulsion proposition that cannot honestly be omitted from any description of Islamic teaching on religion.

Biblical law, by contrast, contains covenantal penalties for worshiping other gods in ancient Israel. Modern Christianity generally does not apply those provisions as civil law.

This creates one of the book’s cleanest methodological contrasts:

> Christians routinely distinguish the civil law of ancient Israel from the obligations of a modern Christian citizen. The existence of classical Islamic legal rules likewise does not answer, by itself, what an American Muslim believes the state should enforce today.

## 3. Blasphemy

Leviticus 24:16 provides a death penalty by stoning for blaspheming the divine name, applying the rule to native and foreigner alike.

Islamic legal traditions also developed severe blasphemy rules, with differences among schools, offenses, defendants, and political settings. This pass does not yet attempt a full fiqh survey; that belongs in the later “hard cases” pass.

For narrative purposes, the essential point is that a death penalty for religious offense is not a uniquely Islamic phenomenon. Ancient biblical law contains one explicitly.

## 4. Adultery and sexual offenses

Leviticus 20:10 states that both adulterer and adulteress are to be put to death. Other Torah passages also prescribe severe penalties for sexual offenses.

Qur’an 24:2 prescribes one hundred lashes for unlawful sexual intercourse in the textual case addressed there. Classical Islamic law also developed stoning for certain forms of adultery based primarily on hadith and jurisprudence, not the Qur’anic verse alone.

These systems are not identical. The Bible’s Torah text directly states death for adultery; the Qur’an’s direct textual penalty in 24:2 is flogging, while classical fiqh adds other penalties through extra-Qur’anic sources.

The modern constitutional conclusion is straightforward: neither biblical nor Islamic corporal/Capital punishments become enforceable American criminal law merely because adherents consider scripture sacred.

## 5. Retaliation: “eye for eye”

The Hebrew Bible contains lex talionis — “eye for eye” — in Exodus, Leviticus, and Deuteronomy.

The Qur’an explicitly repeats the same formula in 5:45 while identifying it with the Torah: life for life, eye for eye, nose for nose, ear for ear, tooth for tooth, wounds in retaliation. But the Qur’anic verse immediately adds that remission/forgiveness may serve as expiation.

In the New Testament, Matthew 5:38–44 quotes “eye for eye” and then directs Jesus’ followers toward non-retaliation and love for enemies.

This is an especially powerful mirror because the traditions openly intersect. The Qur’an is not hiding that the retaliation principle belongs to earlier biblical law; it cites it as Torah.

Potential line:

> When Americans hear “eye for eye” they usually think Bible. When they hear “Islamic law” they are taught to imagine something alien. The texts themselves are telling us the histories overlap.

## 6. Warfare

### Biblical warfare

Deuteronomy 20 includes rules for attacking cities: offer peace; if a distant city submits, impose forced labor; if it resists, kill the males and take women, children, livestock, and goods as spoil. For certain nearby peoples, the chapter commands complete destruction.

These passages are among the harshest texts in the Bible. Most modern Christians do not read them as standing orders to wage religious war against contemporary non-Christians.

### Qur’anic warfare

Qur’an 2:190 commands fighting those who fight the believers while warning against transgression. The following verses contain direct commands to kill enemies in the conflict context but also say that if they cease, aggression is to cease except against oppressors.

Qur’an 9:5 is often quoted as the “Sword Verse”: kill the polytheists wherever found after the sacred months. But the immediate context concerns treaty relationships and hostile groups. Qur’an 9:6 requires protection and safe passage for a polytheist who seeks asylum; 9:7 requires fidelity toward treaty partners who remain faithful.

Qur’an 60:8 says Muslims are not forbidden from acting righteously and justly toward people who do not fight them because of religion or expel them from their homes.

### Discipline

Do not claim the Qur’an contains only defensive warfare. Classical jurists developed broader doctrines of jihad, empire, tribute, and expansion, just as Christian states developed doctrines and practices of religious war. The textual evidence does, however, refute the crude claim that the Qur’an simply commands Muslims to kill all non-Muslims everywhere.

## 7. Slavery

### Bible

Exodus 21:20–21 regulates the beating of enslaved people and treats the slave as the owner’s property for purposes of the rule. Leviticus 25:44–46 permits Israelites to acquire male and female slaves from surrounding nations, treat them as property, and pass them to descendants as an inheritance.

These passages are unambiguous evidence that biblical law regulated and permitted slavery.

### Qur’an / Islamic tradition

The Qur’an emerged in a slaveholding society and does not abolish slavery outright. It recognizes slavery and captive status in multiple passages. At the same time, it repeatedly treats freeing slaves as a righteous or expiatory act; Qur’an 90:13 explicitly includes freeing a slave among the steep path of righteousness. Other verses use manumission as expiation for certain offenses.

Historically, Muslim societies practiced slavery for many centuries, sometimes on enormous scales. The book must not sanitize that history.

The fair comparison is therefore:

- Bible: regulates and permits slavery; later Christians developed abolitionist and pro-slavery readings.
- Qur’an/fiqh: regulates slavery and strongly encourages manumission in certain contexts; Muslim societies also perpetuated slavery for centuries.

Neither tradition gets a free pass.

## 8. Justice toward enemies

Qur’an 5:8 says believers must not allow hatred of a people to cause injustice; they are commanded to be just precisely toward those they dislike.

Qur’an 4:135 similarly demands justice even when it runs against oneself, parents, relatives, rich, or poor.

Matthew 5:44 directs Jesus’ followers to love enemies and pray for persecutors.

These are not identical ethical formulations, but they belong in the same comparative moral universe: justice and moral obligation are not supposed to disappear when dealing with adversaries.

Potential line:

> The Qur’an does not merely command justice toward friends. One of its most demanding moral instructions is to remain just when hatred is pulling you the other way.

## 9. Neighbors, strangers, poor, and vulnerable people

Qur’an 4:36 tells believers to do good to parents, relatives, orphans, the needy, the near neighbor, the more distant neighbor, companions, travelers, and those under their authority.

Biblical law and the teachings of Jesus contain parallel emphases on neighbor-love, care for the stranger, poor, widow, orphan, and enemy.

This matters because “Sharia” in ordinary Muslim life is very often experienced through exactly these moral obligations rather than through penal law.

A future interview pass should ask American Muslims what living under Sharia means in daily practice. The expected hypothesis to test—not presume—is that answers will frequently center prayer, fasting, honesty, charity, family duties, diet, modesty, contracts, and moral discipline rather than a desire to replace American criminal law.

## 10. Contracts and evidence

Qur’an 2:282 gives a long, highly specific treatment of debt documentation, scribes, witnesses, and preservation of evidence. This is a useful corrective to the popular image of Sharia as primarily penal law.

The Bible also contains extensive commercial, debt, property, witness, restitution, and judicial rules.

The mirror here is not about identical doctrine. It is about category error: ancient religious legal traditions were comprehensive social systems, not merely lists of punishments.

## 11. The asymmetry in modern American rhetoric

A recurring polemical pattern works like this:

1. Select the harshest Qur’anic verse.
2. Remove it from its literary and historical setting.
3. Ignore centuries of interpretive disagreement.
4. Treat the harshest classical legal ruling as the single meaning of Islam.
5. Ask an ordinary Muslim to answer for it personally.

But when confronted with a severe biblical passage, many Christians instinctively do something much more sophisticated:

1. Identify Old vs. New Covenant.
2. Identify ancient Israel’s political setting.
3. Distinguish descriptive from prescriptive material.
4. Consult genre and context.
5. Interpret through later scripture and theology.
6. Distinguish religious morality from modern civil enforcement.

The book’s argument should be that intellectual honesty requires allowing both traditions their interpretive history.

## 12. What this pass does NOT prove

Do not claim:

- the Bible and Qur’an are morally identical;
- Islam and Christianity have the same doctrines;
- classical Islamic law was liberal by modern standards;
- every severe Qur’anic passage is merely metaphorical;
- every Muslim rejects classical corporal punishments;
- every Christian rejects biblical civil penalties;
- violent or authoritarian interpretations of Islam do not exist;
- violent or authoritarian Christian political movements do not exist.

The book should instead insist on symmetrical analysis.

## 13. Strongest comparative cases for the eventual narrative

### A. Apostasy / idolatry

Bible: Deuteronomy 13 and 17 contain death penalties connected to leading people toward or worshiping other gods.

Islam: classical jurists generally developed a death penalty for apostasy, while the Qur’an contains no simple earthly death command for merely changing one’s personal belief and also contains 2:256 against religious compulsion.

Narrative effect: the frightening category exists in both traditions, but legal development and modern interpretation matter.

### B. Blasphemy

Bible: Leviticus 24:16 — death by stoning.

Islam: severe classical blasphemy rules.

Narrative effect: religious-offense capital law is not uniquely Islamic.

### C. Adultery

Bible: Leviticus 20:10 — death.

Qur’an: 24:2 — lashes; classical fiqh later developed additional penalties including stoning in certain cases.

Narrative effect: both contain corporal/capital legal histories alien to modern U.S. constitutional law.

### D. Warfare

Bible: Deuteronomy 20 — forced labor, killing, plunder, and for certain peoples total destruction.

Qur’an: direct warfare commands, including 2:190–193 and 9:5, but also prohibitions on transgression, cessation when enemies cease, asylum in 9:6, treaty fidelity in 9:7, and justice toward peaceful noncombatants in 60:8.

Narrative effect: neither scripture can responsibly be reduced to a single war verse.

### E. Slavery

Bible: Exodus 21 and Leviticus 25 regulate and permit slavery/property status.

Qur’an: slavery is recognized rather than abolished, while manumission is repeatedly praised/required in certain contexts.

Narrative effect: both traditions emerged in slave societies; later moral development cannot be understood by pretending the ancient texts never contained slavery.

### F. Retaliation and forgiveness

Bible/Torah: eye for eye.

Qur’an 5:45: explicitly repeats the Torah formula and adds remission/forgiveness.

Matthew 5:38–44: cites eye-for-eye and moves disciples toward non-retaliation and enemy-love.

Narrative effect: the traditions are historically and textually entangled rather than alien worlds.

## 14. The constitutional answer

The United States solves this problem in a way neither polemical camp should fear:

The Constitution does not require government to decide which scripture is the true one.

A Christian may believe biblical law is divinely inspired.

A Muslim may believe the Qur’an and Sharia are divinely authoritative.

A Jew may live by halakhic obligations.

But no religious text becomes superior to the Constitution as enforceable governmental law merely because believers consider it sacred.

This gives us one of the book’s clearest formulations:

> America protects your right to live by your scripture. It does not give you the right to make everyone else live under it.

That applies equally to Bible and Qur’an.

## 15. Potential narrative lines to preserve

- “The frightening verse is easy to find in somebody else’s scripture.”
- “Christians know instinctively that Deuteronomy is not a criminal code for Arkansas in 2026. The question is why we pretend Muslims are incapable of making comparable distinctions inside their own tradition.”
- “If quoting Deuteronomy 20 does not prove your Baptist neighbor wants to conquer the next county, quoting Qur’an 9:5 does not by itself prove your Muslim neighbor wants to conquer America.”
- “Context is not an excuse Christians invented for Muslims. Christians use it every Sunday.”
- “The Constitution protects the Christian who believes the Bible and the Muslim who believes the Qur’an precisely because it prevents either one from turning scripture into compulsory national law.”
- “The danger is not that a citizen has a sacred law. The danger begins when any sacred law acquires the coercive power of the state.”

## 17. Pass-nine conclusion

The most defensible conclusion is not that the harsh texts cancel one another out. It is that selective literalism is bad history.

Ancient biblical law contains rules that virtually no mainstream American Christian wants enacted as civil law. Islamic scripture and jurisprudence also contain ancient and classical rules that many modern Muslims interpret, limit, historicize, or reject as state policy. Both traditions also contain powerful moral commands toward justice, mercy, charity, restraint, and care for neighbors and vulnerable people.

The constitutional genius relevant to this project is that America does not need to settle the theological dispute. It protects conscience while withholding coercive governmental supremacy from every religion.

The line to carry forward:

> Religious liberty is not the promise that every sacred text will agree with the Constitution. It is the promise that no sacred text gets to replace it—and no believer loses citizenship because someone else is frightened by their scripture.
