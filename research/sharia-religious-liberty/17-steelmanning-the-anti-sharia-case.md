# Research Pass 17 — Steelmanning the Anti-Sharia Case

## Purpose

This pass deliberately builds the strongest evidence-based case against state-enforced or coercive interpretations of Sharia before evaluating which criticisms are correct, partly correct, historically contingent, exaggerated, or unsupported. It does not treat ordinary Muslim religious practice as a security threat and does not assume that one classical school, modern state, political Islamist movement, or individual Muslim represents Islam as a whole.

The central methodological distinction is between (1) Sharia as a divine moral ideal, (2) fiqh as human jurisprudence, (3) voluntary personal religious observance, (4) voluntary religious mediation, (5) state-enforced Islamic law, and (6) political movements seeking Islamic supremacy. Criticism that moves evidence from category 5 or 6 onto every person in category 3 is not analytically sound.

## Executive finding

The strongest anti-Sharia argument is not that every Muslim who follows Sharia is plotting to overthrow the Constitution. The evidence does not establish that proposition. The strongest argument is narrower and substantially true: important doctrines in classical Islamic jurisprudence, and some laws currently enforced by governments claiming Islamic authority, conflict directly with modern American constitutional commitments to freedom of religion, freedom of speech, equal citizenship, sex equality, due process, and prohibitions on cruel punishment if those doctrines are given coercive state power.

The corresponding constitutional answer is also narrower than either political camp often admits. American law does not have to declare Sharia benign, false, true, or dangerous as a theology. It protects voluntary Muslim belief and practice while refusing coercive religious rules that violate controlling civil law and constitutional rights.

## 1. Apostasy — criticism substantially correct as to classical law

A serious critic can accurately say that pre-modern Islamic jurisprudence developed legal restrictions on apostasy and that the majority classical tradition treated apostasy as a punishable public offense, frequently including capital punishment under specified conditions. Abdullah Saeed's scholarship traces this development while also explaining the historical fusion of apostasy, political defection, rebellion, and treason in formative Muslim societies.

This is a genuine collision with the American constitutional order if applied to a private change of religion. A state cannot execute, imprison, fine, or civilly disable a citizen merely because that person leaves Islam.

Important qualification: the Qur'an condemns apostasy but does not contain a simple criminal statute saying that every person who privately leaves Islam must be executed. The capital doctrine is principally a product of hadith interpretation and fiqh. Modern Muslim reformers have developed substantial arguments for abandoning the classical penalty and recognizing freedom to change religion.

Grade: **Correct as a criticism of major classical fiqh and some modern legal systems; false if converted into the claim that every Muslim believes apostates should be killed.**

## 2. Blasphemy — a real free-speech conflict

Islamic legal traditions developed serious sanctions for blasphemy, insulting Muhammad, and related religious offenses. Contemporary blasphemy regimes in some Muslim-majority states demonstrate that this is not merely medieval history. Human-rights scholarship documents imprisonment, death sentences, intimidation, and chilling effects connected to apostasy and blasphemy restrictions.

From an American constitutional perspective, the state may punish threats, violence, harassment, or other independently unlawful conduct, but religious offensiveness itself cannot become a crime simply because a sacred figure, scripture, or community has been insulted.

Grade: **Correct where Sharia is interpreted to authorize state punishment for religious insult; not evidence that ordinary American Muslim objection to offensive speech is itself unconstitutional.**

## 3. Hudud and corporal punishment — criticism correct where coercively enforced

Classical jurisprudence contains corporal and capital punishments associated with offenses including theft, zina, false accusation, and highway robbery. The tradition also developed demanding evidentiary standards, doctrines of doubt (shubha), repentance rules, and procedural barriers that could sharply restrict application. Those restraints matter historically, but they do not erase the constitutional collision.

Flogging, amputation, and similar punishments would face fundamental American constitutional objections if imposed as criminal penalties by government.

Grade: **Correct. Internal procedural restraints complicate the caricature but do not make corporal punishment constitutionally acceptable.**

## 4. Women and family law — a serious equality problem, but not a single rulebook

The strongest feminist and human-rights critique identifies sex-differentiated rules in classical family law concerning marriage authority, divorce, inheritance, guardianship, maintenance, interfaith marriage, and some evidentiary contexts. Modern Saudi personal-status law provides a contemporary example of government-enforced male guardianship and unequal divorce authority. Human Rights Watch reports that Saudi law requires guardian permission for women's marriage, imposes marital-obedience consequences, gives men unilateral divorce authority while women face more restricted judicial routes, and preserves paternal guardianship structures.

This is not merely Western propaganda; Muslim feminists and reform scholars have spent decades challenging discriminatory family laws from within Islamic intellectual traditions.

At the same time, classical schools differ, modern Muslim jurisdictions differ, and American Muslims differ. A religious teaching that assigns different family roles does not automatically violate the Constitution when voluntarily practiced. Government-enforced unequal civil status is the constitutional problem.

Grade: **Correct regarding significant classical and contemporary legal inequalities; exaggerated when generalized into 'Islam gives women no rights' or projected automatically onto every Muslim family.**

## 5. Religious minorities and dhimma — tolerance without modern equality

Classical Islamic governance developed systems protecting certain non-Muslim communities, especially Jews and Christians, while assigning them a differentiated legal status. Such arrangements could provide meaningful security and communal autonomy compared with many historical alternatives, but they did not amount to modern equal citizenship. Jizya, restrictions on political authority, legal distinctions, and other disabilities varied by time and place.

Anver Emon's constitutional scholarship identifies the modern problem directly: if Sharia constitutional clauses incorporate historical rules that discriminate by religion, they can conflict with constitutional commitments to religious liberty and nondiscrimination.

Grade: **Correct that important classical systems distinguished citizens by religion; misleading to reduce the entire history to 'convert or die.'**

## 6. Sharia supremacy clauses — the strongest institutional criticism

A particularly serious concern arises when a constitution declares that no legislation may contradict Sharia. Such clauses create a genuine constitutional-design question: who determines what Sharia requires, and what happens when that interpretation conflicts with rights guarantees?

Scholarship on Islamic constitutional systems documents real conflicts between Sharia-repugnancy clauses and religious-freedom or human-rights provisions, including apostasy disputes. This is not analogous to an American Muslim privately believing God's law is morally supreme. It is a governmental allocation of coercive authority.

For the United States, an amendment declaring that no law could contradict Islamic law would be fundamentally different from the current constitutional system. The same establishment problem would arise from an equivalent rule requiring every law to conform to Christianity, biblical law, Catholic canon law, or another sectarian authority.

Grade: **Correct and institutionally important.**

## 7. Jihad and political violence — critics identify a real tradition but often overgeneralize it

It is historically false to say jihad has only ever meant inner spiritual struggle. Islamic jurisprudence developed extensive doctrines governing armed jihad, war, truces, territorial relations, and military conduct. Muslim empires fought expansionary wars, just as other premodern empires did.

Therefore critics are justified in rejecting apologetic claims that armed jihad is an invention with no grounding in Islamic history.

But the opposite leap is equally unsound: the existence of classical military jurisprudence does not establish that an American Muslim who prays, fasts, gives zakat, or says she follows Sharia intends armed conquest. Christianity has traditions of just war, crusade, religious warfare, and biblical warfare texts without making every contemporary Christian a latent combatant.

Grade: **Correct that armed jihad is historically and jurisprudentially real; unsupported when converted into collective suspicion of Muslims.**

## 8. Survey evidence — some uncomfortable views are genuinely held

Pew's 2013 multinational survey is important evidence that must not be buried. Among Muslims who favored making Sharia official law, substantial shares in some surveyed countries supported corporal punishment, stoning for adultery, and execution for apostasy. In Egypt, Jordan, Afghanistan, Pakistan, Malaysia and other countries, support for some severe rules was substantial. Yet the results varied dramatically across countries and regions, with much lower support elsewhere.

This evidence defeats the claim that virtually no Muslims support severe classical punishments.

It does **not** establish that American Muslims hold the same views. The survey covered different national populations, political systems, histories, and understandings of 'Sharia.' It also shows precisely why the word cannot be treated as carrying one universally agreed political program.

Grade: **Correct evidence of real support for illiberal interpretations in parts of the Muslim world; invalid when mechanically projected onto American Muslims.**

## 9. Theocracy and sovereignty — legitimate concern, often asked too crudely

Some Islamic political movements advocate states in which legislation and government must conform to Islamic norms. That model can conflict with American constitutional religious neutrality depending on institutional design and the rights permitted to dissenters.

But theological belief that God is sovereign is not itself proof of political disloyalty. Millions of Christians also believe divine authority is higher than human government. The constitutional question is whether a citizen accepts the lawful civil order and the equal rights of others, not whether the citizen privately believes God possesses ultimate authority.

Grade: **Correct as a concern about coercive theocratic institutions; unsupported as a loyalty test based solely on theology.**

## 10. Parallel religious legal systems — a bounded concern

A critic can reasonably object to any religious tribunal that attempts to coerce nonconsenting people, evade criminal law, deny statutory rights, conceal abuse, or enforce judgments contrary to public policy. Those objections apply to Muslim, Jewish, Christian, or other institutions.

But voluntary religious mediation, arbitration, pastoral counseling, marriage ceremonies, internal discipline, and religious advice do not automatically constitute a rival sovereign court system. American civil law determines whether an agreement or award receives civil enforcement.

Grade: **Correct when coercion or evasion of civil law occurs; exaggerated when every imam-mediated family dispute is labeled a Sharia takeover.**

## 11. Ex-Muslims and internal critics must be included

A credible book cannot interview only Muslims who experience Sharia positively. Former Muslims, dissidents, women's-rights advocates, religious minorities from Muslim-majority states, and Muslim reformers may have experienced coercive Islamic rules very differently.

Their testimony can reveal family pressure, threats over apostasy, forced or coerced marriage, restrictions on women, censorship, sectarian discrimination, or trauma from theocratic governments. Such testimony should neither be dismissed as Islamophobia nor treated as proof about every Muslim.

The interview architecture should include both believers who say Sharia structures a peaceful moral life and people who say coercive Sharia harmed them.

## 12. What the strongest anti-Sharia case does NOT establish

After steelmanning the criticism, the evidence still does not establish the following propositions:

- that American Muslims as a class are engaged in a coordinated effort to replace the Constitution;
- that saying one 'follows Sharia' means supporting hudud criminal penalties;
- that mosque construction, halal food, hijab, Ramadan, zakat, Islamic finance, or Muslim political participation constitutes incremental theocracy;
- that taqiyya gives every Muslim a general religious license to deceive non-Muslims about political conquest;
- that classical jihad doctrine proves the intentions of an individual Muslim citizen;
- that one country's law can be treated as the universal meaning of Islam;
- that protecting Muslim free exercise requires allowing Islamic criminal law to supersede the Constitution.

These are separate propositions requiring separate evidence.

## 13. Criticism matrix

### Substantially correct
- Classical apostasy penalties conflict with freedom of conscience if state enforced.
- Blasphemy laws can conflict directly with freedom of expression and religion.
- Hudud corporal punishments can conflict with modern constitutional criminal-law norms.
- Classical family law includes significant sex-based distinctions inconsistent with modern equal-citizenship norms if imposed by the state.
- Classical systems often differentiated legal status by religion.
- Armed jihad is a genuine part of Islamic legal history.
- Sharia supremacy clauses can create real conflicts with constitutional rights.
- Some contemporary Muslim populations show significant support for severe classical penalties.

### Partly correct / requires context
- 'Sharia discriminates against women.' Some important classical and modern rules do; Sharia is not one code and Muslim interpretations differ dramatically.
- 'Sharia discriminates against non-Muslims.' Important classical institutions did; historical treatment varied and often included protection as well as inequality.
- 'Sharia contains violent rules.' Yes, but that does not define all Sharia or predict individual Muslim conduct.
- 'Religious arbitration can threaten rights.' Yes when coercive or contrary to controlling law; not merely because it is religious.

### Historically contingent
- Apostasy as treason or political defection in formative Muslim states.
- Dhimma and jizya as premodern systems of imperial religious pluralism.
- Classical warfare doctrines formed in a world of competing empires and weak modern distinctions between religion, citizenship, and geopolitical allegiance.

### Exaggerated or unsupported
- Every observant Muslim seeks a theocracy.
- Ordinary Sharia observance is a constitutional threat.
- Every mosque is a political beachhead.
- Every denial of a Muslim conspiracy is taqiyya.
- American Muslim civic participation is evidence of infiltration.
- Protecting Islamic religious liberty means permitting Sharia to supersede American law.

## 14. The strongest anti-Sharia argument, stated fairly

A serious critic can say:

> Some historically authoritative interpretations of Islamic jurisprudence contain rules governing apostasy, blasphemy, punishment, gender, religious status, and warfare that are incompatible with modern American constitutional rights if enforced by government. Some contemporary governments still enforce or invoke such rules, and survey evidence shows that substantial populations in some Muslim-majority societies support at least some of them. Americans are therefore justified in opposing any effort—Islamic or otherwise—to place coercive religious law above constitutional rights.

That argument is substantially defensible.

What cannot be appended without additional evidence is:

> Therefore American Muslims who follow Sharia are participants in a covert movement to impose those rules on the United States.

The second proposition does not follow from the first.

## 15. The constitutional answer

The Constitution provides a remarkably simple way to take the legitimate criticism seriously without creating religious collective guilt.

A Muslim may believe Sharia is divinely authoritative.
A Christian may believe the Bible is divinely authoritative.
A Jew may believe halakha is divinely authoritative.

The state protects belief, worship, expression, association, and lawful religious practice. It does not permit any believer to use coercive governmental or private power to strip another person of constitutional or statutory rights.

This means the constitutional response to the genuinely hard parts of Sharia is not to outlaw Islam. It is to maintain constitutional supremacy.

**Religious liberty protects the believer. Constitutional supremacy protects everyone else.**

## 16. Narrative implications

The book should not tell frightened readers, 'There is nothing in Islamic law you should ever worry about.' That claim would be false and unnecessary.

The stronger argument is:

**There are things in Islamic legal history that Americans should reject if anyone tries to impose them through government. There are also things in Christian and biblical legal history Americans would reject if imposed through government. The constitutional achievement is that we do not have to destroy either religion to prevent either religion from becoming coercive state law.**

A second key line:

**The anti-Sharia argument becomes strongest when it attacks coercion and weakest when it turns millions of individual Muslims into suspects.**

And the crucial logical distinction:

**A doctrine can be dangerous in the hands of a state without making every believer dangerous as a citizen.**

## 18. Evidence discipline

A — Primary legal text, official statute, constitutional text, high-quality direct survey data, or directly documented historical doctrine.

B — Strong academic consensus or well-supported scholarly reconstruction.

C — Serious but contested interpretation or transmission claim.

D — Comparative or structural analogy.

E — Unsupported inference, collective attribution, or conspiracy claim.

No E-grade claim should enter the final book as fact.

## Final conclusion

Pass 17 does not vindicate the claim that Sharia is harmless in every interpretation. It establishes something more useful: **the legitimate case against coercive Sharia can be separated from the illegitimate case against Muslim citizenship.**

The United States can simultaneously say no to apostasy executions, blasphemy prosecutions, religiously mandated corporal punishment, sex-based civil inequality, religious caste citizenship, private holy war, and sectarian supremacy—and say yes to the Muslim's right to pray, fast, worship, eat halal, wear religious clothing, organize a mosque, give zakat, enter lawful contracts, vote, hold office, and live according to conscience.

That is not a compromise with theocracy. It is constitutional religious liberty.
