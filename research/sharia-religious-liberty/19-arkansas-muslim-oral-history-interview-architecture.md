# Research Pass 19 — Arkansas Muslim Oral History and Interview Architecture

## Purpose

Pass 19 converts the documentary project into a human reporting project. The book has established the legal, historical, theological, and constitutional framework. The next evidentiary layer should ask actual Muslims—and actual critics of Islam—what these abstractions mean in lived American and Arkansas life.

The purpose is not to assemble witnesses who prove a predetermined thesis. It is to build an oral-history record strong enough to complicate the thesis when reality requires it.

Core reporting principle:

> Do not ask Muslims to represent Islam. Ask people to describe their own lives, beliefs, disagreements, experiences, and understanding of citizenship.

The project should deliberately include Muslims who disagree with one another and critics/former Muslims who can identify coercive or harmful experiences that a celebratory religious-liberty narrative might otherwise miss.

---

## 1. Why Arkansas should be the primary reporting laboratory

Arkansas gives the project a uniquely useful setting because the larger research has already established several tensions:

- Arkansas possesses unusually expansive constitutional language protecting conscience.
- Modern Arkansas statutes expressly protect churches, synagogues, shrines, mosques, and temples.
- Arkansas has publicly celebrated its religious-liberty protections.
- *Holt v. Hobbs* placed an Arkansas Muslim at the center of a unanimous U.S. Supreme Court religious-liberty decision.
- Arkansas has simultaneously experienced modern disputes over government-sponsored Christian religious expression.
- Muslim institutional life in Arkansas is decades old and includes Black American Muslim history, immigrant communities, students, professionals, families, and interfaith relationships.

This makes Arkansas more than a convenient local case study. It is a controlled constitutional question:

> What does religious liberty look like when a heavily Christian state applies its principles to a religious minority many voters know primarily through national political rhetoric?

---

## 2. The central oral-history question

The reporting should repeatedly return to one question:

> What does it mean to be both Muslim and American in Arkansas?

Secondary questions:

- What does Sharia mean in ordinary life?
- What parts of Islam are invisible to non-Muslim neighbors?
- What do Arkansas Muslims think American law should do when religious doctrine conflicts with civil law?
- What do they think about apostasy, blasphemy, hudud, jihad, women's rights, LGBTQ rights, democracy, and religious pluralism?
- Do they believe the Constitution protects their religion adequately?
- Have they experienced discrimination or unusual accommodation?
- What do they think Christian Arkansans misunderstand about them?
- What do they misunderstand about Christian Arkansans?
- What would they defend about American constitutionalism even if it protects conduct their religion considers sinful?

---

## 3. Interview sample

Target: 20–30 substantive interviews for the first reporting corpus, with approximately 12–20 potentially appearing in the finished narrative.

The sample should seek diversity rather than statistical representativeness.

### Arkansas Muslim voices

Seek, without assuming any individual's views:

1. An imam serving an Arkansas congregation.
2. A Muslim woman who wears hijab.
3. A Muslim woman who does not wear hijab.
4. A Black American Muslim with roots in Arkansas or the South.
5. A Muslim immigrant or first-generation American.
6. A second-generation Muslim Arkansan.
7. A convert to Islam.
8. A Muslim physician or healthcare professional.
9. A Muslim attorney or legally trained professional.
10. A Muslim small-business owner.
11. A Muslim veteran or military family member, if a willing participant can be found.
12. A university student or recent graduate.
13. A parent raising Muslim children in Arkansas.
14. A politically conservative Muslim.
15. A politically progressive Muslim.
16. A Muslim who is largely apolitical.
17. A Muslim involved in interfaith work.
18. A Muslim with substantial experience living in rural Arkansas rather than only Little Rock or Northwest Arkansas.
19. A Muslim who has encountered discrimination or a religious-accommodation dispute.
20. A Muslim who reports largely positive relationships with Christian neighbors.

### Internal critics and former Muslims

The book should not omit people whose experience complicates a religious-liberty defense.

Seek:

- a former Muslim willing to discuss leaving the faith;
- a Muslim reformer critical of coercive interpretations;
- a woman critical of gendered religious expectations;
- where feasible, someone with personal experience under coercive religious law outside the United States;
- a scholar or advocate able to discuss apostasy and freedom of conscience from an internal-critical perspective.

These participants should not be treated as representatives of all Muslims any more than practicing Muslims should.

### Non-Muslim Arkansas voices

Include:

- a Baptist or evangelical pastor committed to religious liberty for Muslims;
- a conservative Christian who has serious concerns about Sharia;
- a Jewish Arkansas religious leader;
- an interfaith organizer;
- a constitutional-law scholar or Arkansas attorney;
- a civil-liberties advocate;
- a corrections/prison-religion expert familiar with *Holt v. Hobbs*;
- where appropriate, a legislator or former legislator involved with Arkansas religious-liberty legislation.

The strongest chapter will contain disagreement rather than staged consensus.

---

## 4. Geographic architecture

Reporting should not reduce Arkansas Islam to one Little Rock mosque.

Priority geographic zones:

### Central Arkansas
Little Rock, North Little Rock, Jacksonville, Conway and surrounding communities.

### Northwest Arkansas
Fayetteville, Springdale, Rogers, Bentonville and university/corporate immigrant communities.

### Northeast Arkansas
Jonesboro and Arkansas State University-connected communities.

### Fort Smith / River Valley
Look for established Muslim institutional and professional communities.

### Rural Arkansas
The highest-value interviews may involve Muslims living outside large metropolitan communities, because the book's intended audience includes rural Christian readers.

Do not publish private home addresses or other unnecessary identifying information.

---

## 5. Institutional reporting targets

Potential public institutions to research and approach include established Islamic centers and mosques, university Muslim Student Associations, interfaith organizations, civil-liberties organizations, and Arkansas institutions involved in religious-accommodation questions.

Use public institutional contacts rather than scraping personal information.

The Islamic Center of Little Rock is historically important because its published institutional history documents decades of Arkansas Muslim community development and early Christian-Muslim interfaith cooperation.

Additional institutions should be verified individually before publication or outreach.

---

## 6. Interview sequence

Do not begin an interview with terrorism, hudud, or apostasy.

That reproduces the very distortion the book is investigating: asking a Muslim to begin by defending himself against the worst thing someone else associates with his religion.

Use five movements.

### Movement One — Person before doctrine

- Where did you grow up?
- Tell me about your family.
- How long have you lived in Arkansas?
- What do you love about living here?
- What does a normal week look like for you?
- What role does religion play in your ordinary life?

### Movement Two — Lived Islam

- When you hear the word “Sharia,” what does it mean to you personally?
- What parts of Sharia affect an ordinary Tuesday in your life?
- Does it influence prayer, food, money, marriage, charity, work, parenting, or something else?
- What religious practices would you be afraid to lose if government became hostile to Islam?
- Is there anything commonly called Sharia that you personally reject or interpret differently?

### Movement Three — America and Arkansas

- What does being American mean to you?
- What does being an Arkansan mean to you?
- Is there any conflict between those identities and being Muslim?
- What does the First Amendment mean to you?
- Should Christians be free to live according to Christian teaching?
- Should atheists be free to reject religion entirely?
- Should a Muslim ever receive a legal privilege that a Christian would not receive?
- Should a Christian receive one a Muslim would not receive?
- If your religious belief conflicts with a valid American law, what should happen?

### Movement Four — Hard cases

Ask respectfully and directly.

- Should a person be legally free to leave Islam?
- Should government ever punish someone for blaspheming Islam?
- Do you believe hudud punishments should ever be enforced by an American government?
- What does jihad mean to you, and do you think Muslims sometimes sanitize its historical military meaning when explaining it to non-Muslims?
- Do men and women have equal civil rights?
- Should non-Muslims have exactly the same citizenship rights as Muslims?
- Should an atheist be eligible to become president?
- Should a Muslim be eligible?
- Should American law ever be invalid simply because an Islamic scholar says it violates Sharia?
- What if a Christian pastor says the law violates the Bible?
- Are there interpretations of Islam you believe are dangerous?
- What responsibility do Muslims have to challenge them?

### Movement Five — Neighbor

End by returning from abstraction to relationship.

Ask every Muslim participant:

> What would you want a Christian neighbor in rural Arkansas to understand about your faith that you don't think they currently understand?

Then ask:

> What would you want to understand better about that Christian neighbor?

This reciprocal question is important. The project should not portray rural Christians merely as people who need correction.

---

## 7. Questions for former Muslims and internal critics

These interviews require special care because criticism of Islam can carry family, community, immigration, security, or reputational consequences.

Questions:

- What was Islam in your childhood or community actually like?
- What did you value about it?
- What harmed or constrained you?
- When did you begin questioning it?
- Were you free to leave?
- Did anyone threaten, shame, isolate, or economically pressure you?
- Which problems came from scripture, jurisprudence, culture, family, government, or a mixture?
- Do you distinguish Islam from Muslims?
- Do you distinguish criticism of Islamic doctrine from hostility toward Muslim citizens?
- What do Muslim reformers get right?
- What do they minimize?
- What do anti-Muslim political movements get right?
- What do they exaggerate?
- Should Muslims have the same religious liberty as Christians?
- What would genuine freedom of conscience require for both Muslims and former Muslims?

Key principle:

> A religious-liberty project that protects the Muslim must also protect the Muslim who stops believing.

This is constitutionally essential.

---

## 8. Questions for conservative Christian critics of Sharia

Do not invite caricature. Ask for the strongest version of the concern.

- When you say “Sharia,” what specifically do you mean?
- Which Islamic doctrines concern you most?
- What evidence leads you to believe those doctrines threaten Arkansas or the United States?
- Do you distinguish private Muslim religious practice from state-enforced Islamic law?
- Should Muslims be able to build mosques on the same zoning terms as churches?
- Should Muslim prisoners receive reasonable religious accommodations?
- Should a Muslim be eligible for every public office for which a Christian is eligible?
- Should Christians be allowed to use voluntary religious arbitration?
- Should Muslims?
- If a Muslim follows halal rules, prays five times daily, fasts during Ramadan, gives zakat, and structures private conduct around Islamic teaching while accepting constitutional supremacy, what is the public-policy objection?
- What constitutional rule would you adopt that prevents Islamic theocracy without also empowering Christian theocracy?

The last question is especially important.

---

## 9. The symmetry test

For every proposed restriction on Muslim religious practice, ask:

> Would we accept the same rule if the religion were Christianity?

For every proposed Muslim religious privilege, ask the reverse.

Examples:

- Mosque zoning ↔ church zoning
- Halal diet ↔ kosher/religious diet
- Muslim beard ↔ Christian/Sikh/Jewish religious grooming
- Islamic arbitration ↔ Christian mediation / Jewish beth din
- Qur'anic political conviction ↔ biblical political conviction
- Sharia supremacy ↔ biblical-law supremacy
- Muslim prayer ↔ Christian prayer
- Leaving Islam ↔ leaving Christianity

Symmetry does not automatically answer every constitutional question, but asymmetry is a warning that fear or favoritism may be doing work that neutral principle should do.

## 10. Reporting ethics

### No tokenization
Never write “Muslims believe…” from one interview.

Use formulations such as:

- “For [person], Sharia means…”
- “Several interviewees described…”
- “The Muslims interviewed for this project disagreed sharply about…”

### No representative burden
Participants do not have to answer for Saudi Arabia, Iran, ISIS, the Taliban, Hamas, or every Muslim jurist in history merely because they are Muslim.

Hard doctrinal questions remain legitimate, but frame them as questions about the participant's belief rather than presumptive complicity.

### Protect vulnerable participants
Discuss attribution before recording.

Possible attribution levels:

1. Full name and identifying details.
2. Name with limited personal details.
3. First name/pseudonym with reason disclosed to reader.
4. Background-only interview not quoted.

Never promise anonymity casually. Establish what can actually be protected before the interview.

### Trauma-aware interviewing
Former Muslims, refugees, immigrants, victims of religious coercion, hate crimes, or domestic/family pressure may be discussing trauma. Do not press for unnecessary details merely because they are dramatic.

### Translation
If translation is required, preserve original-language recordings/transcripts where possible and independently check quotations that become central evidence.

---

## 11. Evidence handling

Every interview should generate a standardized record:

- Interview ID
- Date
- Location/general region
- Interviewer
- Participant attribution agreement
- Recording permission
- Transcript status
- Biographical verification status
- Key claims requiring external verification
- Quotable passages
- Contradictions/uncertainties
- Relevant research-pass links
- Follow-up questions
- Publication permission notes

Separate three categories:

### Personal experience
The participant is authoritative about what happened to them, subject to normal reporting verification when allegations involve others.

### Religious interpretation
Attribute the interpretation to the speaker unless independently supported as a broader doctrinal position.

### Historical/legal/factual claim
Independently verify before publication.

A participant saying “Islam has always taught X” is an interview quote, not historical proof of X.

## 13. Narrative scenes to seek

The book needs scenes, not merely quotations.

Look for ordinary moments that reveal the constitutional question:

- dawn prayer before an Arkansas workday;
- a physician arranging prayer around patients;
- a Muslim parent packing school lunch during Ramadan;
- Friday prayer followed by ordinary work or family errands;
- a Muslim and Christian neighbor helping one another;
- a hijabi woman entering a rural Arkansas business;
- an interfaith gathering;
- a convert explaining Islam to Christian parents;
- a former Muslim explaining why freedom to leave matters;
- a Muslim attorney explaining where religious belief stops and civil law begins;
- a conservative Christian explaining an authentic fear of Islamic theocracy while nevertheless defending a mosque's First Amendment rights.

The reporting goal is to move “Sharia” from an abstract political noun to observable human conduct.

---

## 14. The opening-scene possibility

One potential narrative opening:

It is before sunrise in Arkansas.

An American Muslim wakes, washes, faces Mecca, and prays.

Nothing has been overthrown.

No court has been replaced.

No Constitution has disappeared.

And yet, in the believer's own vocabulary, this is Sharia.

The book can then ask how the same word came to mean, in American political rhetoric, an invading legal regime.

This scene should only be written from actual reporting, not invented composite detail.

---

## 15. *Holt v. Hobbs* oral-history opportunity

Research whether Gregory Holt / Abdul Maalik Muhammad, his attorneys, prison-religion experts, or people directly involved in the litigation are available for responsible historical interviewing.

Do not assume access or current circumstances.

Questions for litigation participants could include:

- Why did half an inch of beard matter religiously?
- Why did Arkansas resist?
- How did the case become a Supreme Court case?
- What did the unanimous ruling mean?
- Did participants understand the case as a Muslim-rights case, a general religious-liberty case, or both?
- What should Christians learn from it?

Potential narrative line:

> One of the clearest modern tests of Muslim religious liberty in Arkansas concerned not a caliphate, a criminal code, or a competing court. It concerned half an inch of hair.

---

## 16. Arkansas Christian-Muslim interfaith history

The Islamic Center of Little Rock's published history reports early Muslim participation in Jewish-Christian-Muslim dialogue and assistance from Second Presbyterian Church, including use of church facilities.

This should be independently reported through surviving participants, institutional archives, church records, local newspapers, photographs, minutes, newsletters, and oral histories where available.

Potential theme:

> Before “Sharia” became a national political scare word, some Arkansas Christians were already practicing religious liberty in the most ordinary possible way: their Muslim neighbors needed a room, so they opened the church door.

Do not publish the line as historical fact beyond what the records support.

## 17. The rural-Arkansas experiment

A high-value reporting experiment would bring the book's constitutional question into a rural setting without manufacturing confrontation.

Possible format:

- interview a rural Christian pastor;
- interview a Muslim Arkansan;
- separately establish their views;
- if both voluntarily agree, facilitate a recorded conversation about conscience, Sharia, Christianity, religious liberty, and constitutional limits.

Do not stage a debate for spectacle.

The objective is understanding rather than conversion.

Questions both participants should answer:

1. What does religious liberty mean?
2. What religious practice of the other person makes you uncomfortable?
3. Should that practice nevertheless be protected?
4. Where should government draw the line?
5. What would you fear if the other person's religion controlled the government?
6. What would you fear if government became hostile to your own religion?
7. Can the same constitutional rule solve both fears?

That final question may become the human version of the book's thesis.

---

## 18. What this pass must NOT become

Do not turn the project into:

- a mosque promotional campaign;
- an anti-Islam investigation;
- an interfaith public-relations exercise;
- a statistical survey based on a convenience sample;
- a debate over whether Islam is “really” peaceful;
- a demand that Muslims prove patriotism;
- a demand that Christians apologize for skepticism;
- a collection of only sympathetic interviewees.

The reporting standard is curiosity plus adversarial verification.

---

## 19. Core narrative findings to test, not assume

The interviews should test these hypotheses:

### Hypothesis A
For many Arkansas Muslims, Sharia primarily operates as personal religious and moral practice rather than a program to replace American civil government.

### Hypothesis B
American Muslims differ significantly among themselves about theology, politics, gender, sexuality, jurisprudence, and reform.

### Hypothesis C
Many non-Muslim Arkansans use “Sharia” to refer principally to criminal punishments or theocracy, creating a semantic mismatch with ordinary Muslim usage.

### Hypothesis D
Some conservative concerns about coercive Islamic jurisprudence are grounded in real doctrines and real foreign-state practices, but are often generalized to American Muslims without individualized evidence.

### Hypothesis E
Constitutional neutrality provides a common rule capable of protecting both conservative Christians and Muslims from government religious coercion.

### Hypothesis F
Freedom of conscience is incomplete unless it protects both entering and leaving a religion.

Every hypothesis is falsifiable. If interviews contradict it, change the book.

## 20. Publication architecture

Potential oral-history material can feed several chapters rather than one isolated “Muslim voices” chapter.

Examples:

- A dawn-prayer scene can open the definition of Sharia.
- An Arkansas Muslim attorney can anchor the constitutional-supremacy discussion.
- A former Muslim can anchor apostasy and conscience.
- A Christian pastor can anchor the symmetry test.
- A Muslim woman can complicate abstract Western arguments about gender.
- An immigrant from a coercive religious state can distinguish Islam from state Islamization.
- An interfaith Arkansas story can connect historical constitutional theory to lived community.
- *Holt* can anchor Arkansas religious liberty.

This avoids segregating Muslims into a single testimonial chapter after the “real” history has already been told.

## 21. Proposed fieldwork workflow

### Phase A — Institutional mapping
Verify Arkansas Muslim institutions, university organizations, interfaith organizations, scholars, attorneys, and public contacts.

### Phase B — Pilot interviews
Conduct 4–6 interviews representing sharply different perspectives. Test the interview guide and revise it.

### Phase C — Arkansas corpus
Conduct approximately 20–30 substantive interviews across regions and viewpoints.

### Phase D — Contradiction pass
Identify claims or themes missing from the sample and intentionally seek people likely to disagree.

### Phase E — Verification
Fact-check all publishable factual claims and allegations.

### Phase F — Narrative extraction
Identify scenes, quotes, conflicts, and recurring themes without flattening minority views.

### Phase G — Participant accuracy check
For highly technical or sensitive quotations, consider checking quotation accuracy/context with the participant without surrendering editorial control or offering story approval.

## 22. The constitutional heart of the oral history

The interviews should eventually allow the reader to see the same constitutional principle from opposing directions.

The Muslim says:

> I don't want government telling me I cannot practice my religion.

The Christian says:

> I don't want government forcing another religion on me.

The former Muslim says:

> I don't want religious authority preventing me from leaving.

The constitutional answer can protect all three.

That is the central reporting insight of Pass 19.

> “A religious-liberty project that protects the Muslim must also protect the Muslim who stops believing.”

> “The strongest chapter will contain disagreement rather than staged consensus.”

> “The political argument starts with Sharia at the courthouse. The Muslim answer may start with the alarm clock before dawn.”

> “The question is not whether your neighbor's religion makes you comfortable. The question is what government may do about his discomforting belief.”

> “The same Constitution that keeps Sharia from becoming compulsory law keeps Christianity from becoming compulsory law—and protects both religions while they remain voluntary faith.”

## 24. Pass 19 conclusion

The documentary record can establish what founders wrote, what constitutions say, what jurists taught, what statutes require, and what courts held.

It cannot tell us what Sharia means when an Arkansas Muslim wakes up tomorrow morning.

Only people can do that.

The next reporting phase should therefore move deliberately from archives to kitchens, offices, mosques, churches, campuses, courtrooms, farms, and ordinary Arkansas lives.

The project's strongest possible conclusion cannot be predetermined. It has to survive contact with the people whose liberty it claims to defend.

Working final line:

> Religious liberty stops being an abstraction when the person protected by it is sitting across the table from you.
