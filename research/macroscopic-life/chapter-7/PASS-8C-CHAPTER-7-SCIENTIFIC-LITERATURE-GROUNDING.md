## 1. Long-distance signaling is real biology

### Choi et al. 2016
Won-Gyu Choi, Richard Hilleary, Sarah J. Swanson, Su-Hwa Kim, Simon Gilroy. `Rapid, Long-Distance Electrical and Calcium Signaling in Plants.` Annual Review of Plant Biology 67:287–307. DOI: 10.1146/annurev-arplant-043015-112130.

Use: strong established precedent that stimuli sensed locally in plants can be converted into mobile electrical, calcium, reactive-oxygen, chemical, and related signals that influence distant tissues and systemic responses.

Limit: this supports organism-level plant signaling because the plant is already an independently established biological individual. It does not support forest-level or planetary ownership of those signals.

### Suda et al. 2026
Hiraku Suda, Takuma Hagihara, Satoshi Ogawa, Kazuo Ebine, Masayoshi Nakamura, Masatsugu Toyota. `Long-Range Signals Built upon Plant Structural Continuity.` Annual Review of Plant Biology 77:503–527. DOI: 10.1146/annurev-arplant-070225-024248.

Use: current review of plant long-range signaling through plasmodesmata, vascular structures, electrical potentials, calcium signals, chemical mechanisms, and hydraulic pathways.

Limit: structural continuity and signal propagation are not themselves evidence of higher-level agency.

### Van Norman et al. 2013
`Spatiotemporal signalling in plant development.` Nature Reviews Genetics.

Use: supports long-range and local signaling classes in plant development, including phytohormones and mobile regulatory molecules.

## 2. Mechanistic communication control: bacterial quorum sensing

### Miller & Bassler 2001
Melissa B. Miller and Bonnie L. Bassler. `Quorum Sensing in Bacteria.` Annual Review of Microbiology 55:165–199. DOI: 10.1146/annurev.micro.55.1.165.

Use: canonical example of chemically mediated cell-to-cell communication in which bacteria produce, release, detect, and respond to autoinducers, altering gene expression in population-density-dependent ways.

### Jayaraman & Wood 2008
Arul Jayaraman and Thomas K. Wood. `Bacterial Quorum Sensing: Signals, Circuits, and Implications for Biofilms and Disease.` Annual Review of Biomedical Engineering 10:145–167. DOI: 10.1146/annurev.bioeng.10.061807.160536.

Use: supports source/signal/receptor/response architecture and signal specificity.

### Wang, Payne & Bentley 2020
Sally Wang, Gregory F. Payne, William E. Bentley. `Quorum Sensing Communication: Molecularly Connecting Cells, Their Neighbors, and Even Devices.` Annual Review of Chemical and Biomolecular Engineering 11:447–468. DOI: 10.1146/annurev-chembioeng-101519-124728.

Use: modern mechanistic review connecting signaling to regulated response, synthetic circuits, interspecies signaling, and engineered tests.

Limit: terms such as "decision" in engineering or microbiology literature should not be imported into Macroscopic Life as evidence of conscious or higher-level agency.

## 3. Common mycorrhizal networks: use as an adversarial case, not a shortcut

### Barto et al. / Advances in Botanical Research 2017
`Plant–Plant Communication Through Common Mycorrhizal Networks.` DOI: 10.1016/bs.abr.2016.09.001.

Use: documents evidence and hypotheses concerning transfer of plant-produced signals through common mycorrhizal networks and emphasizes that signal identity and plant/fungal control can remain uncertain.

### Fungal Biology Reviews 2012
`Mycorrhizal networks: Mechanisms, ecology and modelling.` DOI: 10.1016/j.fbr.2012.01.001.

Use: supports the physical existence of common mycorrhizal networks and literature on transfer of carbon, nutrients, water, defense-related signals, and allelochemicals.

### Nature Plants 2024 — Mycoheterotrophy in the wood-wide web

Use: important restraint source. It explicitly notes that popular descriptions of common mycorrhizal networks have sometimes produced hyperbole and that several reviews have challenged broad claims about widespread resource and information transfer, while arguing that some network-mediated carbon transfer is well supported in particular biological contexts.

Chapter rule: network connection, material transfer, and correlated plant response must never be treated as automatic evidence that a forest-level individual is communicating.

## 4. Collective behavior without a control room

### Gordon 2019
Deborah M. Gordon. `The Ecology of Collective Behavior in Ants.` Annual Review of Entomology 64:35–50. DOI: 10.1146/annurev-ento-011118-111923.

Use: supports distributed collective behavior emerging through local interaction without requiring a centralized controller. Appropriate as a precedent for system-level behavioral regularity without implying a unified mind.

Limit: ant-colony collective behavior cannot simply be transferred by analogy to forests, cities, or planets.

## 5. Synchronization and resonance doctrine

Chapter 7 may rely on standard dynamical-systems physics for the following baseline propositions:

- coupled oscillators can synchronize;
- resonance is increased response under specified forcing conditions;
- phase-locking or synchronous change can arise through common forcing or coupling;
- synchronization alone does not establish communication, semantic content, agency, individuality, or consciousness.

No specialist citation is required merely to establish these textbook-level propositions unless the final prose introduces a narrow empirical biological example.

Permanent brake:

`FREQUENCY IS A MEASUREMENT, NOT AN EXPLANATION.`

`SYNCHRONIZATION IS A DYNAMIC RELATION BEFORE IT IS A COMMUNICATION CLAIM.`

## 6. Common-driver doctrine

A correlated or synchronous change between distant components can arise from:

- shared environmental forcing;
- direct or indirect coupling;
- common upstream control;
- spatial autocorrelation;
- shared seasonality;
- sampling and lag artifacts;
- post hoc boundary selection;
- hidden variables.

These are standard rival classes. The specific Macroscopic Life requirement that a candidate communication claim be attacked by the strongest plausible common-driver model is PROJECT SYNTHESIS.

## 10. Information doctrine

Chapter 4 already separated statistical, functional, and semantic information. Chapter 7 should use that separation conservatively.

Safe claims:

- statistical dependence does not imply semantic meaning;
- a signal can be physically propagated without establishing semantic content;
- experimentally perturbing a signal and observing a selective downstream consequence can support a functional signaling interpretation;
- semantic or representational interpretations require additional commitments and should not be inferred casually.

Project signature candidate:

`INFORMATION CAN EXIST FOR THE SCIENTIST BEFORE IT EXISTS FOR THE SYSTEM.`

## 11. False-language control

The chapter should include an engineered common-driver example in which many devices change together because one external input drives them independently. This is a thought experiment/control example, not empirical evidence.

Purpose: demonstrate that synchronized population behavior can look communicative even when there is no component-to-component message exchange.

`A PATTERN CAN CROSS A SYSTEM WITHOUT THE SYSTEM SENDING A MESSAGE.`

## 12. Perturbation ladder for a candidate signal

Recommended Chapter 7 experimental logic:

1. Detection — reproducible physical variable.
2. Propagation — measurable spatial/temporal transmission or coupling.
3. Encoding — signal variation covaries reliably with relevant states.
4. Perturbation — manipulating the candidate signal changes downstream response.
5. Specificity — different signal states produce discriminable responses under controls.
6. Function — response contributes to measurable organization or performance.
7. Ownership — function belongs nontrivially to the proposed higher-level organization.
8. Prediction Advantage — higher-level informational description prospectively improves comparison with strongest rival.
9. Replication — survives new times, sites, implementations, and investigators.

Status: PROJECT SYNTHESIS INFORMED BY ESTABLISHED EXPERIMENTAL PRACTICE.
