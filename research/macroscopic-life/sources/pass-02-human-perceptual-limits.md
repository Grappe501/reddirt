# Research Pass 02 — Human Perceptual Limits

## Objective

Establish the second evidentiary pillar of Book One: the human nervous system samples narrow, biologically constrained channels of environmental information, while instruments and sensory-substitution technologies demonstrate that useful information can be translated into channels the brain can learn to use.

Supports:
- Act I, Chapter 3 — *The Human Interface*
- Act I transition into dimensional and embedded-observer questions
- ML-002 and ML-021

## Core Finding

> Human perception is selective rather than comprehensive. Our biological receptors respond to limited ranges and forms of physical stimulation, and the nervous system constructs useful perceptual representations from those inputs.

This is not merely philosophical. Human vision and hearing have measurable response ranges, and sensory-substitution/augmentation research demonstrates that environmental information normally unavailable through one channel can be translated into another channel and learned.

---

# 1. The Visible Window

Human vision is sensitive to only a limited wavelength band of electromagnetic radiation. A neuroscience review describes approximate human visual sensitivity around 380–750 nm, while broader electromagnetic radiation extends far beyond this band in both directions.

The exact boundaries of "visible light" are not perfectly sharp: sensitivity varies with intensity, observer, and convention. Therefore Book One should use approximate ranges rather than imply a rigid biological wall.

### Evidence class
E1.

### Book meaning

The sky is full of electromagnetic radiation that is physically present but not represented as ordinary human color or vision. Infrared cameras, ultraviolet detectors, radio telescopes, X-ray observatories, and other instruments do not create those phenomena; they convert otherwise inaccessible information into forms humans can inspect.

### Source anchors
- Sliney, D. H. (2016), *What is light? The visible spectrum and beyond*, Eye. PMCID: PMC4763133. PMID: 26768917.
- *How We Hear: The Perception and Neural Coding of Sound*, review noting approximate human visual sensitivity of 380–750 nm. PMCID: PMC5819010.

---

# 2. The Audible Window

Young humans with healthy hearing are commonly described as detecting frequencies from roughly 20 Hz to 20 kHz, with the upper limit generally declining with age. Sensitivity is not uniform across that range.

Other mammals occupy different acoustic windows. Some bats detect frequencies far above the ordinary human upper limit.

### Evidence class
E1.

### Book meaning

Silence is partly observer-relative. A sound wave can be physically present while falling outside a listener's auditory sensitivity.

This gives Book One a particularly clean analogy:

> "I hear nothing" is a statement about the observer and the signal together, not necessarily a statement that no acoustic energy exists.

### Source anchors
- NCBI Bookshelf, *The Audible Spectrum*, Neuroscience, 2nd ed.
- *How We Hear: The Perception and Neural Coding of Sound*, PMCID: PMC5819010.
- *Extended high frequency hearing and speech perception implications in adults and children*, PMCID: PMC7431381.

# 3. Detection Is Not the Same as Conscious Experience

A receptor's physical sensitivity is only the beginning of perception. Signals are transformed, filtered, combined, prioritized, interpreted, and related to action by the nervous system.

Book One should therefore avoid the simplistic metaphor that senses are cameras recording reality. A better model is an adaptive interface: sensory organs transduce selected environmental variables and nervous systems build representations useful for behavior.

### Evidence class
E1/E2 depending on the specific perceptual theory.

### Guardrail

Do not imply neuroscience has settled one universal philosophical theory of perception. The empirical point is narrower: sensory transduction and neural processing are selective and constructive processes, not exhaustive measurement of the environment.

---

# 4. Attention Narrows the Window Further

Even information reaching sensory systems is not all equally available to conscious awareness or action. Perceptual systems have finite processing capacity; sensory-substitution literature explicitly discusses attentional capacity and information overload as constraints.

### Book meaning

There are at least three filters:

1. What physical information exists.
2. What the biological receptors can transduce.
3. What the nervous system selects, integrates, and makes behaviorally useful.

This makes "human reality" a filtered model rather than an exhaustive census of the physical environment.

### Source anchor
- Kristjánsson et al. (2016), *Designing sensory-substitution devices: Principles, pitfalls and potential*, PMCID: PMC5044782. PMID: 27567755.

---

# 5. Instruments Are Translation Machines

Scientific instruments extend human knowledge by detecting variables outside unaided sensory ranges and translating measurements into visible images, audible signals, numbers, graphs, haptic patterns, or other representations.

A radio telescope does not give the retina a new photopigment. It detects radio-frequency electromagnetic radiation and converts measurements into a representation humans can analyze.

An infrared camera similarly translates infrared measurements into visible display values.

### Book meaning

Technology demonstrates something philosophically important: humanity already accepts that reality contains information we cannot directly sense. Much of modern science depends on indirect measurement.

### Evidence class
E1 as a description of instrumentation.

### Important limitation

Instruments do not remove every observational limitation. They have sensitivity thresholds, calibration requirements, model assumptions, noise, finite resolution, and domain-specific constraints.

---

# 6. Sensory Substitution

Sensory-substitution systems take environmental information normally associated with one sensory channel and encode it into another—for example, translating visual-camera information into tactile or auditory patterns.

Research shows that users can learn to employ such translated information for object recognition, localization, navigation, and action.

### Why this matters enormously for Macroscopic Life

It demonstrates that a nervous system can extract meaningful structure from environmental information delivered through an unconventional interface.

The deep lesson is not that humans can magically gain unlimited senses. The lesson is that the brain's usable world depends partly on the information channel made available to it.

### Evidence class
E1.

### Source anchors
- Bach-y-Rita & Kercel (2003), *Sensory substitution and the human-machine interface*. PMID: 14643370.
- Proulx et al. (2016), *Other ways of seeing...*, PMCID: PMC4927905. PMID: 26599473.
- Kristjánsson et al. (2016), PMCID: PMC5044782.

# 7. Sensory Expansion and Sensory Addition

Modern research distinguishes among:

- **substitution** — replacing information from a missing/impaired modality;
- **expansion** — extending an existing modality's informational range;
- **addition** — providing a new informational signal, potentially one humans do not naturally possess as a conscious sense.

Recent haptic research explicitly discusses widening experience to electromagnetic information outside visible light and supplying magnetic-direction information through wearables.

### Evidence class
E1 for existence of these research programs and learned device use; E2 for how completely novel signals become integrated into adult subjective perception.

### Source anchors
- Novich & Eagleman / related sensory augmentation literature summarized in *The future of sensory substitution, addition, and expansion via haptic devices*, PMCID: PMC9880183. PMID: 36712151.
- Nardini et al. (2024), *Towards Human Sensory Augmentation*, PMCID: PMC11533871. PMID: 39497728.

# 8. The Interface Principle

Passes 01 and 02 together support a stronger formulation:

> Perception is an interface between an organism and selected information in its environment. Change the receptors, translation mechanism, processing, or available signal and the organism's usable world can change.

This does not require claiming that ordinary experience is unreal. A graphical interface on a computer is real and useful even though it does not display every transistor state beneath it.

The analogy should be used carefully but is powerful for general readers.

---

# 9. A Hierarchy of Human Access

Book One can distinguish four levels:

### Level 1 — Directly sensed
Visible light, audible sound, pressure, temperature, chemical stimuli, proprioception, vestibular information, etc., within biological limits.

### Level 2 — Biologically consequential but not necessarily consciously sensed as such
Physical or chemical variables may affect biological systems without appearing as a distinct conscious perceptual modality.

### Level 3 — Instrumentally detected
Signals outside unaided human sensory access can be measured and translated by scientific instruments.

### Level 4 — Currently undetected or unknown
Possible phenomena for which humanity presently has no validated detector or established evidence.

### Critical rule

Level 4 cannot be inferred merely because Levels 1–3 exist.

The existence of ultraviolet light before humans could measure it is not evidence that every proposed invisible phenomenon is real.

It establishes only that epistemic humility is rational.

---

# 10. Strongest Skeptical Interpretation

A skeptic can accept every fact in Passes 01–02 without accepting Macroscopic Life.

The conventional explanation is straightforward:

- sensory systems evolved to detect information useful for survival and reproduction;
- no organism needs to detect every physical variable;
- instruments extend measurement;
- hidden macroscopic organisms are not required to explain sensory limitations.

**This skeptical interpretation is correct as far as the current evidence goes.**

Macroscopic Life therefore cannot use perceptual limitation as positive evidence for a larger organism.

It uses perceptual limitation to defeat one premature objection:

> "If something larger existed, surely we would simply see it."

That objection is no longer sufficient by itself.

# 11. Narrative Engineering — Chapter 3

Open with darkness.

A person says, "There is nothing there."

Then introduce an infrared camera. The blank room gains thermal structure.

Turn on an ultrasonic detector. Another layer appears.

Add a magnetometer. Another variable becomes measurable.

Add a radio receiver. Signals fill what seemed empty.

The room did not change.

**The interface changed.**

Then move from machines back to the body:

> Human beings did not evolve as universal detectors. We evolved as survivors.

The chapter should explain that science itself is, in part, the history of building interfaces to realities our bodies cannot directly perceive.

Then introduce sensory substitution: even the route by which information reaches the brain can sometimes be changed.

Final turn:

> The dangerous question is not whether invisible things exist. Of course invisible things exist; modern physics measures them every day. The dangerous question is how much of reality remains outside every interface we have yet built.

Immediately add the guardrail:

> That question is not permission to believe anything. It is permission to keep looking.
