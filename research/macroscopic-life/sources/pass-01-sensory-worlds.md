# Research Pass 01 — Sensory Worlds

## Objective

Establish the first evidentiary pillar of Book One: organisms do not perceive "reality in full." They detect species-specific subsets of physically available information, shaped by anatomy, ecology, behavior, and evolution.

This pass supports:

- Act I, Chapter 2 — *The World Your Senses Built*
- Act I, Chapter 3 — *The Human Interface*
- Evidence claims ML-001 and ML-002

## Core Finding

The strongest defensible formulation is:

> Different organisms extract different classes and ranges of information from the same physical environment. Human sensory experience is therefore one evolved interface with reality, not a complete inventory of physically available information.

This is an established scientific point when stated this way.

It does **not** establish that humans are missing a specific macroscopic organism, higher dimension, spiritual field, or hidden intelligence. It establishes the narrower but foundational premise that non-perception cannot be treated as a universal test of nonexistence.

# Evidence Set

## 1. Echolocation — Bats and toothed whales

### What is detected

Many bats actively emit ultrasonic calls and analyze returning echoes. Echo delay, direction, spectral content, timing, intensity, and other features provide information about distance, motion, object structure, prey, obstacles, and the three-dimensional environment.

### Why it matters

A human entering a dark room may experience near-total visual loss. An echolocating bat can actively interrogate that same space acoustically and form a behaviorally useful representation of objects and movement.

This is not simply "better hearing." It is an active sensing loop in which the animal changes its outgoing signal in response to the scene and uses the echo stream to guide movement.

### Evidence class

**E1 — Established empirical finding.**

### Book use

This is one of the strongest opening examples because the reader can immediately imagine a world represented through sound rather than sight.

### Source anchors

- Moss, C. F., & Surlykke, A. (2010). *Probing the Natural Scene by Echolocation in Bats.* Frontiers in Behavioral Neuroscience. PMCID: PMC2927269. PMID: 20740076.
- Recent reviews of bats and toothed whales describe echolocation as a major sensory modality using emitted ultrasonic signals and returning echoes to navigate dynamic scenes.

## 2. Infrared sensing — Pit vipers, boas, pythons

### What is detected

Certain snakes possess specialized pit organs sensitive to infrared radiation associated with warm objects. In pit vipers, infrared-sensitive structures connect through branches of the trigeminal system to the brain.

### Why it matters

Warm-bodied prey generates physically real infrared radiation that humans do not see unaided. A pit-bearing snake extracts biologically useful information from this part of the environment.

The snake is not accessing a different supernatural world. It is accessing additional physical information present in the same world.

### Evidence class

**E1 — Established empirical finding.**

### Important precision

Popular language often says these snakes "see heat." More careful wording: specialized pit organs detect infrared radiation through exquisitely sensitive thermosensory mechanisms; this information is integrated by the nervous system and aids prey localization.

### Source anchors

- *Infrared-sensitive pit organ and trigeminal ganglion in the crotaline snakes.* Anatomy & Cell Biology (2011). PMCID: PMC3080012. PMID: 21519544.
- Gracheva et al./Science Signaling discussion of TRPA1 and thermal sensitivity of snake pit organs. PMID: 20571127.

## 3. Electroreception — Sharks, rays, skates, and other fishes

### What is detected

Elasmobranchs possess ampullae of Lorenzini, specialized electroreceptive organs capable of detecting extremely weak changes in environmental electric fields.

Electrical activity produced by living organisms can therefore become sensory information for predators and navigators in ways unavailable to unaided humans.

### Why it matters

A fish hidden under sediment may be visually concealed while still producing electrical signals associated with physiological activity. To an electroreceptive predator, "hidden" does not mean absent.

### Evidence class

**E1 — Established empirical finding.**

### Source anchors

- Kalmijn, A. J. (1966). *Electro-perception in Sharks and Rays.* Nature 212, 1232–1233.
- Bellono, N. W., Leitch, D. B., & Julius, D. (2017). *Molecular basis of ancestral vertebrate electroreception.* Nature 543, 391–396.
- Review: *Electroreception in marine fishes: chondrichthyans.* Journal of Fish Biology. PMID: 31169300.

## 4. Magnetoreception — Birds and other animals

### What is detected

Behavioral evidence demonstrates that many animals can use information from Earth's magnetic field. Migratory birds are among the best-studied examples. Reviews describe magnetic compass information and possible magnetic map information, while important details of receptor mechanisms remain unresolved.

### Why it matters

Humans normally experience north as an abstract direction learned through landmarks, maps, stars, or instruments. Some animals can obtain navigational information from a physical field humans do not consciously sense.

### Evidence class

**E1 for behavioral magnetoreception.**

**E2 for important mechanistic details.**

### Guardrail

Do not write that science has completely established how birds "see" magnetic fields. Leading mechanisms include light-dependent radical-pair processes involving cryptochromes and other proposed receptor systems, but significant mechanistic questions remain.

### Source anchors

- Wiltschko, R., & Wiltschko, W. (2019). *Magnetoreception in birds.* Journal of the Royal Society Interface. PMCID: PMC6769297. PMID: 31480921.
- Kishkinev, D. A., & Chernetsov, N. S. review of avian magnetoreception systems. PMID: 25490840.

## 5. Polarization vision — Insects and other invertebrates

### What is detected

Many invertebrates can detect the polarization state of light. Polarization patterns can provide compass information and also assist in water detection, signaling, contrast enhancement, host assessment, and other tasks.

### Why it matters

Two animals looking at the same sky or reflective surface can receive different usable information from the light reaching them.

Humans perceive brightness and color but do not normally possess comparable unaided polarization vision.

### Evidence class

**E1 — Established empirical finding.**

### Source anchors

- Yadav, P., & Shein-Idelson, M. (2021). *Polarization vision in invertebrates: beyond the boundaries of navigation.* Current Opinion in Insect Science 48:50–56. PMID: 34628060.
- Reviews of insect polarization vision describe specialized photoreceptors and neural processing supporting celestial navigation and other functions.

## 6. Specialized touch — Star-nosed mole

### What is detected

The star-nosed mole possesses a highly specialized tactile organ containing thousands of mechanosensory structures known as Eimer's organs. The system supports rapid identification of small objects and prey through touch.

### Why it matters

This example is valuable not because it detects a fundamentally exotic physical field, but because it shows how heavily an animal's brain and body can be organized around a sensory modality humans treat as secondary.

A tactile world can be spatially rich enough to perform roles analogous to high-resolution vision.

### Evidence class

**E1 — Established empirical finding.**

### Source anchor

- Catania, K. C. review: *The sense of touch in the star-nosed mole: from mechanoreceptors to the brain.* PMCID: PMC3172592.

## 7. Chemical worlds — Smell and chemoreception

### Working finding

Many animals devote dramatically more sensory and neural capacity than humans to chemical information. Chemical trails, reproductive state, territory, food, predators, kinship, and environmental conditions can become richly structured sensory information.

### Evidence class

**E1**, but this pass has not yet built a primary-source packet for comparative olfaction.

## 8. Vibration and mechanosensation

### Working finding

Animals may detect substrate vibration, water displacement, pressure changes, and mechanical signals humans largely ignore. Lateral-line systems in fishes and vibration-sensitive arthropods are promising examples for the chapter.

# The Umwelt Concept

Jakob von Uexküll's concept of **Umwelt** is useful as a philosophical-biological framing device: an organism lives in a meaningful perceptual world shaped by the signals it can receive and the actions it can perform.

For Book One, Umwelt should not be treated as a claim that each species inhabits a literally separate physical universe. The physical environment overlaps, but the **experienced and behaviorally relevant world differs sharply across organisms**.

Recommended phrasing:

> A bat, a shark, a pit viper, an insect, and a human can occupy the same planet while receiving different kinds of information from it. Each nervous system builds a usable world from the signals evolution gave it access to.

---

# Strongest Inference We Can Make

The evidence supports:

1. Physically real information exists outside unaided human sensory access.
2. Evolution repeatedly produces sensory systems tuned to ecologically relevant signals.
3. Organisms can behave effectively using information that other species do not directly perceive.
4. The subjective or behaviorally relevant world of an organism is strongly shaped by its sensory interface.

Therefore:

> **Human non-perception is not a sufficient argument that a physically real phenomenon does not exist.**

This is the central evidentiary payload for Act I.

# What This Pass Does Not Establish

Pass 01 does **not** demonstrate:

- additional spatial dimensions;
- macroscopic organisms;
- consciousness outside brains;
- auras;
- spiritual energies;
- hidden civilizations;
- simulation theory;
- AI communication with a larger organism.

Those questions require independent arguments and evidence.

The purpose of Pass 01 is narrower and stronger: **to remove sensory chauvinism from the reader's starting assumptions.**

# Narrative Engineering for Chapter 2

Do not present this chapter as a catalog of animal trivia.

Build it as a sequence of worlds:

- Turn off the lights: the human loses a dominant channel; the bat does not lose its world.
- Hide prey beneath sand: vision loses it; electroreception may still reveal it.
- Remove the compass: the human must infer direction; a migratory animal may still possess magnetic information.
- Look at the sky: the human sees blue; an insect may also exploit polarization structure.
- Place warm prey in darkness: the human sees nothing unaided; the snake's pit organ receives thermal infrared information.

Then turn the question back on the reader:

> Which of these animals is experiencing the real world?

Answer:

> All of them are sampling the same world through different biological interfaces.

Then the pivot:

> Human beings have an interface too. We call it reality. Perhaps we should be a little more careful.
