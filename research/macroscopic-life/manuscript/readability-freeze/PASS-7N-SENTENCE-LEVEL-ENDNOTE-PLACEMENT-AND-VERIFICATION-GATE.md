Endnotes should be sparse enough for a readable popular-science book but strong enough that every thesis-bearing specialist factual claim can be traced to an appropriate literature family.

## Endnote doctrine

Use an endnote when a sentence or compact paragraph does one or more of the following:
1. makes a specialist empirical claim;
2. makes a factual claim central to the argument's bridge from established science to the project's hypothesis;
3. invokes a disputed mechanism or plural scholarly framework;
4. makes a quantitative claim;
5. would reasonably cause an expert reader to ask, `What literature establishes that?`

Do not endnote:
- thought experiments as if they were observations;
- rhetorical questions;
- project-created terminology merely to make it appear externally authorized;
- logical cautions such as `correlation does not establish communication` unless a specialist methodological source materially helps;
- signature lines whose authority comes from the argument rather than an external source.
