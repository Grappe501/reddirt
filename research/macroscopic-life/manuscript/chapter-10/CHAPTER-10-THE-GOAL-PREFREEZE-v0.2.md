# Chapter 10 — The Goal

A marble rests at the bottom of a bowl.

Push it halfway up one side and let go. It rolls down, crosses the bottom, climbs the opposite wall, returns, and eventually settles close to where it began. Push it from another direction and the route changes, but the destination does not. Start it higher. Start it lower. Give it a harder shove. Within limits, the same landscape keeps drawing the motion toward the same place.

It is difficult to watch this happen without importing a verb.

The marble returns. The marble seeks the bottom. The marble prefers the bottom.

Did the marble want the bottom?

Of course not. Gravity, geometry, friction, and the marble's initial motion were enough. The bottom mattered to the trajectory, but nothing in that fact requires the marble to detect a deviation or act with respect to a target. The shape of the system can produce convergence all by itself.

**RETURN IS NOT YET A GOAL.**

The word *yet* matters. In dynamical systems, trajectories can converge toward states or regimes we describe as attractors. In biology and philosophy, there are legitimate traditions that use goal-directed or teleonomic language for feedback, regulation, self-maintenance, and related forms of organization.[1] We do not need to outlaw that language. We need to ask a harder question: when does a proposed goal belong to the organization of the system we are studying?

## A Real Target

We have met the thermostat before. In Chapter 4 it taught us how easily our verbs can outrun a mechanism. Now it returns for a different reason.

Set a thermostat to seventy-two degrees. The control architecture is physically sensitive to a reference condition or rule; alter that reference and the controller's behavior changes. Depending on the design, this can be implemented through mechanical switching, hysteretic control, digital logic, or more elaborate feedback. Disconnect the relevant sensing or control pathway and regulation fails in predictable ways.

This is more interesting than the marble. It also prevents us from making the opposite mistake. Goal-directed control does not require consciousness. A controller can act with respect to a target without feeling cold, wanting warmth, imagining tomorrow, or knowing that it exists.

But the thermostat gives us another problem. Who chose seventy-two?

A person may have entered the setting. An engineer designed the architecture that can use it. The controller implements a reference condition, but the reference was not necessarily authored by the controller. A target can therefore be real, physically consequential, and nonconscious while still raising a question about ownership.

Living systems make that question much harder. Bodies regulate temperature, hydration, glucose, ionic conditions, and many other variables important to continued organization. Walter Cannon's homeostasis framework made physiological regulation explicit nearly a century ago. In influential allostasis frameworks, successful regulation need not mean returning every variable to one fixed value: operating conditions can change with context, and present action can prepare for expected demand.[2] The terminology and scope of allostasis remain debated, so this is not a claim that physiology has converged on one universal regulatory theory. The narrower point is enough: one variable can move while another constraint remains within a viability-relevant range.

That changes the experiment.

If a system repeatedly returns toward one state, we can ask whether the return is passive or actively regulated. But if several candidate constraints cannot all be preserved at once, the system faces a more revealing test.

Which one gives way?

## What Will It Sacrifice?

Imagine a living system pushed into a condition where two constraints conflict. Preserving one variable perfectly would drive another outside its pre-specified range. There is not enough energy, water, time, material, or control capacity to do everything.

Now recovery alone tells us less. The revealing question is what fails first.

A proposed hierarchy should predict the answer before the outcome is known. Under one disturbance, the system may tolerate a large deviation in A while B remains constrained. Under another, it may recruit a different mechanism while B again remains within range. Under a third, B may fail as a different constraint is preserved.

This does not establish consciousness or agency. Fixed network architecture, energetic scarcity, differential fragility, evolutionary history, local feedback, and hard-wired priority structure can all generate prospective tradeoffs. Predicting sacrifice is evidence for organized priority structure. It becomes evidence for higher-level goal ownership only if a higher-level target model adds prediction or intervention power beyond comparably rich mechanistic priority models.

**A GOAL IDENTIFIED ONLY AFTER THE OUTCOME IS A STORY UNTIL IT PREDICTS THE NEXT TRADEOFF.**

That gives us a way to force competing descriptions apart without assuming that the system has already chosen anything.

## The Body That Rebuilds

Some of the most provocative examples come from development and regeneration.

Cut living tissue and repair begins. Alter developmental conditions and cells may compensate. In some regenerative systems, large-scale anatomical outcomes can be restored despite substantial perturbation to components and routes by which the outcome is produced. Research on morphogenesis and bioelectric signaling has examined how membrane-potential patterns, gap-junction communication, biochemical signals, mechanical interactions, and gene-regulatory processes participate in distributed anatomical patterning.[3]

The temptation is immediate. Somewhere, we want to say, the body must know what shape it is supposed to be.

That sentence is stronger than the experiment.

Terms such as *target morphology* and *anatomical homeostasis* are especially prominent in a particular research program associated with work on regenerative pattern control. They should not be mistaken for settled field-wide ontology. They can nevertheless be productive experimental ideas: they direct attention toward large-scale outcomes and variables that can be missed if we inspect only one cell at a time. Restoration of form does not automatically establish a stored blueprint, symbolic representation, intelligence, or system-owned goal.

Target morphology should therefore enter this book as an experimental hypothesis before it enters as an ontology.

That restraint does not make regeneration less astonishing. No individual cell need contain a literal, complete spatial description of the finished anatomy for distributed processes to build or restore coherent form. Different local mechanisms can contribute to a large-scale outcome. The causal organization can be real while the strongest interpretation remains unsettled.

> **THE WHOLE CAN SOLVE A PROBLEM THAT NO SINGLE COMPONENT SOLVES ALONE.**

Here *solve* is functional language: distributed interactions achieve a constrained outcome under perturbation. It does not imply cognition, awareness, or an internal problem statement.

Biological robustness makes the same warning necessary. Feedback, redundancy, degeneracy, modular organization, plasticity, and response diversity can allow different mechanisms to preserve similar outcomes.[4] Multiple roads reaching the same destination are evidence of robust organization. They are not, by themselves, evidence that the destination is a target owned by the whole.

## The Objective in Our Notebook

There is another way to manufacture a goal, and scientists are especially good at it.

Take a complicated system and record enough variables. We may discover that its behavior can be described as minimizing variance, preserving biomass, maintaining diversity, maximizing throughput, remaining inside a viability region, or minimizing some mathematical quantity. With enough skill, the resulting objective function may predict the system beautifully.

But who chose the objective?

Perhaps the proposed objective corresponds to a causally privileged variable or constraint in the physical organization. Or perhaps we chose it because it compresses the observations.

Those possibilities cannot be separated by fit alone. If many objective functions generate the same observed behavior, behavior alone cannot identify which, if any, is causally used by the system.

**AN OBJECTIVE FUNCTION CAN EXIST IN OUR MODEL BEFORE A GOAL EXISTS IN THE SYSTEM.**

This problem becomes especially important in theories that use optimization language. A physical process can sometimes be represented as minimizing or maximizing a quantity. Evolutionary models use fitness-related quantities. Engineers specify loss functions. Active-inference and free-energy approaches use optimization-like formalisms to model self-organizing and adaptive systems. Those frameworks can be scientifically useful, and debates about their realist interpretation remain active.[5] Formal optimization by itself does not establish that the proposed objective is a causally privileged variable or constraint of the physical system.

The test is causal. If perturbing the physical organization associated with a proposed target changes action in the specific direction the model predicts, that is stronger evidence than discovering afterward that a trajectory can be fit by an objective function. If several objectives fit equally well, ownership becomes weaker still.

This is the same discipline we used with prediction. A forecast could exist in our model before predictive control existed in the system. Now an objective can exist in our notebook before goal-directed control belongs to the whole.

## What Selection Preserved

Evolution adds another source of apparent purpose.

Eyes are for seeing. Hearts pump blood. Wings contribute to flight. Biological language is full of function, but *function* itself has several scientific and philosophical meanings. Selected-effect, causal-role, and organizational approaches ask different questions about why a trait exists and what it does in a present system.[6]

Historical selection and present control are not the same thing.

A trait can exist because ancestors possessing it left more descendants without the present organism carrying an internal scalar called *fitness* and trying to maximize it. Organisms can, of course, sense and regulate variables that are fitness-relevant proxies. That is different from treating evolutionary fitness itself as an online objective used by the organism.

The same warning applies when we move upward in scale. Trees, fungi, microbes, insects, and other organisms can carry regulatory capacities shaped by selection. Their interactions can stabilize larger ecological variables. That stabilization may be scientifically important. It does not automatically establish that the containing forest maintains that variable as its own target.

What selection preserved is not automatically what the present system pursues.

## Force the Tradeoff

Return to our hypothetical forest.

A severe disturbance pushes it away from a familiar regime. Perhaps drought changes water availability. Perhaps a storm removes canopy. Perhaps fire reorganizes the landscape. Over years, some variables move toward earlier ranges while others settle somewhere new.

We may be tempted to write: *the forest is healing itself.*

Treat that sentence as a hypothesis-generating metaphor, not a scientific conclusion.

This forest is a composite hypothetical experiment, not a report of one empirical site. Its apparent recovery could arise from succession, environmental forcing, interacting organism-level regulation, species replacement, redundancy, soil and hydrological dynamics, or an analyst's decision about which baseline counts as recovery. A serious lower-level rival gets those interactions, nonlinearities, histories, and comparable model capacity. The stronger model does not get weaker opponents.

So we make the experiment harder.

First, specify a candidate target before the disturbance. Call it G. G cannot mean something vague such as *forest health*. It must be measurable and bounded: for example, a hypothetical relation among canopy function, soil-moisture buffering, and water or carbon flux that is expected to remain within a defined tolerance over a stated time horizon. The example is illustrative, not an empirical claim. The boundary, variables, tolerance, horizon, and failure criterion must be fixed before confirmation.

Second, create or exploit a condition in which two candidate constraints cannot both remain within their pre-specified ranges. The experiment forces a tradeoff, not agency. If the goal hypothesis is useful, it should predict which deviation will be tolerated and which compensatory routes will be recruited.

Third, predict the tradeoff before the outcome is known.

That is the heart of the test.

Different perturbations may recruit different mechanisms while preserving the same prospectively specified constraint. Redundancy, degeneracy, attractor dynamics, distributed feedback, and fixed priority networks remain serious rivals. The question is whether a target-structured model predicts the route, the tradeoff, and the consequences of intervention better than those rivals do when resources and flexibility are matched fairly.

Then we separate discovery from confirmation. Candidate target and boundary can be developed in one dataset or regime, but they must be frozen before prospective tests in held-out disturbances, times, sites, or implementations. Serious alternative targets considered during discovery are recorded rather than disappearing when they lose. We keep the losing goals, too. Otherwise a high-dimensional forest gives us nearly unlimited opportunities to discover a goal after the fact.

## When the Goal Cannot Be Reached

Now make the experiment harder again.

Suppose G cannot be restored.

A simple controller may continue pushing because its actuator is saturated or because a fixed feedback rule has no alternative. Persistent corrective action therefore proves little by itself. A stronger target-structured model should prospectively distinguish among continued correction, context-sensitive reallocation, transition to another viable regime, graceful degradation, collapse, or ordinary dynamics predicted by a lower-level rival.

None of those outcomes is frustration, disappointment, or surrender unless psychological capacities are independently established. They are physical responses under an unreachable constraint.

This matters because a return curve can no longer carry the argument. When the familiar destination is unavailable, competing models must predict behavior away from the easy case.

If A and B cannot both be maintained, we specify the expected tradeoff before the conflict. A goal identified only after the outcome remains a story until it predicts the next tradeoff.

## Who Owns the Goal?

By now the ownership test can be stated without mystery.

First, specify the candidate target or constraint independently of the result. Second, identify physical organization through which deviation from that target changes action. Third, show that different disturbances produce discriminating compensation and tradeoffs. Fourth, compare the target-structured account with strong component-level, dynamical, evolutionary, fixed-priority, and externally imposed alternatives. Fifth, require prospective prediction and, where possible, intervention advantages that survive new conditions.

The target does not need to be a symbolic number stored in one location. Organizational approaches to biological function and teleology examine how self-maintaining networks and closure of constraints can ground goal-directed or normative descriptions without a little internal representation labeled *goal*.[7] Macroscopic Life need not defeat that framework. It asks whether organization of comparable causal relevance exists at the proposed macroscopic level and whether the chosen boundary is justified.

A function can exist inside a system before the system owns the function.

Goal-directed regulation can exist inside a system before the containing system owns the goal.

A forest full of organisms regulating themselves does not automatically establish a self-regulating forest. A tissue containing cells that respond to local signals does not automatically establish that every larger boundary we draw around them owns the resulting anatomical outcome. Boundary, causal organization, and intervention evidence must travel together.

We can formalize one part of the comparison:

`PA_goal = Performance(Goal-Structured Model) - Performance(Best Fair Rival)`

The inherited Prediction Advantage standard still applies: the prediction target is specified prospectively; rivals receive comparable data, history, capacity, and tuning opportunity; evaluation occurs on held-out or genuinely new conditions; and the performance difference is assessed with uncertainty and calibration appropriate to the task. A flexible goal model does not earn anything merely by having more parameters.

If `PA_goal > 0`, the goal-structured model has earned predictive utility under that comparison. Ownership requires independent boundary and causal or intervention evidence. Prediction Advantage remains different from Intervention Advantage. Neither establishes consciousness, desire, agency, purpose, or ontological independence.

## Remove the Goal

There is a simpler way to ask the same question.

Remove the higher-level goal variable from the model.

This is not a vocabulary trick. Keep the strongest mechanistic dynamical account available, with the same data, histories, interactions, model capacity, and opportunity to succeed. Ablate the proposed higher-level target or constraint and ask what scientific capability disappears.

Perhaps nothing does. If the mechanistic account predicts novel disturbances, tradeoffs, compensatory routes, and intervention effects just as well, stronger goal ownership has not earned necessity in the science.

But perhaps something is lost. A higher-level target variable might compress changing implementations into a stable predictive structure. It might predict sacrifice under conflict. It might identify interventions difficult to see from component descriptions alone. It might generalize across turnover or new disturbance types.

If so, the higher level has done scientific work.

That still leaves more than one outcome. Lower-level sufficiency remains legitimate. Model B remains available when a higher-level target-structured description improves explanation, prediction, or intervention without earning stronger goal ownership. A property-specific Model C would require the independently justified whole to regulate with respect to a prospectively specified constraint through discriminating organization that survives serious rivals and generalizes beyond the cases used to discover it.

That is a discovery even if the stronger property remains unearned.

## Let the Forest Fail

The forest should be allowed to disappoint us.

Perhaps an attractor or succession model predicts recovery just as well. Perhaps a capacity-matched component-interaction model predicts every tradeoff. Perhaps the apparent target changes whenever we change the analyst's metric. Perhaps the target model works on known disturbances and collapses on the first prospective test. Perhaps its advantage vanishes when excess flexibility is removed. Perhaps intervention on the proposed target-maintaining organization adds no leverage. Perhaps another boundary works equally well or better.

Then the stronger claim loses.

That would not mean nothing happened. The forest may still be robust. It may still contain extraordinary organism-level regulation, distributed feedback, ecological memory, anticipatory responses, and compensatory dynamics. A higher-level description may still earn Model B utility. What failed was the stronger claim that the forest itself owns the goal we proposed.

This is exactly what a useful theory must permit.

## Purpose

There is one more word waiting behind goal, and it is even more dangerous.

Purpose.

We established the distinction earlier and should keep it now: function is not goal. Goal is not purpose. Even strong evidence for a system-owned regulatory target would not establish subjective desire, conscious intention, moral purpose, cosmic purpose, or a reason the system exists.

If one day a larger-scale system earns a goal claim, that would be scientifically remarkable. It would not mean the forest wants to survive, Earth has a mission, or the universe has a plan.

The stronger the word, the more it must earn.

A goal tells us what counts as success.

It does not yet tell us who selects among possible paths.

**Can the whole choose?**
