# Chapter 10 — The Goal

A marble rests at the bottom of a bowl.

Push it halfway up one side and let go. It rolls down, crosses the bottom, climbs the opposite wall, returns, and eventually settles close to where it began. Push it from another direction and the route changes, but the destination does not. Start it higher. Start it lower. Give it a harder shove. Within limits, the same landscape keeps drawing the motion toward the same place.

It is difficult to watch this happen without importing a verb.

The marble returns. The marble seeks the bottom. The marble prefers the bottom.

Did the marble want the bottom?

Of course not. Gravity, geometry, friction, and the marble's initial motion were enough. The bottom mattered to the trajectory, but nothing in that fact requires the marble to detect a deviation, compare its position with a target, or act to restore one. The shape of the system can produce convergence all by itself.

**RETURN IS NOT YET A GOAL.**

The word *yet* matters. In dynamical systems, trajectories can converge toward states or regimes we describe as attractors. In biology and philosophy, attractors, homeostasis, feedback, and self-maintaining organization can legitimately support forms of goal-directed or teleonomic description.[1] We do not need to outlaw that language. We need to ask a harder question: when does a goal belong to the organization of the system we are studying?

## A Real Target

We have met the thermostat before. In Chapter 4 it taught us how easily our verbs can outrun a mechanism. Now it returns for a different reason.

Set a thermostat to seventy-two degrees. When the measured temperature departs sufficiently from the control rule, heating or cooling changes. Here the target is not something we invented after watching the temperature trace. It is physically implemented in the controller. Change the setting and the behavior changes. Disconnect the sensing or control pathway and regulation fails in predictable ways.

This is much more interesting than the marble. It also prevents us from making the opposite mistake. Goal-directed control does not require consciousness. A controller can act with respect to a target without feeling cold, wanting warmth, imagining tomorrow, or knowing that it exists.

But the thermostat gives us another problem. Who chose seventy-two?

A person may have entered the setting. An engineer designed the architecture that can use it. The controller implements the target, but the target was not necessarily authored by the controller. A target can therefore be real, physically consequential, and nonconscious while still raising a question about ownership.

Living systems make that question much harder. Bodies regulate temperature, hydration, glucose, ionic conditions, and many other variables necessary for continued organization. Walter Cannon's homeostasis framework made this regulatory character of physiology explicit nearly a century ago, while later work on allostasis emphasized that successful regulation need not mean returning every variable to one fixed value.[2] Operating ranges can shift with context. Present action can prepare for expected demand. One variable can be allowed to move so that something more important remains viable.

That last possibility changes the experiment.

If a system always returns to one number, we can ask whether the return is passive or actively regulated. But if several important variables cannot all be preserved at once, the system faces a different problem.

Which one gives way?

## What Will It Sacrifice?

Imagine that a living system is pushed into a condition where two constraints conflict. Preserving one variable perfectly would drive another outside a viable range. There is not enough energy, water, time, material, or control capacity to do everything.

Now recovery alone tells us less. The more revealing question is:

> **What will the system allow to fail first?**

A proposed goal hierarchy should predict the answer before the system reveals it. Under one disturbance, the system may tolerate a large deviation in A to preserve B. Under another, it may recruit a different mechanism while still protecting B. Under a third, even B may become expendable because C is more tightly connected to continued organization.

This does not prove that the system consciously ranks its priorities. Fixed network architecture, energetic scarcity, differential fragility, evolutionary history, and local feedback can all generate tradeoffs. But that is exactly why the prediction must come first. We cannot watch a variable survive and then announce that survival proves it was the true goal.

**A GOAL DISCOVERED ONLY AFTER THE SYSTEM CHOOSES IS A STORY UNTIL IT PREDICTS THE NEXT CHOICE.**

This gives us something stronger than simple return. It gives us a way to force competing descriptions apart.

## The Body That Rebuilds

Some of the most provocative examples come from development and regeneration.

Cut living tissue and repair begins. Alter a developmental pathway and cells may compensate. In some regenerative systems, large-scale anatomical outcomes can be restored despite substantial perturbation to the components and routes by which the outcome is produced. Research on morphogenesis and bioelectric signaling has shown that membrane-potential patterns, gap-junction communication, biochemical signals, mechanical interactions, and gene-regulatory processes can participate in distributed control of anatomical patterning.[3]

The temptation is immediate. Somewhere, we want to say, the body must know what shape it is supposed to be.

That sentence is stronger than the experiment.

Researchers sometimes use terms such as *target morphology* or *anatomical homeostasis* to describe the remarkable stability of large-scale form under perturbation. Those can be productive operational ideas. They can guide interventions and suggest variables that are invisible if we look only at one cell at a time. But restoration of form does not automatically establish a stored blueprint, a symbolic representation, intelligence, or a system-owned goal in the strongest sense.

**TARGET MORPHOLOGY IS AN EXPERIMENTAL HYPOTHESIS BEFORE IT IS AN ONTOLOGY.**

That restraint should not make the result less astonishing. It should make the question sharper. A distributed collection of cells can sometimes solve a large-scale patterning problem even though no single cell contains the finished anatomy it helps produce. Different local mechanisms can participate in restoring a coherent whole. The causal organization is real even when the strongest interpretation remains unsettled.

> **THE WHOLE CAN SOLVE A PROBLEM THAT NO SINGLE COMPONENT SOLVES ALONE.**

A whole-system solution can emerge from distributed interaction without proving that the whole represents or owns a goal.

Biological robustness makes the same warning necessary. Redundancy, degeneracy, modular organization, feedback, plasticity, and response diversity can allow different mechanisms to preserve similar outcomes.[4] Multiple roads reaching the same destination are evidence of robust organization. They are not, by themselves, evidence that the destination is a target owned by the whole.

## The Objective in Our Notebook

There is another way to manufacture a goal, and scientists are especially good at it.

Take a complicated system and record enough variables. We may discover that its behavior can be described as minimizing variance, preserving biomass, maintaining diversity, maximizing throughput, remaining inside a viability region, or minimizing some mathematical quantity. With enough skill, the resulting objective function may predict the system beautifully.

But who chose the objective?

Perhaps the system did, in a physically meaningful sense. Perhaps the quantity corresponds to a real control architecture. Or perhaps we chose it because it compresses the observations.

Those possibilities cannot be separated by fit alone.

**AN OBJECTIVE FUNCTION CAN EXIST IN OUR MODEL BEFORE A GOAL EXISTS IN THE SYSTEM.**

This problem becomes especially important in theories that use optimization language. A physical process can sometimes be written as if it minimizes or maximizes a quantity. Evolutionary models use fitness-related quantities. Engineers specify loss functions. Active-inference and free-energy approaches employ optimization-like formalisms to model self-organizing and adaptive systems. These frameworks can be scientifically useful without automatically proving that the modeled system measures the mathematical quantity or owns it as an online objective.[5]

The test is causal. If changing the proposed target structure changes action in the specific way the goal model predicts, that is stronger evidence than discovering afterward that a trajectory can be fit by an optimization function. If several mathematically convenient objectives fit equally well, ownership becomes weaker still.

This is the same discipline we used with prediction. A forecast could exist in our model before predictive control existed in the system. Now an objective can exist in our notebook before goal-directed control belongs to the whole.

## What Selection Preserved

Evolution adds another source of apparent purpose.

Eyes are for seeing. Hearts pump blood. Wings contribute to flight. Biological language is full of function because natural selection can preserve structures whose effects contribute to reproduction, persistence, or organismal organization. Philosophers of biology disagree about exactly how function should be analyzed; selected-effect, causal-role, and organizational accounts capture different aspects of the problem.[6]

But historical selection and present control are not the same thing.

A trait can exist because ancestors possessing it left more descendants without the organism carrying an internal variable called *fitness* and trying to maximize it. Fitness is enormously useful in evolutionary explanation. It is not thereby a little number inside the organism.

The same warning applies when we move upward in scale. Trees, fungi, microbes, insects, and other organisms can carry regulatory capacities shaped by selection. Their interactions can stabilize larger ecological variables. That stabilization may be scientifically important. It does not automatically mean the containing forest maintains that variable as its own target.

What selection preserved is not automatically what the present system pursues.

## Make the Forest Choose

Return to our hypothetical forest.

A severe disturbance pushes it away from a familiar regime. Perhaps drought changes water availability. Perhaps a storm removes canopy. Perhaps fire reorganizes the landscape. Over years, some variables move back toward earlier ranges while others settle somewhere new.

The sentence almost writes itself:

The forest is healing itself.

Do not write that sentence into the science yet.

This forest is a composite hypothetical experiment, not a report of one empirical site. Its apparent recovery could arise from succession, environmental forcing, interacting organism-level regulation, species replacement, redundancy, soil and hydrological dynamics, or an analyst's decision about which baseline counts as recovery. A serious lower-level rival gets all of those interactions, nonlinearities, and histories. The stronger model does not get weaker opponents.

So we make the experiment harder.

First, name the candidate target before the disturbance. Call it G. It might be a range, relationship, constraint, or higher-level state rather than one number. The boundary of the proposed system must also be justified independently; we do not get to redraw the forest after seeing which boundary makes G look best.

Second, force the system to choose among incompatible recoveries. Use different disturbances, or exploit natural disturbances, that make preservation of G conflict with preservation of other candidate variables. If the goal hypothesis is useful, it should predict which deviations will be tolerated and which compensatory routes will be recruited.

Third, predict what the system will sacrifice before it chooses.

That is the heart of the test.

Different perturbations may recruit different mechanisms while preserving the same prospectively specified constraint. We can call this compensatory convergence without pretending the phrase itself proves a goal. Redundancy, degeneracy, attractor dynamics, and distributed feedback remain strong rivals. The question is whether a target-structured model predicts the route, the tradeoff, and the consequences of intervention better than those rivals do.

Then we separate discovery from confirmation. Candidate target and boundary can be developed in one dataset or regime, but they must be frozen before prospective tests in held-out disturbances, times, sites, or implementations. Failed target hypotheses stay in the record. Otherwise a high-dimensional forest gives us nearly unlimited opportunities to discover the goal after the fact.

## When the Goal Cannot Be Reached

Now make the experiment crueler.

Suppose G cannot be restored.

If the system is genuinely organized around G in the sense we are testing, the goal model should tell us something about what happens next. Does corrective activity persist? Does control shift toward another viable regime? Are resources reallocated? Does the system degrade gracefully? Does it collapse? Or does nothing special happen beyond the ordinary dynamics predicted by the lower-level model?

None of those outcomes is frustration, disappointment, or surrender unless we independently establish psychological capacities that this chapter does not claim. They are physical responses under an unreachable constraint.

This matters because a return curve can no longer carry the argument. When the familiar destination is unavailable, competing models must predict behavior away from the easy case.

The same logic applies to multiple candidate goals. If A and B cannot both be maintained, we should specify the expected priority before the conflict. A goal discovered only after the outcome remains a story until it predicts the next choice.

## Who Owns the Goal?

By now the ownership test can be stated without mystery.

First, specify the candidate target or constraint independently of the result. Second, identify physical organization through which deviation from that target changes action. Third, show that different disturbances produce discriminating compensation and tradeoffs. Fourth, compare the target-structured account with strong component-level, dynamical, evolutionary, and externally imposed alternatives. Fifth, require prospective prediction and, where possible, intervention advantages that survive new conditions.

The target does not need to be a symbolic number stored in one location. Organizational accounts of biological teleology explicitly explore how goal-directedness can arise through self-maintaining networks and regulatory closure without a little internal representation labeled *goal*.[7] That is an important rival and an important positive possibility. Macroscopic Life's additional problem is level ownership.

A function can exist inside a system before the system owns the function.

Goal-directed regulation can exist inside a system before the containing system owns the goal.

A forest full of organisms regulating themselves is not automatically a self-regulating forest. A tissue containing cells that respond to local signals is not automatically evidence that every larger boundary we draw around them owns the resulting anatomical outcome. The boundary, causal organization, and intervention evidence must travel together.

We can formalize one part of the comparison:

`PA_goal = Performance(Goal-Structured Model) - Performance(Best Fair Rival)`

If `PA_goal > 0` on held-out tests, the goal-structured model has earned predictive utility under that comparison. It has not thereby earned consciousness, desire, agency, purpose, or ontological independence. And Prediction Advantage remains different from Intervention Advantage. A model can forecast well without identifying the causal organization that owns the target.

## Remove the Goal

There is a simpler way to ask the same question.

Remove the goal.

Replace every goal-directed sentence with the strongest mechanistic dynamical account available. Give that rival the same data, history, interactions, model capacity, and opportunity to succeed. Then ask what scientific capability disappears.

Perhaps nothing does. If the mechanistic account predicts novel disturbances, tradeoffs, compensatory routes, and intervention effects just as well, stronger goal language may be unnecessary for the property claim we are testing.

But perhaps something is lost. A higher-level target variable might compress many changing implementations into one stable predictive structure. It might predict what gets sacrificed under conflict. It might identify interventions that are difficult to see from component descriptions alone. It might generalize across turnover or new disturbance types.

If so, the higher level has done scientific work.

That still leaves more than one outcome. Lower-level sufficiency remains a legitimate result. Model B remains available when a higher-level target-structured description improves explanation, prediction, or intervention without earning stronger goal ownership. A property-specific Model C would require the independently justified whole to preserve or pursue a prospectively specified constraint through discriminating, flexible organization that survives serious rivals and generalizes beyond the cases used to discover it.

And Model B is still a discovery, not a consolation prize.

## Let the Forest Fail

The forest should be allowed to disappoint us.

Perhaps an attractor or succession model predicts recovery just as well. Perhaps a rich component-interaction model predicts every tradeoff. Perhaps the apparent target changes whenever we change the analyst's metric. Perhaps the target model works on known disturbances and collapses on the first prospective test. Perhaps intervention on the proposed target-maintaining organization adds no leverage. Perhaps another boundary works equally well or better.

Then the stronger claim loses.

That would not mean nothing happened. The forest may still be robust. It may still contain extraordinary organism-level regulation, distributed feedback, ecological memory, anticipatory responses, and compensatory dynamics. A higher-level description may still earn Model B utility. What failed was the stronger claim that the forest itself owns the goal we proposed.

This is exactly what a useful theory must permit.

## Purpose

There is one more word waiting behind goal, and it is even more dangerous.

Purpose.

We established the distinction earlier and should keep it now: function is not goal. Goal is not purpose. Even strong evidence for a system-owned regulatory target would not establish subjective desire, conscious intention, moral purpose, cosmic purpose, or a reason the system exists.

If one day a larger-scale system earns a goal claim, that would be scientifically remarkable. It would not mean the forest wants to survive, Earth has a mission, or the universe has a plan.

The stronger the word, the more it must earn.

A goal tells us what counts as success.

It does not yet tell us who chooses the path.

**Can the whole choose?**
