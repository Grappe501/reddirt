# ACT II — WHEN MANY BECOME ONE

## Chapter 5 — When Parts Become a Whole

A crowd is not a person.

A forest is not a tree.

A traffic jam can move, expand, contract and respond to disturbances, but we do not call it an animal.

So when does a collection of living things become something more than a collection?

This is the question that prevents Macroscopic Life from collapsing into metaphor.

If size were enough, a forest would qualify. If communication were enough, the internet would qualify. If energy consumption were enough, a city would qualify. If complex motion were enough, a hurricane would qualify.

None of those criteria is sufficient.

Biology forces us to look deeper.

### Individuality Had to Evolve

We tend to treat the organism as the starting point of evolution.

But some of the organisms around us are themselves products of earlier evolutionary mergers and transitions.

Multicellularity is the obvious example. A multicellular animal exists because cells reproduce, specialize and cooperate inside an architecture in which the success of the larger organism profoundly shapes the evolutionary fate of the cellular lineages within it.

That arrangement was not inevitable.

It had to evolve.

The same is true, in different ways, of chromosomes, eukaryotic cells and some highly integrated insect societies. Evolutionary biologists use the language of **major transitions** to describe episodes in which previously more independent entities became components of a new level of organization.

The details differ dramatically from transition to transition.

The pattern matters.

A new individual can emerge when the relationships among old individuals change deeply enough.

### The Conflict Problem

Cooperation sounds pleasant.

Evolution is less sentimental.

A higher-order individual must survive the possibility that its components pursue incompatible interests.

Your body demonstrates this every day.

Most cells obey extraordinary restrictions on when they divide, where they remain, what functions they perform and when they die. Programmed cell death can remove cells for the benefit of the organism. Immune systems police threats. Development constrains cell fate. Tissue architecture limits behavior.

Cancer exposes what happens when some of those constraints fail.

A cellular lineage can gain a short-term reproductive advantage while damaging the organism that sustains it.

At the cell level, more division may look like success.

At the organism level, it can be catastrophe.

This means higher-order individuality requires more than cooperation.

It requires some degree of **conflict management**.

The components do not need identical interests. But the system must keep lower-level competition from destroying the higher-level organization.

### Division of Labor

One sign of increasing integration is specialization.

A human neuron does not need to digest lunch.

A red blood cell does not need to think.

A muscle fiber does not need to manufacture antibodies.

Specialization allows the whole to perform functions no component could perform alone.

But specialization also creates dependence.

A neuron removed from the physiological environment that sustains it is not a tiny independent animal suddenly set free. Its specialized existence depends on the larger system.

This dependence is important.

When components can freely leave and reproduce independently, the collective may remain more colony-like. When components become deeply dependent on the collective, the boundary between *many organisms cooperating* and *one organism composed of many living units* can shift.

### Reproduction Changes Everything

Reproduction is one of the hardest problems for any proposed macroscopic individual.

Human organisms reproduce through relatively clear developmental cycles. Genetic information passes through a bottleneck. Offspring begin new organismal histories.

Civilizations do not obviously do this.

Cities can found other cities. Cultures spread. Institutions replicate. Technologies reproduce patterns. Colonies historically established new settlements.

But these analogies are weak compared with biological reproduction.

There is no established civilization-level germline.

No obvious reproductive bottleneck.

No clear parent-offspring lineage in the biological sense.

No demonstrated population of civilizations undergoing Darwinian selection as coherent individuals.

That is not a minor problem for Macroscopic Life.

It is one of the strongest arguments against treating present human civilization as a biological superorganism.

We will keep it.

### A Gradient, Not a Switch

The temptation is to demand a single moment when a collection crosses a line and becomes an individual.

Biology may not always provide one.

Individuality can be graded across different dimensions.

A system can be highly integrated physiologically but weakly integrated reproductively. It can possess strong division of labor but persistent internal conflict. It can maintain a boundary while lacking centralized control. It can reproduce collectively while its components retain substantial autonomy.

Instead of asking only:

**Is it an individual?**

we can ask:

**How individualized is it, and along which dimensions?**

That change is powerful.

It prevents us from forcing complicated systems into categories designed around familiar animals.

For this book, we will repeatedly inspect a small set of dimensions:

**Boundary. Integration. Matter flow. Energy flow. Information flow. Regulation. Repair. Memory. Action. Conflict suppression. Reproduction. Heredity. Evolution.**

No single score proves life.

The pattern across them tells us whether the higher-level description is becoming scientifically useful.

### The Ant Colony Test

An ant colony is an ideal challenge.

Individual ants perform specialized roles. Chemical communication coordinates activity. Colonies regulate nest conditions, allocate labor, forage, defend themselves and reproduce through mechanisms operating at colony scale.

Calling some eusocial colonies *superorganisms* is not merely poetic. The analogy can illuminate genuine biological organization.

Yet an ant colony does not become one giant ant.

That is the lesson.

A transition in individuality does not enlarge the previous organismal architecture.

It creates a new architecture.

The colony's boundaries are different. Its communication is different. Its reproductive organization is different. Its components remain visibly animal individuals even while colony-level properties become biologically meaningful.

If we ever discover stronger individuality above humans, we should expect the same surprise.

It may not look like an enlarged human.

### The Higher-Level Test

Here is the standard we will use from this point forward:

> **A higher organizational level earns explanatory status when describing the whole improves our ability to explain, predict or manipulate outcomes beyond what we gain by treating the components as independent actors.**

This does not mean we abandon reductionism.

The whole remains physically implemented by its components.

But knowing every component does not always make the higher-level pattern dispensable.

Temperature is real even though no individual molecule possesses a temperature in the same statistical sense as a gas.

A heartbeat is real even though no isolated heart cell contains the heartbeat of the organism.

Natural selection can operate on organism-level traits even though organisms are made of cells and molecules.

Higher levels can be real without being supernatural.

They are patterns of organization with consequences.

That is exactly what Macroscopic Life must demonstrate if it is ever to become more than an idea.

### The Uncomfortable Possibility

We now know three things.

First, living components can become integrated into higher-order individuals.

Second, that transition requires more than proximity or communication.

Third, individuality can be multidimensional and evolutionary rather than absolute.

So we can finally sharpen the question that began with our imaginary cell:

**Could humans participate in a higher-order transition without recognizing it as such?**

Maybe.

But possibility is cheap.

The next question is harder.

Every organism we know appears to have some way of distinguishing what belongs to its organization from what does not.

If a higher-order individual has no giant skin, where would it end?

## Chapter 6 — Where Does the Individual End?

Draw the boundary of a human being.

Most people would trace the skin.

That is a good first approximation.

It is also incomplete.

Air crosses the boundary continuously. Water crosses it. Food enters and is dismantled until molecules that were once outside become part of tissues we call self. Waste leaves. Heat radiates outward. Microbes live on and inside us. Light changes our physiology. Language changes our brains. Other humans alter our hormones, behavior and survival.

The boundary is real.

It is not absolute.

### A Membrane Is Not a Wall

Cells offer the simplest correction.

A cell membrane does not isolate a cell from the world. If it did, the cell would die.

The membrane regulates exchange.

Some substances cross. Others are transported. Signals are detected. Gradients are maintained. Internal conditions differ from external conditions precisely because the boundary is selectively permeable.

A living boundary is therefore not merely a barrier.

It is a controlled interface.

That changes how we should think about larger systems.

The relevant question is not:

**Is the system sealed?**

Living systems are not sealed.

The question is:

**Does the system regulate relationships across a persistent boundary strongly enough to maintain an identifiable organization?**

### Skin Is Only One Boundary

Your anatomical boundary is visible.

Other boundaries are functional.

The immune system continually distinguishes among threats, tolerable outsiders and the body's own tissues—imperfectly, but consequentially.

Physiological regulation maintains internal ranges that differ from the external environment.

Behavior extends the organism's effective reach. A beaver's dam changes the environment in ways that feed back into the animal's survival. A spider web functions outside the spider's body but participates directly in sensing and capturing prey.

This is sometimes discussed through the idea of the **extended phenotype**: genes can influence structures and effects beyond the physical body of the organism.

That does not mean everything an organism touches becomes part of the organism.

Influence is not membership.

We need a stronger test.

### Membership Versus Influence

The Sun profoundly influences you.

It is not part of your body.

Your phone influences your behavior.

That does not automatically make it an organ.

Your gut microbes can contribute to physiology, but they retain evolutionary histories of their own.

A useful boundary test must therefore distinguish **coupling** from mere causation.

How persistent is the relationship?

How reciprocal is it?

Does disruption impair the larger organization?

Does the system regulate access?

Do the components participate in common maintenance?

Are they replaceable without loss of identity?

Does information routinely cross the boundary in both directions?

Does the proposed whole constrain the behavior of its parts?

The answers create a profile rather than a simple line.

### The Boundary of a Colony

Consider an ant colony again.

Where is it?

Inside the nest?

That fails as soon as workers leave to forage.

Inside the bodies of the ants?

That ignores the nest architecture, stored resources, chemical trails and environmental structures participating in colony function.

A functional colony boundary can move.

Its members can disperse spatially while remaining integrated through recurring interaction.

This gives us another principle:

> **Organizational boundaries need not coincide perfectly with geometric boundaries.**

That statement is useful.

It is also dangerous.

If we loosen boundaries too far, the entire biosphere becomes one connected thing simply because everything affects everything else.

That would destroy the concept of individuality.

So connection alone cannot define the boundary.

### Coupling Has Strength

Imagine two systems exchanging information once a year.

Now imagine two systems exchanging signals thousands of times per second, regulating one another continuously, sharing resources and failing rapidly when separated.

Both are connected.

They are not equally integrated.

Boundary questions therefore become questions about the **strength, persistence and organization of coupling**.

This will matter when we reach civilization.

A human in Arkansas can communicate almost instantaneously with a human in Japan. Markets can transmit price changes globally. A disease detected in one city can trigger responses across continents. Satellite observations can influence agricultural decisions thousands of miles away.

Humanity is more tightly coupled than it was ten thousand years ago.

But tighter coupling does not automatically mean a new organism has appeared.

It means one prerequisite for some forms of higher organization has changed.

### The Internet Is Not a Membrane

It is tempting to call the internet civilization's nervous system.

We will use that analogy later, but carefully.

The internet does not define a clean boundary between civilization and its environment. It does not regulate material exchange the way a cell membrane does. It contains machines, humans, automated agents, misinformation, entertainment, financial systems, sensors and countless competing goals.

It is an information infrastructure.

That is already remarkable.

We do not need to pretend it is literally a nervous system.

The discipline is simple:

**functional analogy can suggest a test; it cannot substitute for the result.**

### The Self Can Move

A living organism also changes its own boundaries through time.

A wound opens and closes.

Pregnancy creates an extraordinary temporary relationship between genetically distinct organisms.

Transplanted organs complicate immune identity.

Microbiomes change.

Development transforms the body's geometry.

The infant and adult do not have identical boundaries or material composition, yet we treat them as stages of one continuing organism.

This suggests that biological identity depends less on maintaining a frozen edge than on maintaining a **history of organized self-production**.

The boundary is part of that process.

Not the whole of it.

### The Remove-the-Boundary Test

Suppose we think we have identified the boundary of a candidate higher-order system.

Remove or disrupt it.

What happens?

If nothing important changes, perhaps the boundary was imaginary.

If internal regulation collapses, information becomes disorganized, resource flows fail or the candidate loses its characteristic identity, the boundary was doing real work.

This is the spirit of a test we will use repeatedly:

**Do not merely name a higher-level structure. Perturb it.**

A real organizational level should leave causal fingerprints.

### Where Do You End?

The answer is stranger than the outline of your skin.

You are a bounded biological process continuously exchanging matter, energy and information with an environment.

Your boundary is maintained rather than absolute.

Your identity survives enormous material turnover.

Your organization incorporates living components with partially independent histories.

And your actions alter structures outside your anatomy that feed back into your survival.

None of that makes the word *individual* meaningless.

It makes individuality richer.

Now we are ready for the next step.

Because once we stop imagining the body as a bag of cells enclosed by skin, we can ask how those cells actually coordinate.

Part of the answer is chemical.

Part is mechanical.

And part is electrical.

Not mystical electricity.

Not a supernatural force.

Ordinary ions, voltages and fields participating in the construction of a living whole.

# ACT III — THE BODY KNOWS MORE THAN THE CELL

## Chapter 7 — The Body Electric

You are electrical.

That sentence sounds mystical only because popular culture has spent decades making ordinary biology sound mysterious.

Every living cell maintains differences in charged ions across its membrane. Those differences create electrical potentials. Ion channels open and close. Pumps spend energy maintaining gradients. Electrical state influences cellular behavior.

Your nervous system depends on this electrochemical reality.

So does your heart.

But neurons did not invent bioelectricity.

Electricity is woven into cellular life more deeply than the familiar image of a nerve impulse suggests.

### Voltage Before Thought

A cell membrane separates solutions containing charged particles.

Because ions are distributed unequally and membranes regulate their movement, a voltage can exist across the membrane.

Change the permeability of the membrane and the voltage can change.

Cells can respond to those changes.

In excitable tissues such as nerves and muscles, rapid electrical events can transmit information and coordinate action.

But slower bioelectric states also occur in non-neural tissues.

Development, wound responses and cell behavior can involve electrical gradients and voltage-dependent processes alongside biochemical and mechanical signaling.

This is important because it expands our picture of biological communication.

The body is not merely a chemical soup with wires added later.

Electrochemical organization is fundamental.

### The Dangerous Leap

Here is where we need the brakes.

The existence of bioelectricity does not prove that thoughts radiate meaningful frequencies into the universe.

It does not establish telepathy.

It does not establish spiritual auras.

It does not show that weak endogenous electromagnetic fields can carry arbitrary biological information across large distances.

Those are separate claims requiring separate mechanisms and evidence.

The body electric is fascinating enough without inventing physics.

Our question is narrower:

**Can electrical state participate in the information-and-control architecture through which local cells coordinate larger-scale biological form?**

The answer, in important contexts, is yes.

### Wounds Are Electrical Events Too

Break an epithelial layer and you do more than create a mechanical hole.

Normal ionic gradients can be disrupted. Endogenous electric fields associated with wounds can influence cell migration, a phenomenon studied under terms such as electrotaxis or galvanotaxis.

Cells can therefore respond to electrical features of damaged tissue alongside chemical and mechanical cues.

Notice the scale transition.

A macroscopic event—tissue damage—changes local physical conditions.

Those changes become signals individual cells can detect.

The cell does not need to understand the sentence *the organism has been wounded*.

The wound is translated into actionable local information.

This principle will become crucial in the next chapters.

### Cells Do Not Read a Blueprint

It is tempting to imagine DNA as an architectural drawing.

DNA is indispensable, but the metaphor becomes misleading if taken literally.

A genome is not a tiny picture of the adult body.

The same genome can participate in producing neurons, muscle, skin and liver. Development depends on gene regulation, signaling, position, timing, mechanics, geometry and interactions among cells and tissues.

Bioelectric state can be part of that regulatory environment.

So can chemical gradients.

So can force.

The organism emerges through a dynamic network of constraints.

The better image is not a blueprint being read from top to bottom.

It is a system continually building conditions that influence what happens next.

### Electrical Prepatterns

Experimental work in developmental and regenerative biology has explored spatial patterns of membrane voltage and ion-channel activity associated with tissue patterning. Manipulating bioelectric states in some model systems can alter developmental outcomes.

These findings are scientifically interesting because they suggest that electrical state can sometimes be **instructive**, not merely a passive by-product.

But we must keep levels of interpretation separate.

An experiment may show that changing ion-channel activity changes anatomy.

That does not automatically establish that a tissue contains a complete electrical blueprint of the organism.

It does not prove that voltage is the master code of morphology.

And it certainly does not prove that electrical patterns are conscious.

A disciplined sequence is:

**experiment → observation → mechanism → systems interpretation → cautious extrapolation.**

Skipping steps produces mythology.

### Information Requires a Receiver

A voltage difference is not automatically a message.

For a physical state to function as biological information, something in the system must respond to it in a context-dependent way that changes subsequent behavior.

Cells possess mechanisms through which electrical state can influence signaling, transport, gene expression and other processes.

This gives bioelectric patterns potential informational significance without requiring anything supernatural.

The same rule applies at every scale.

A radio wave passing through an empty desert contains structured physical variation, but communication requires a receiver capable of coupling to and decoding relevant features.

When people invoke mysterious frequencies, this is the question they often skip:

**What is the receiver?**

Then:

**How does it discriminate signal from noise?**

Then:

**What changes when the signal is received?**

Those questions turn wonder into science.

### Resonance Without Magic

Resonance is real physics.

Systems can respond strongly to particular driving frequencies under appropriate conditions. Oscillators can synchronize. Biological rhythms can entrain to environmental cycles. Neural and physiological systems exhibit oscillatory behavior.

But resonance is not a universal password that makes every proposed interaction possible.

Coupling matters.

Distance matters.

Energy matters.

Damping matters.

Noise matters.

Geometry matters.

The existence of a resonant frequency in one system does not imply meaningful communication with another system that happens to oscillate.

That distinction protects one of the original intuitions behind this book while making it testable.

Perhaps unknown forms of biological coupling remain undiscovered.

Science should be open to that possibility.

But possibility enters the laboratory as a question, not an answer.

### From Electrical Cell to Electrical Tissue

The deeper lesson of bioelectricity is not that the body glows with hidden mystical energy.

It is that individual cells participate in electrical relationships that can matter at tissue scale.

Local membrane states can influence neighboring cells. Gap junctions can permit direct passage of ions and small molecules between cells. Tissue-level electrical patterns can emerge from many cellular states interacting.

This is exactly the kind of multiscale organization Macroscopic Life cares about.

The physical mechanism remains local.

The function can belong to a larger level.

A single cardiac cell can generate electrical activity.

A coordinated heart rhythm belongs to the organ.

A neuron can fire.

A thought is not therefore located inside one action potential.

A cell can change voltage.

A developmental pattern can depend on relationships among many such cells.

> **A mechanism can be entirely local while the function it implements belongs to a larger organizational level.**

### The Aura Question Returns

What, then, surrounds a human body?

Heat, certainly.

Chemical emissions, certainly.

Sound and mechanical disturbance, certainly.

Electrical and magnetic phenomena associated with physiological activity can be measured with sufficiently sensitive instruments under appropriate conditions.

Beyond that, claims must be separated and tested.

Could another organism detect some weak signal we do not consciously detect?

Possibly, depending on the signal and receptor.

Could human beings possess an undiscovered sensory pathway for a particular physical variable?

Possible in principle.

Has science established a generalized external aura that reliably encodes personality, morality, emotion or consciousness?

No.

That answer does not close the door.

It tells us where the door actually is.

### The Larger Lesson

Electrical biology gives Macroscopic Life no proof of a larger organism.

What it gives us is a mechanism lesson.

A component does not need to understand the global pattern.

It needs access to local variables through which the global organization is physically implemented.

If higher-order organization exists above us, its components may likewise respond to local information without possessing a representation of the whole.

Humans might respond to prices, laws, social signals, media, infrastructure, resource availability, institutional rules and communication networks in ways that collectively generate higher-level patterns none of us intends.

That analogy remains only an analogy.

But now it points toward a serious question:

**How can local information construct global form?**

The developing body has been solving that problem for hundreds of millions of years.

And it does something even stranger.

It knows when to stop.

## Chapter 8 — The Body Builds Itself

No cell in a human embryo has seen the finished human.

There is no foreman standing outside the embryo comparing construction to an architectural drawing.

There is no tiny observer inside the fertilized egg looking ahead at the adult body and issuing instructions.

Yet development produces organized anatomy.

An anterior end differs from a posterior end. Limbs emerge in characteristic places. Organs acquire structure. Tissues meet other tissues. Growth is constrained. Cells differentiate. Boundaries form.

And, most of the time, the process stops in roughly the right places.

How?

### DNA Is Essential—and Not a Picture

The genome is foundational.

But DNA does not contain a literal miniature body map.

Genes encode products and regulatory relationships that participate in developmental processes. Cells with largely the same genome can become radically different tissues because different genes are active, different signals are present and different histories have occurred.

Development is therefore generative.

The body is produced through rules, interactions and changing states.

This distinction matters because people often smuggle a hidden designer into biological language without realizing it.

We say cells “know” where to go.

Tissues “remember” their shape.

The embryo “knows” what it should become.

Those phrases can be useful shorthand.

They become dangerous when metaphor is mistaken for a little mind inside the tissue.

There is no evidence for a ghost blueprint.

The target, to the extent target-like behavior exists, must be physically instantiated in the organization of the system.

### Position Is Information

A developing cell's behavior depends partly on where it is.

But *where* is not always represented by a coordinate label.

Cells can infer positional context from gradients of signaling molecules, neighboring cells, tissue geometry, mechanical forces, electrical states and developmental history.

A classic conceptual model is positional information: different concentrations of a morphogen can contribute to different developmental responses.

Real embryos are more complicated than a simple gradient diagram.

Signals interact. Boundaries sharpen. Timing matters. Feedback changes the field. Cells move. Tissues deform.

Position is dynamically constructed.

### Mechanics Talks Back

Biology is not software running on passive meat.

Physical forces matter.

Cells pull on one another. Tissues bend. Pressure changes. Extracellular structures constrain movement. Geometry changes signaling environments.

The shape being built changes the conditions under which further building occurs.

That creates a feedback loop:

**genes influence cells → cells alter tissue → tissue geometry and mechanics alter signals → signals alter gene activity and cell behavior.**

The developing body writes part of the environment its own cells subsequently read.

This is why one-way blueprint metaphors fail.

### The Body Writes and Reads Itself

Add the channels together.

Genetic regulation.

Chemical gradients.

Cell-cell signaling.

Bioelectric state.

Mechanical force.

Geometry.

Timing.

Metabolism.

Environmental input.

None alone is the body plan.

The pattern emerges through their relationships.

A useful way to think about development is as a recursive process:

**STATE → SIGNAL → RESPONSE → NEW STATE → NEW SIGNAL → NEW RESPONSE.**

The body continually writes conditions and reads the consequences.

> **The body writes and reads itself.**

### Robustness Is the Clue

If development were a fragile chain in which every cell followed one predetermined microscopic instruction, small disturbances should routinely destroy the result.

Instead, biological development often exhibits remarkable robustness.

Embryos can tolerate variation. Cells can compensate. Tissues can regulate after perturbation. Developmental systems can converge on similar outcomes despite some differences in starting conditions.

This does not mean development is indestructible.

Birth defects, developmental disorders and lethal failures prove otherwise.

But robustness tells us that biological form is often controlled through feedback rather than produced by an entirely open-loop sequence.

The system can respond to deviation.

That is a profound distinction.

### Target States Without Tiny Minds

Suppose a developing tissue tends to converge on a particular anatomical outcome across a range of disturbances.

It can be useful to describe that outcome as a **target state**.

The phrase does not imply conscious intention.

A thermostat has a target temperature without wanting anything.

A control system can reduce deviation from a state without possessing subjective experience.

The scientific question is whether target-state language improves prediction.

If we perturb the tissue, can we predict that the system will compensate toward a characteristic morphology?

Can we alter the regulatory state and cause the system to converge on a different stable form?

Can we identify mechanisms implementing that correction?

Those are experimental questions.

### Read the Pattern. Write the Pattern. Test the Result.

If a proposed morphogenetic code exists in any meaningful sense, scientists should eventually be able to do more than observe correlations.

They should be able to **read** states that predict anatomical outcomes.

They should be able to **write** or alter those states.

And the resulting anatomy should change in predicted ways.

This is a much stronger standard than saying a beautiful pattern exists.

Correlation is the beginning.

Intervention is the test.

### No Cell Contains the Whole

This may be the most important lesson of development for Macroscopic Life.

No single ordinary cell needs to contain a complete representation of the organism for organism-level form to be real.

The larger pattern can be distributed across relationships among components.

That means the question “Which component knows the whole?” may be badly formed.

Perhaps none does.

The information necessary to produce the whole can exist in the organization of interactions.

> **A higher-order form can be physically maintained even though no individual component contains a complete representation of that form.**

Now return to our intelligent cell.

It might understand its own genes.

It might measure neighboring signals.

It might catalog voltage.

It might understand mechanics.

And still fail to infer the animal whose body those processes collectively construct.

Not because the animal is supernatural.

Because the animal exists at another organizational level.

### The Stop Problem

Building is only half the mystery.

Why does a hand usually stop with five digits rather than fifty?

Why does an organ stop growing within characteristic ranges?

Why does wound repair ordinarily terminate instead of continuing indefinitely?

Growth requires regulation not merely of *whether* cells proliferate but *how much*, *where* and *when to stop*.

Cancer again reveals the importance of termination.

A system that can start growth but cannot appropriately constrain it does not maintain normal form.

So morphology is not simply construction.

It is controlled construction.

And controlled construction implies comparison—implemented somehow—between current state and permissible future states.

We should be cautious with the word *comparison*. There need not be a central comparator reading a body diagram.

Distributed feedback can perform the function.

But the functional problem remains:

**How does the system respond differently when enough structure exists?**

That question becomes even sharper when anatomy is removed after development is complete.

Building a limb once is remarkable.

Building it again after it is gone is something else.

Because then the remaining organism must somehow respond not only to what is present—

but to what is missing.
