# MACROSCOPIC LIFE

## Chapter 7 — The Signal

Before dawn, a forest can look almost motionless.

The air is cool. Leaves hang still. Soil temperatures change slowly. The trunks seem fixed in place, and if you were standing among them with nothing but your eyes and ears, you might reasonably conclude that very little is happening.

Now imagine the same forest wired for observation.

Soil probes measure moisture at different depths. Sensors track sap flow, leaf temperature, humidity, light, wind, pressure, electrical potential, fungal activity, volatile compounds, and minute changes in chemistry. Cameras watch the canopy. Acoustic sensors record vibrations outside the range of ordinary attention. Instruments collect data every second, every minute, every hour, across hundreds of locations.

At 4:17 in the morning, something changes.

A cluster of sensors on one side of the forest shifts first. Minutes later, another region changes. Then another. Electrical measurements alter. Sap flow begins to move. Chemical concentrations change. A pattern travels across the map.

If we saw something similar inside a nervous system, we might call it signaling. If we saw it in an endocrine system, we might ask what message was being carried. If we saw it in a computer network, we would immediately look for information moving between nodes.

In a forest, the temptation is enormous.

Maybe the forest is talking.

Maybe one part has detected something and the rest is responding.

Maybe the pattern is a message.

Or maybe nothing has spoken to anything at all.

That is the problem of the signal.

A pattern can cross a system without the system sending a message.

### The Easiest Explanation

Suppose the changes in our forest line up beautifully. The western trees alter first, then the center, then the eastern edge. A machine-learning system can predict the order. The correlations are strong enough to survive repeated measurements. There is clearly structure in the data.

Now place a weather station several miles away.

A pressure front is moving across the region.

The trees are not signaling to one another. They are responding independently to a common physical driver.

Nothing about the pattern was false. The measurements were real. The correlation was real. The sequence was real. The prediction was real. Only the story changed.

This is one of the easiest mistakes to make in complex systems. When many parts respond to the same cause, their behavior can look coordinated even when no information passes among them.

Imagine a city filled with thermostats. As outdoor temperature rises, thousands of air conditioners switch on. A map of electrical demand would show neighborhoods changing together. The pattern might travel across the city as afternoon heat moves from one region to another. A sufficiently good model could predict which neighborhoods will activate next.

We would not conclude that the air conditioners had formed a language.

They are participating in a shared pattern because they are exposed to shared conditions.

The same caution applies everywhere. Birds may begin calling because dawn arrives. Plants may close stomata because humidity falls. Animals may move because a storm approaches. Financial markets may shift because participants respond to the same announcement. Cells may change because a common hormone reaches them. Similar behavior can emerge from a common cause without direct communication among the responding parts.

Correlation therefore tells us where to look. It does not tell us what moved.

Even propagation is not enough.

Drop a stone into a pond and a wave travels outward. The water carries a physical disturbance. Energy moves. The effect reaches distant points. Yet the pond is not necessarily transmitting a message to itself.

Propagation is not communication.

That distinction sounds obvious when the example is a pond. It becomes harder when the propagating event is electrical, chemical, biological, or exquisitely patterned. The more interesting the pattern becomes, the more carefully we have to separate what happened from the verbs we use to describe it.

### When Signaling Is Not a Metaphor

Fortunately, biology gives us examples where signaling is not merely poetic language.

Plants are an excellent place to begin because their internal coordination does not resemble the animal nervous system most people use as their intuitive model. A plant can experience injury or environmental change in one region and produce electrical, calcium, chemical, hydraulic, and reactive-oxygen-related signals that influence tissues far from the original event. Researchers have measured long-distance electrical and calcium signaling within plants and shown that local stimuli can trigger systemic responses elsewhere in the organism.[1]

The important point is not that plants have tiny hidden brains. They do not need one for the argument.

What matters is that a physical event occurs in one part of an already established organism, a signal propagates through identifiable biological pathways, distant tissues change state, and those changes affect the organism's regulation.

We can describe the chain without mystery:

Source → Signal → Channel → Receiver → State Change → Functional Consequence.

Bacteria provide another useful case. In quorum sensing, cells produce and respond to signaling molecules whose concentrations and detection can alter gene regulation and collective behavior. The mechanisms vary greatly among species, but the central principle is experimentally tractable: molecules are produced, detected, and connected to downstream changes in cellular behavior.[2]

Again, the word communication is not doing the scientific work. The mechanism is.

If we remove the molecules, alter their production, block the receptor, or change downstream circuitry, the behavior changes in ways that can be measured.

That is what makes the concept useful.

The danger begins when we carry the vocabulary upward without carrying the evidence with it.

A forest contains organisms that signal. It contains fungi, microbes, insects, plants, animals, chemical gradients, electrical changes, volatile compounds, water movement, and countless other interacting processes. Communication can therefore exist inside the forest without the forest itself being the communicator.

The same is true of a city. People communicate within it constantly. Computer networks exchange signals. Traffic lights coordinate flows. Institutions send information. None of that alone establishes that the city is one communicating individual.

A function can exist inside a system before the system owns the function.

That rule becomes essential here.

### The Resonance Temptation

Some words seem to invite larger interpretations almost automatically. Frequency and resonance are among the strongest.

The reason is understandable. Oscillation is everywhere.

Hearts beat. Neural systems generate rhythms. Circadian systems cycle. Chemical reactions can oscillate. Mechanical structures resonate. Populations fluctuate. Electrical circuits have characteristic frequencies. Waves interact. Coupled oscillators can synchronize.

When systems begin moving together, the visual effect can be extraordinary.

Place several metronomes on a movable platform and, under the right conditions, their motions can gradually synchronize because each one influences the shared support. Pendulum clocks mounted on the same structure can interact through tiny mechanical forces. Fireflies can synchronize flashes. Biological oscillators can become entrained by environmental cycles.

None of those examples requires a hidden message.

Synchronization is a dynamic relation before it is a communication claim.

Two systems can fall into step because their physical coupling changes their trajectories. Resonance can amplify a response when forcing occurs near a system's characteristic frequency. Those are important physical facts, but they do not automatically establish symbols, meaning, intention, agency, or consciousness.

The discipline is simple to state and difficult to maintain:

Frequency is a measurement, not an explanation.

If someone proposes that an oscillation is carrying information, the next questions become concrete. What is oscillating? What physical mechanism couples the systems? Can the signal be modulated? Do different patterns produce different downstream responses? Can the proposed receiver be identified? Does disrupting the oscillation selectively alter the claimed effect? Does another mechanism explain the same result more simply?

Now resonance becomes scientifically interesting.

Suppose a plant, microbial community, or engineered system uses oscillatory dynamics in a way that changes downstream behavior. If one pattern predicts one response and another pattern predicts a different response, if controlled manipulation of the oscillation changes that response, and if the effect survives ordinary explanations, then we may be looking at information carried by a dynamic process.

But the oscillation does not become a message merely because it is beautiful.

### The Forest That Learned to Talk

Few modern scientific stories demonstrate the danger of language inflation better than the idea often described as the “wood-wide web.”

The underlying biology is fascinating. Mycorrhizal fungi form associations with plant roots. Fungal networks can connect with more than one plant. Material can move through fungal systems. These relationships can affect nutrient exchange, ecology, competition, and plant performance under some conditions.

From there, popular language has sometimes taken a much larger step.

Trees are described as talking. Mature trees become mothers sending resources to offspring. Forests warn one another. Underground networks become analogues of brains or social media. The images are powerful because they attach familiar human meanings to unfamiliar biological relationships.

Some of those hypotheses are scientifically testable. That is exactly why the language has to be controlled.

Reviews of common mycorrhizal networks have warned that several popular generalizations are more weakly supported than public discussion often suggests. Field evidence varies, alternative explanations can be difficult to eliminate, and claims about preferential resource or defense-signal transfer have sometimes exceeded what has actually been demonstrated.[3]

This should not make the subject less interesting.

It should make it more interesting.

A physical connection is not the same thing as communication. Material transfer is not automatically a message. A benefit to one organism does not establish altruism. A network does not become a nervous system because it links many components. And even genuine communication among organisms would not, by itself, establish a communicating forest-level individual.

The strongest version of the hypothesis must survive the strongest ordinary alternatives.

If two trees change after one is damaged, perhaps a signal moved between them. Or perhaps both responded to the same airborne chemical, shared pathogen, soil disturbance, environmental shift, fungal process, or measurement artifact. If carbon appears in another plant, we need to know the pathway, whether transfer occurred through the proposed network, how much moved, whether the transfer altered function, and whether alternative routes can be excluded.

This is not skepticism as a refusal to look.

It is skepticism as a better detector.

The attractive story is not the enemy. The attractive story is the hypothesis generator.

Then reality gets to decide how much of it survives.

### Information for Whom?

The word information creates another trap because scientists use it in several legitimate but different ways.

Suppose we discover that humidity at dawn strongly predicts fungal activity six hours later. The earlier measurement contains statistical information about the later state because knowing one reduces uncertainty about the other.

That does not mean the fungus is reading a humidity message.

The information exists for the scientist before we have shown that it exists for the system.

This distinction matters because modern analysis can find predictive structure almost anywhere in sufficiently rich data. A machine-learning model may detect subtle relationships across hundreds of variables. Those relationships can be scientifically valuable even when no biological subsystem uses them as signals.

To move from statistical information toward functional information, intervention becomes important.

Change the candidate signal while holding relevant alternatives constant. If downstream behavior changes in the predicted direction, the claim strengthens. Change the pattern again. If a different signal state produces a different response, the architecture becomes more interesting still.

Now we are approaching something like selectivity.

A receptor that responds differently to different chemical ligands, a regulatory system that distinguishes one concentration range from another, or a signaling pathway whose temporal pattern alters downstream gene expression is doing more than merely co-varying with another process.

The physical state of one component is influencing what another component does.

Even then, we should be careful with meaning.

Scientists may label a signal “danger,” “hunger,” “density,” or “injury” because those words help us summarize the conditions associated with it. The system does not need to possess a human concept of danger for the signal to participate in regulation.

Semantic language should be earned separately from causal function.

Information can exist for the scientist before it exists for the system.

And functional information can exist for a system without becoming a thought.

### There May Be No Control Room

Human beings have another bias that becomes increasingly important as we move upward in scale.

We expect important organization to have a center.

Brains have centralized structures. Governments have capitals. Companies have headquarters. Machines often have processors or controllers. Because centralized control is familiar, we tend to look for a place where the system's decisions are made.

Biology repeatedly complicates that intuition.

An ant colony can produce organized foraging, nest construction, defense, and task allocation without a single ant possessing a complete plan of the colony. Local interactions can generate large-scale patterns. No ant needs to stand above the nest issuing instructions for coordinated colony behavior to emerge.

Development creates an even deeper example. Cells respond to local signals, gradients, mechanical conditions, gene-regulatory states, and neighboring cells. No individual cell contains a miniature finished body directing construction from above. Yet ordered anatomy can emerge reliably from distributed interactions.

Engineered systems can work the same way. Distributed networks, swarms, and decentralized control systems can solve problems without a single central processor representing every state of the whole.

A system-level function does not require a system-level control room.

But this rule cuts both ways.

The absence of a control room cannot be used to dismiss higher-level organization.

It also cannot be used as evidence for it.

Distributed behavior is common. The question is what the distributed interactions collectively belong to.

An ant colony is a useful biological case because evolutionary and ecological evidence can support treating the colony as a meaningful higher-level unit for some questions. A traffic jam also exhibits distributed dynamics, but we do not infer that the jam possesses agency simply because no driver controls it.

Decentralization therefore tells us almost nothing by itself about ownership.

It merely removes one bad requirement.

The whole does not need a throne.

### Who Owns the Message?

Imagine two neighboring organisms exchanging a chemical signal.

The exchange is real. The molecules are identifiable. Receptors respond. Behavior changes.

Now ask a strange question:

Who owns the function?

If each organism uses the interaction entirely in service of its own independent regulation, then communication occurs inside the larger ecological system without belonging to the larger system as an integrated individual.

If, however, disrupting that exchange selectively damages a stable higher-level organization, if the effect persists across replacement of individual components, if the boundary of that organization has already been established independently, and if the higher-level description improves prediction or intervention, then the ownership question becomes more difficult.

This is where communication connects back to the earlier chapters.

Chapter 3 required the boundary to earn itself. Chapter 4 required agency language to survive mechanistic replacement. Chapter 5 required the higher level to produce scientific work that the lower-level account could not match as well. Chapter 6 required prospective testing and the possibility of failure.

Communication cannot be allowed to bypass those gates.

We cannot draw a circle around a forest because signals occur within it, call the enclosed region a communicator, and then use the existence of communication as proof that the circle was correct.

That would be circular in both geometry and logic.

The boundary must be earned independently.

Then we can ask whether the communication merely happens inside that boundary or contributes to what makes the boundary scientifically meaningful.

The Macroscopic Life Ownership Test can be stated simply:

Does communication merely occur inside the proposed system, or does its function belong to the organization of the proposed system?

That is a much harder question than asking whether signals exist.

Signals are everywhere.

Ownership is rare enough that we should expect many candidates to fail.

### Return to the Forest

So return to our forest before dawn.

The mysterious wave still crosses the sensor network.

This time, we know what to do.

First, reproduce it.

A one-time pattern is not enough. We need to see whether the candidate signal appears again under comparable conditions.

Second, identify what physically changes. Is the carrier electrical? Chemical? Hydraulic? Mechanical? Acoustic? Atmospheric? Biological? Some combination?

Third, model the ordinary drivers. Weather, light, soil moisture, temperature, topography, seasonal changes, shared pathogens, local diffusion, fungal structure, and other environmental variables must be given every fair chance to explain the pattern.

Fourth, ask whether the signal distinguishes states. Does one pattern occur under one condition and another under a meaningfully different condition? Can those differences be specified before the next event occurs?

Fifth, perturb the proposed pathway.

Block it where possible. Interrupt a connection. Change the candidate signal. Use sham interventions. If the claimed downstream effect survives unchanged, the proposed communication channel weakens. If the effect disappears selectively and returns when the pathway is restored, the hypothesis becomes stronger.

Sixth, test the receiver.

Does a downstream component respond specifically to the candidate signal? Does changing the signal alter that response in a predictable way?

Seventh, return to the boundary.

Does the proposed forest-level unit outperform competing boundaries? Perhaps the real organization follows hydrology rather than the visual edge of the forest. Perhaps fungal topology defines the relevant network. Perhaps no higher-level boundary survives at all.

Eighth, test the higher-level claim prospectively.

If we treat the candidate whole as an organized information-processing unit, do we predict future behavior better than a strong lower-level model using the same relevant data and resources?

Ninth, go somewhere else.

A second site. Another season. Another disturbance. Another research team.

The point is not to protect the signal.

The point is to give it every opportunity to disappear.

### Three Forests

At the end of this experiment, there are at least three scientifically legitimate forests we might discover.

In the first, the mystery collapses.

The apparent communication turns out to be weather, hydrology, local diffusion, correlated physiology, measurement artifacts, or some other ordinary mechanism. The pattern remains interesting, but no communication claim survives.

That is Model A.

Nothing has failed except the stronger interpretation.

In the second forest, genuine communication among components survives. Organisms exchange signals. Pathways can be identified and disrupted. Responses are selective and functional.

But the higher-level ownership claim fails.

The communication belongs to the organisms, fungal partners, microbes, or local subsystems. The forest contains communication without becoming one communicator.

That is Model B.

Model B is a discovery, not a consolation prize.

In the third forest, something more difficult happens.

A communication architecture survives common-driver controls. Its disruption damages an independently justified higher-level organization. Competing boundaries perform worse. The effect survives replacement of particular components. The higher-level model improves prospective prediction or intervention. Replication succeeds.

Now we may have evidence that communication contributes to a larger organized unit.

That would be scientifically important.

It still would not prove that the forest thinks.

Communication is not agency.

Agency is not intelligence.

Intelligence is not consciousness.

The property ladder remains intact even when the result becomes exciting.

### The Thing That Remains

There is one more complication.

Everything we have described so far can happen in the present.

A signal appears. Another part of the system responds. The interaction ends.

But living systems often do something stranger.

The next response depends on what happened before.

A previous drought changes tissue. An immune exposure changes later response. A neural connection changes strength. Gene regulation shifts. Soil chemistry changes. Community composition changes. A pathway becomes easier or harder to activate. The physical system carries something forward.

Persistence alone is not memory.

A scar persists. A crater persists. A bent piece of metal persists. Not every trace is a remembered experience.

But memory cannot exist without some physical consequence of the past remaining capable of altering the future.

That gives us a new problem.

Suppose our forest experiences a disturbance. The immediate signal disappears. Months later, the same disturbance returns, and the system responds differently.

Was the second response caused by a retained physical change?

Did that retained state influence what happened next?

Does the effect belong to individual organisms, to local ecological history, or to a larger organization?

The signal has carried us as far as it can.

Now we need to know whether yesterday can remain inside tomorrow.

**Can the whole remember?**
