# MACROSCOPIC LIFE

## Chapter 7 — The Signal

Before dawn, a forest can look almost motionless. The air is cool. Leaves hang still. Soil temperatures change slowly. The trunks seem fixed in place, and if you were standing among them with nothing but your eyes and ears, you might reasonably conclude that very little is happening.

Imagine an experiment we have not yet run. Wire that forest for observation. Soil probes measure moisture at different depths. Sensors track sap flow, leaf temperature, humidity, light, wind, pressure, electrical potential, volatile compounds, and minute changes in chemistry. Cameras watch the canopy. Other instruments record changes too subtle, too slow, too fast, or too distributed for unaided human attention. Data arrive every second, every minute, every hour, across hundreds of locations.

At 4:17 in the morning, something changes. A cluster of sensors on one side of the forest shifts first. Minutes later, another region changes, then another. Electrical measurements alter. Sap flow changes. Chemical concentrations move. A pattern travels across the map. If we saw something similar inside a nervous system, we might call it signaling. If we saw it in an endocrine system, we might ask what information was being carried. In a forest, the temptation is enormous: maybe one part has detected something and the rest is responding. Maybe the pattern is a message. Maybe the forest is talking.

Or maybe nothing has spoken to anything at all.

**A pattern can cross a system without the system sending a message.**

### The Easiest Explanation

Suppose the changes line up beautifully. The western trees alter first, then the center, then the eastern edge. A machine-learning system predicts the order. The correlations survive repeated measurements. There is structure in the data. Then we place a weather station beyond the forest and discover a pressure front moving across the region.

The observed sequence no longer requires tree-to-tree signaling. A common physical driver is sufficient to generate the pattern we saw. Additional signaling might still occur somewhere in the system, but this observation no longer demonstrates it. Nothing about the measurements was false. The correlation was real. The sequence was real. The prediction was real. Only the explanation changed.

Imagine hundreds of automated lights along a highway, each switching state when the same external sensor network reports that darkness has crossed a threshold. A map could show a wave of activation. A model could predict which lights change next. Yet the lights need not be sending messages to one another. They participate in a shared pattern because they share a driver.

The same caution applies everywhere. Birds may begin calling because dawn arrives. Plants may change physiology because humidity falls. Animals may move because a storm approaches. Markets may shift because participants respond to the same announcement. Similar behavior can emerge from common causes without direct communication among the responding parts. Correlation tells us where to look; it does not tell us what moved.

Even propagation is not enough. Drop a stone into a pond and a wave travels outward. Energy and a physical disturbance move through the water and affect distant points. That alone does not mean the pond is transmitting a message to itself. Propagation is not communication. The distinction becomes harder when the event is electrical, chemical, biological, or exquisitely patterned, because interesting patterns invite interesting verbs.

### When Signaling Is Not a Metaphor

Biology gives us cases in which signaling is experimentally tractable rather than merely poetic. Plants are useful because their internal coordination does not resemble the animal nervous system most people carry as their intuitive model. Local stimuli can trigger long-distance electrical and calcium dynamics associated with systemic responses elsewhere in an already established organism.[1] The point is not that plants contain tiny hidden brains. A physical event occurs in one region, measurable biological processes propagate, distant tissues change state, and those changes can participate in regulation.

For this book, we can use a practical scaffold: Source → Signal → Channel → Receiver → State Change → Functional Consequence. That is an operational tool for asking questions, not a claim that every scientific discipline uses one universal definition of communication.

Bacteria provide another positive control. In quorum sensing, cells produce and respond to signaling molecules whose detection alters gene regulation and behavior. Mechanisms differ among organisms, but production, detection, regulatory circuitry, and downstream consequences can be manipulated experimentally.[2] The word communication is not doing the scientific work. The mechanism is. Alter signal production, reception, or downstream circuitry and ask what changes.

The danger begins when we carry the vocabulary upward without carrying the evidence with it. A forest contains organisms that signal. It contains fungi, microbes, insects, plants, animals, chemical gradients, water movement, electrical changes, volatile compounds, and innumerable interacting processes. Communication can therefore occur inside a forest without the forest itself being the communicator. A city provides the same warning: people, computers, institutions, and machines exchange information constantly, but their communication does not by itself establish the city as one communicating individual.

A function can exist inside a system before the system owns the function.

### The Resonance Temptation

Some words invite larger interpretations almost automatically. Frequency and resonance are among the strongest because oscillation is everywhere. Hearts beat. Neural systems generate rhythms. Circadian systems cycle. Chemical reactions can oscillate. Mechanical structures resonate. Electrical circuits have characteristic frequencies. Populations fluctuate. Coupled systems can synchronize or become entrained.

These phenomena need to be separated before we ask what they mean. Resonance concerns the response of a system to forcing, often with enhanced response near characteristic dynamics. Synchronization and entrainment concern relationships among oscillators or between an oscillator and a driver. They can interact, but they are not synonyms, and neither automatically establishes communication.

Several mechanical oscillators coupled through a shared support can move toward coordinated behavior because each alters the physical conditions experienced by the others. A biological rhythm can become entrained by an environmental cycle. None of that requires symbols, meaning, intention, agency, or consciousness. Synchronization is a dynamic relation before it is a communication claim.

Frequency is a measurement, not an explanation.

If an oscillation is proposed as an information-bearing channel, the questions become concrete. What is oscillating? What is the coupling mechanism? Can the pattern be modulated? Do distinguishable signal states produce distinguishable downstream responses? Can a responsive subsystem be identified? Does a specific perturbation alter the effect while appropriate controls remain intact? Can a simpler physical mechanism explain the same observation?

That is where resonance becomes useful to Macroscopic Life. An oscillation does not become a message because it is beautiful. But if controlled changes in its pattern are selectively used by a regulatory architecture and reliably change what the system does, then the dynamics may participate in information processing. The mechanism, not the metaphor, has to earn that interpretation.

### The Network Beneath the Story

Few modern scientific stories show the danger of language inflation better than the idea popularly called the “wood-wide web.” The underlying biology is genuinely remarkable. Mycorrhizal fungi form associations with plant roots, and fungal structures can connect plants. Field experiments have measured carbon transfer between ectomycorrhizal tree species under particular conditions.[3] That is real evidence worth taking seriously. It is not evidence for every larger story told about the network.

Popular accounts have sometimes taken much larger steps. Trees are described as talking, warning one another, feeding offspring, or participating in a forest-scale network analogous to a brain or social system. Some narrower hypotheses beneath those descriptions are testable, but the stronger the verb, the more evidence it requires. A critical 2023 assessment found that evidence for several broad claims about common mycorrhizal networks in forests was too variable, limited, or confounded to support the generalizations often made about them, and reported no peer-reviewed published evidence for the specific claim that mature trees preferentially send resources and defense signals to offspring through CMNs.[4]

That does not mean the networks are unreal or the research program has failed. It means several questions must remain separate. A fungal connection can be real without carrying a biologically used message. Material can move without establishing encoded communication. An interaction can benefit a recipient without demonstrating altruism. Genuine communication between organisms can occur without creating a communicating forest-level individual.

If two trees change after one is damaged, perhaps a signal moved between them. Perhaps both responded to an airborne compound, shared pathogen, soil disturbance, common environmental change, or another pathway. If labeled carbon later appears elsewhere, we still need to identify the route, magnitude, mechanism, functional consequence, and plausible alternatives. The attractive story is not the enemy; it is the hypothesis generator. Reality decides how much survives.

The same problem has now appeared twice. With resonance, detecting a physical relationship was easier than establishing that the relationship carried information. With a biological network, detecting a connection is easier than establishing what role the connection plays for the larger system. Measurement gives us the relation. Experiment has to earn the function.

### Information for Whom?

The word information creates another trap because scientists use it in several legitimate but different ways. Suppose humidity at dawn strongly predicts a later biological state. In a statistical sense, one variable can reduce uncertainty about another. That formal relationship does not depend on a scientist being present. But a scientist may exploit the relationship for prediction long before we have shown that the biological system itself uses the variable in a regulatory process.

That is the intended meaning of a useful shorthand:

**Information can exist for the scientist before it exists for the system.**

Modern analysis can find predictive structure across enormous collections of variables. Those relationships can be scientifically valuable even when no subsystem uses them as signals. Prediction is therefore not enough to establish functional information.

Intervention helps, but causal influence is not yet functional information either. A falling branch can alter another branch without serving as a signal for it. To make the stronger claim, we should look for state-dependent or selective use within an identifiable regulatory architecture. Manipulate the candidate signal while controlling alternatives. Ask whether distinguishable signal states produce reproducibly different downstream responses and whether those responses contribute to a measurable function.

Even then, meaning requires restraint. Scientists may label a signal “danger,” “density,” “injury,” or “hunger” because those words summarize conditions associated with it. The system does not need a human concept of danger for a physical signal to participate in regulation. Statistical information, functional information, and semantic interpretation should not be collapsed into one word.

### There May Be No Control Room

Human beings often expect important organization to have a center. Governments have capitals. Companies have headquarters. Machines often have controllers. That familiarity can make a centralized command point feel like a requirement for large-scale organization. Biology complicates the intuition.

Ant colonies can produce organized foraging, construction, defense, task allocation, and network maintenance through interactions among individuals. Deborah Gordon's review of collective behavior across ant systems emphasizes feedback, local interactions, environmental dynamics, and variation in how information regulating behavior is distributed or spatially centralized.[5] No single worker needs to possess a complete plan of the colony for collective behavior to emerge.

Development offers another example. Cells contain and respond to molecular, positional, mechanical, and regulatory information, yet no individual cell contains a miniature finished organism directing construction from above. Reliable anatomy can arise through distributed interactions among changing cellular states. Engineered networks and decentralized control systems can likewise solve problems without one component representing every state of the whole.

**A system-level function does not require a system-level control room.**

The rule cuts both ways. The absence of a control room cannot dismiss higher-level organization, but neither can decentralization prove it. Distributed dynamics occur in systems we would never call organisms. What matters is whether the collective function belongs to an independently justified higher-level unit.

### Who Owns the Message?

Imagine two neighboring organisms exchanging a chemical signal. The molecules are identifiable, responsive machinery can be found, and behavior changes. Who owns the function?

If each organism uses the interaction in service of its own independent regulation, communication can occur within a larger ecological network without belonging to that network as an integrated individual. The stronger possibility begins only after the larger unit has earned an independent boundary. Then we can ask whether selectively disrupting the exchange degrades a stable higher-level variable or function, whether the effect survives turnover or substitution of particular components, and whether a higher-level model improves prospective prediction or intervention against fair lower-level rivals given comparable information and modeling resources.

This is the Macroscopic Life Ownership Test, and it is project synthesis rather than a borrowed consensus definition: Does communication merely occur inside the proposed system, or does its function belong to the organization of the proposed system?

The test cannot bootstrap itself. We cannot draw a circle around a forest because signals occur within it, call the enclosed region a communicator, and then use the communication as proof that the circle was correct. The boundary must be earned independently. Ordinary mechanisms must remain free to win. The higher-level description must do scientific work rather than merely provide a more dramatic noun.

### Return to the Forest

Return to our imagined forest before dawn. The mysterious wave still crosses the sensor network, but now the experiment has three jobs.

First, establish the phenomenon. Reproduce the pattern, identify what physically changes, measure plausible carriers, and specify in advance which signal states should distinguish which conditions. At the same time, give weather, light, soil moisture, temperature, hydrology, topography, seasonality, pathogens, diffusion, fungal structure, and measurement artifacts a fair chance to explain the observation. Discovery data do not get to confirm the pattern that discovered them.

Second, attack the mechanism. Perturb the proposed pathway with interventions designed to be as specific as possible, alongside sham controls and measurements of collateral damage. Where feasible, use more than one independent way of changing the pathway. A severed connection may alter water, tissue damage, chemistry, and stress at once; disappearance of an effect after such a manipulation is not automatically evidence for the proposed signal. Convergent perturbations, selective downstream responses, and recovery of function can strengthen the causal case without turning any single intervention into proof.

Third, test the level. Compare competing boundaries. Perhaps the meaningful organization follows hydrology rather than the visual edge of the forest. Perhaps a fungal topology matters. Perhaps no higher-level boundary survives. Then ask prospectively whether treating the candidate whole as an organized information-using unit improves prediction or intervention over a strong lower-level rival given fair access to relevant data, model capacity, and experimental resources. Finally, take the prediction somewhere else: another site, season, disturbance, realization, or research team.

The point is not to protect the signal. The point is to give it every reasonable opportunity to disappear.

### What Could Survive

Several outcomes would count as scientific progress. In one, the apparent communication collapses under common-driver or local-mechanism controls. The pattern remains real and perhaps useful, but the stronger interpretation is unnecessary. That is consistent with the lower-level-sufficiency outcome already defined in this book.

In another, genuine communication among components survives. Pathways can be manipulated, responses are selective, and the interactions have measurable functions, but the higher-level ownership claim fails. The forest contains communication without becoming one communicator. That is still a discovery. Real organization can exist below the stronger claim we hoped to test.

A stronger result would require much more: communication survives common-driver controls; its disruption selectively degrades an independently justified higher-level organization; competing boundaries perform worse; the result survives component turnover or alternative realizations where relevant; the higher-level account provides comparative prediction or intervention advantage; and replication succeeds. Only then should the canonical Model A/B/C framework be applied according to the property actually demonstrated. Chapter 7 does not redefine those models.

Even an extraordinary communication result would leave the property ladder intact. Communication is not agency. Agency is not intelligence. Intelligence is not consciousness. Evidence for one property neither establishes nor automatically excludes another; each claim must face its own operational tests.

### The Thing That Remains

Everything we have described can happen in the present: a signal appears, another part of a system responds, and the interaction ends. Living systems can do something more difficult. Their next response can depend on what happened before because the earlier event changed something physical that persisted.

A scar persists. A crater persists. Neither persistence nor physical history alone is memory. But memory cannot exist without some trace of the past remaining capable of re-entering future causal processing. A retained trace is necessary; whether it becomes functional, selective, or adaptive memory is a harder question.

Suppose our forest experiences a disturbance. The immediate signal disappears. Months later the disturbance returns, and the response is different. Did an earlier physical change alter what happened next? Does that retained influence belong to individual organisms, to local ecological history, or to some larger organization?

The signal has carried us as far as it can. Now we need to know whether yesterday can remain inside tomorrow.

**Can the whole remember?**
