# Source verification

## [3] Positive/common-mycorrhizal-network evidence — VERIFIED

### Primary field evidence

Simard, S. W., Perry, D. A., Jones, M. D., Myrold, D. D., Durall, D. M., & Molina, R. (1997). “Net transfer of carbon between ectomycorrhizal tree species in the field.” *Nature*, 388, 579–582. DOI: `10.1038/41557`.

What it supports:
- compatible plant species can share mycorrhizal fungi/common mycelial connections;
- carbon transfer between ectomycorrhizal tree species was investigated and measured in a field setting;
- the CMN/resource-transfer research program has real empirical foundations.

What it does NOT support:
- universal forest-wide communication;
- intentional transfer;
- altruism;
- semantic messages;
- forest-level agency, intelligence, consciousness, or individuality.

### Balanced review

van der Heijden, M. G. A., & Horton, T. R. (2009). “Socialism in soil? The importance of mycorrhizal fungal networks for facilitation in natural ecosystems.” *Journal of Ecology*, 97, 1139–1150. DOI: `10.1111/j.1365-2745.2009.01570.x`.

What it supports:
- fungal individuals can colonize/interconnect multiple plants;
- resource transfer and facilitative effects have empirical literature;
- effects vary strongly by plant/fungal identity and study system, with positive, negative, and null outcomes.

Why it is useful here:
It supplies the symmetric treatment demanded by Pass 8F: real connectivity and transfer literature exists, while ecological effects are heterogeneous and should not be inflated into stronger communication/agency claims.

Optional additional study if needed later:
Teste et al. (2009), *Ecology*, DOI `10.1890/08-1884.1`, experimentally examined access to mycorrhizal networks, seedling performance, and carbon/nitrogen transfer in Douglas-fir forest systems.

## [4] Critical CMN source — VERIFIED AND METADATA CLOSED

Karst, J., Jones, M. D., & Hoeksema, J. D. (2023). “Positive citation bias and overinterpreted results lead to misinformation on common mycorrhizal networks in forests.” *Nature Ecology & Evolution*, 7, 501–511. DOI: `10.1038/s41559-023-01986-1`.

Published online 13 February 2023; issue April 2023. An author correction appeared at *Nature Ecology & Evolution* 7, 623 (2023), DOI `10.1038/s41559-023-02035-7`, removing extraneous references from the original article. The correction does not reverse the article's central caution about overgeneralized CMN claims.

What it supports:
- concern that popular and some scientific claims about CMNs can outrun evidence;
- field evidence is variable and some common generalizations remain insufficiently supported;
- alternative explanations and citation practices matter.

What it does NOT support:
- claim that CMNs do not exist;
- claim that interplant transfer never occurs;
- blanket dismissal of mycorrhizal ecology.

The v0.2 prose already uses this source within those limits.

## [5] Social-insect distributed organization — VERIFIED

Gordon, D. M. (2019). “The Ecology of Collective Behavior in Ants.” *Annual Review of Entomology*, 64, 35–50. DOI: `10.1146/annurev-ento-011118-111923`.

What it supports:
- ant colonies provide multiple examples of collective behavior and task/activity regulation;
- colony behavior can arise through interaction dynamics without requiring a single worker to contain a complete colony plan.

Additional open-access support:
Gordon, D. M. (2014). “The Ecology of Collective Behavior.” *PLOS Biology*, 12(3), e1001805. DOI: `10.1371/journal.pbio.1001805`.

This source explicitly describes collective behavior as operating without central control to regulate activity and growth.

Optional narrower review:
Gordon, D. M. (2016). “From division of labor to the collective behavior of social insects.” *Behavioral Ecology and Sociobiology*, 70, 1101–1108. DOI: `10.1007/s00265-015-2045-3`.

What these sources do NOT support:
- all ant colonies meet one uniform higher-level-individuality criterion;
- colony collective behavior automatically establishes agency, intelligence, or consciousness;
- distributed organization alone establishes system ownership.

The v0.2 wording is appropriately narrower than these stronger claims.
