# Chapter 14
# The Inheritance

Open an old family photograph and the experiment seems almost too easy.

A child has her father's eyes. A grandson carries the shape of his grandmother's face. Brothers tilt their heads the same way when they laugh. A voice, a gesture, a way of standing in a doorway can seem to travel through a family as clearly as a surname.

We call this inheritance because some of it is inheritance. But the photograph cannot tell us what crossed the generation.

Families share more than ancestry. They can share food, language, homes, neighborhoods, expectations, habits, pollutants, microbes, customs, and histories. A child can resemble a parent because something was transmitted through a biological lineage, because both developed under similar conditions, because one learned from the other, or because several of those pathways operated together.

The resemblance is real.

The mechanism is still on trial.

**RESEMBLANCE IS NOT YET HEREDITY.**

The question left by the previous chapter is therefore harder than it first appeared. If a whole can reproduce, can what differs between wholes survive the journey?

To answer it, we need to know what actually made the journey.

## The Difference Is the Detector

Return to the seed.

If every parent and every offspring were identical in every measurable respect, reproduction might still occur, but heredity would be difficult to dissect. There would be no contrast among lineages telling us which differences persist and which disappear.

Variation gives us that contrast.

Imagine two parent lineages that differ in some prospectively measured state. Call the difference D.

`P1[D1] → O1[?]`

`P2[D2] → O2[?]`

If descendants of P1 tend toward D1 while descendants of P2 tend toward D2, we have discovered a pattern worth explaining.

But we have not yet discovered its mechanism.

That distinction matters. Variation is not itself heredity. It is the difference that allows heredity to become experimentally visible.

We can now ask whether D follows lineage, environment, components, developmental reconstruction, inherited surroundings, or some filtering process that occurs after the descendant begins.

Without variation, those explanations can hide inside similarity. With variation, we can force them to compete.

## Memory Is Not Inheritance

We have met persistence before.

A tree can retain the physical consequences of a wound. A nervous system can remain changed by an earlier event. A forest can carry the effects of drought, fire, disease, or disturbance into its later state.

Chapter 8 called this a memory problem: does history leave a physical difference that later changes what the system does?

Heredity adds a boundary that memory does not require.

The causal difference must cross a lineage relation from a parent to a descendant.

A state can persist for centuries inside one continuing system without ever being inherited. And a descendant can resemble its parent without inheriting the relevant state from it.

Memory asks whether the past remains causally available later.

Heredity asks whether lineage carries a causal difference across generations.

Those questions can overlap. They are not interchangeable.

## The Same House

Imagine two generations living in the same house.

They drink water from the same pipes. They eat similar foods. They breathe the same air. They hear the same language and absorb many of the same routines. If a measurable trait appears in both generations, ancestry and environment are tangled together before we begin.

Move the descendant to a different environment and some differences may disappear. Others may remain.

This intuition sits behind common-garden and reciprocal-environment experiments. Descendants from different lineages are raised or measured under matched conditions, or environmental conditions are exchanged, so that lineage and present environment stop pointing in exactly the same direction.

If a lineage-associated difference persists in a common environment, a simple current-environment explanation becomes weaker. If the difference follows the new environment instead, environmental reconstruction becomes stronger.

But the common garden is not a machine that separates genes from everything else.

Descendants can arrive carrying maternal effects, epigenetic states, cytoplasmic factors, symbionts, microbiomes, developmental histories, transmitted resources, or other lineage-associated differences. A matched present environment does not erase the past.

The experiment therefore does not ask, “genes or environment?”

It asks which causal channels still carry the difference when particular alternatives are separated.

## What Counts as Inherited?

DNA is central to biological heredity. It is not the only scientifically discussed inheritance channel.

Depending on the organism and framework, lineage-associated effects can involve epigenetic states, cytoplasmic factors, parental effects, symbionts, ecological modifications, and social or cultural transmission. These categories should not be treated as interchangeable. Their mechanisms, stability, fidelity, timescales, and evidentiary strength differ.[1]

This is especially important when epigenetics enters popular discussion. Evidence that an epigenetic state can persist across generations in one organism does not authorize us to assume the same mechanism in another. Claims about plants, microbes, laboratory animals, and humans carry different evidentiary burdens, and claims of human transgenerational epigenetic inheritance require particular caution.[2]

The rule is simple even when the mechanisms are not:

**AN INHERITANCE CHANNEL MUST BE IDENTIFIED, NOT ASSUMED.**

That channel need not be a molecule physically copied intact from parent to offspring. Some inherited states can be rebuilt in descendants through lineage-dependent processes.

This gives us a deeper distinction.

## Rebuilt Each Generation

We often imagine heredity as copying.

Something in the parent is duplicated and placed in the offspring. The metaphor works beautifully for some molecular processes, but it can become misleading when applied to complex traits and developmental systems.

A descendant state can sometimes be reconstructed rather than directly replicated. The parent-offspring lineage preserves causal organization that helps rebuild the state in the next generation.[3]

That does not make the state imaginary or uncaused. It changes what we should look for.

The mature trait does not need to exist as a tiny completed object inside the parent. What must persist is enough lineage-dependent causal structure to make the descendant outcome systematically dependent on its ancestry under the conditions being tested.

**HEREDITY DOES NOT REQUIRE PERFECT COPYING. IT REQUIRES LINEAGE-DEPENDENT CAUSAL CONTINUITY.**

That principle makes higher-level heredity possible in theory without granting it to any particular higher-level system.

A forest-level property, for example, would not need to be packaged like a chromosome. But if we claim that the property is inherited by forests, we must identify how forest lineage contributes to rebuilding it and show that lower-level inheritance and environmental reconstruction do not already explain the result.

## The Selection Counterfeit

Now imagine that transmission is noisy.

A parent has state D. Its descendants begin with many different states. During development, environmental filtering or differential survival removes most of them. Only descendants resembling D remain when we finally measure the mature generation.

Parent and offspring now look remarkably alike.

But the resemblance at the end did not necessarily come from faithful transmission at the beginning.

**WHAT SURVIVES SELECTION IS NOT NECESSARILY WHAT WAS FAITHFULLY TRANSMITTED.**

This is why timing matters.

Measure the descendant at founding. Measure again during development. Measure again after strong filtering has occurred.

`founding → development → post-filtering state`

If D is already lineage-associated at founding, the evidence for transmission strengthens. If D emerges only after particular variants are eliminated or environments channel development toward the parental state, then selection or reconstruction may be creating the appearance of fidelity.

This chapter therefore has to protect itself from the next one.

Selection can counterfeit heredity if we look only at survivors.

## Heredity Is Not Heritability

There is another word that sounds similar enough to cause trouble: heritability.

In quantitative genetics, heritability describes how variation in a trait within a specified population and environment is statistically related to genetic variation or, depending on the measure, to transmissible variation under a defined model.[4]

It is not the percentage of an individual's trait that was “caused by genes.” It is not a universal constant attached to a trait. Change the population or environment and the estimate can change.

A high heritability estimate also does not identify the molecular mechanism carrying a trait.

Heredity and heritability therefore answer different questions.

One concerns causal continuity across lineage. The other concerns the statistical structure of variation in a specified population and context.

We need both kinds of thinking in biology, but we cannot exchange the words when the argument becomes difficult.

## Two Forest Families

Now return to the forest.

Suppose Chapter 13's reproduction problem has survived far enough that we can prospectively identify candidate parent and offspring forest units. We still do not assume that forest-level heredity exists.

Before descendants form, we identify two candidate parent lineages with a measurable difference.

`P1[D1] → O1[?]`

`P2[D2] → O2[?]`

D cannot be whatever difference looks interesting afterward. It has to be chosen before we see the descendant result.

Perhaps D is a recovery trajectory after disturbance. Perhaps it is a network-level pattern of resource flow, a response threshold, a spatial organization, or a reproducible successional sequence. The exact variable matters less here than the experimental rule: it must genuinely belong to the level being tested and be measured consistently.

Years later, O1 resembles P1 and O2 resembles P2.

At first glance, we have higher-level heredity.

Now try to destroy it.

## Swap the Ground

First attack the environment.

If descendants from P1 develop D1 only on sites with the same soil, hydrology, climate, disturbance history, or other conditions associated with P1, the lineage explanation may be unnecessary.

Where feasible, compare descendant formation under matched environments, exchanged environments, or natural contrasts that separate ancestry from site history.

Entire forests do not need to be lifted out of the ground and traded like potted plants. Mesocosms, provenance studies, historical reconstruction, component manipulations, replicated sites, natural experiments, and model comparison can all help separate causal pathways.

The question is whether D follows lineage when present environment stops serving as its shadow.

## Swap the Parts

Then attack the components.

Suppose O1 resembles P1 because a dominant tree genotype from P1 colonizes O1. Or the same fungal lineage, microbial consortium, seed source, or other component carries the relevant difference.

That could be excellent evidence of inheritance at the component level.

It is not automatically evidence of inheritance by the forest.

Where possible, exchange candidate components, compare their provenance, or model their contribution separately. Ask whether D tracks the identity and ancestry of particular parts or whether the proposed forest-level lineage still adds explanatory or predictive power after those effects are accounted for.

The ownership problem has returned.

Reproduction of the parts was not, by itself, reproduction of the whole. Inheritance by the parts is not, by itself, inheritance by the whole.

## Swap the Legacy

Then attack the inherited environment.

A forest can alter soil chemistry, hydrology, seed banks, deadwood structure, microbial communities, resource distributions, and disturbance conditions. Those modifications can persist and influence what establishes later. Niche-construction theory and ecological-inheritance research give us legitimate scientific language for such historical environmental effects.[5]

Suppose O1 resembles P1 because O1 develops inside an environmental legacy created by P1.

That is causal continuity.

The remaining question is ownership.

Under the explicit framework being tested, is the legacy part of a forest-level inheritance pathway? Or does ecological inheritance fully explain descendant resemblance without requiring us to assign an inherited property to a forest individual?

Vocabulary cannot decide that question for us.

The rival models have to compete.

## Who Owns the Inheritance?

We can now state the Macroscopic Life version of the test.

Call it the **Inheritance Ownership Test**.

When descendant difference D tracks lineage, does treating D as a property transmitted by the proposed whole add predictive, explanatory, or intervention value beyond the strongest fair models based on components, present environment, inherited environment, developmental reconstruction, and selection?

Before running the test, several conditions have to be fixed.

The parent and offspring units must already be independently justified. D must be chosen prospectively. Lineage must be established without using D itself as the proof. Candidate transmission and reconstruction channels must be specified. Where possible, the descendant should be measured before strong filtering hides the founding variation. And the rival models must receive fair information and capacity.

Then make the higher level compete.

Let H be the higher-level inheritance model and B the strongest fair rival.

`PA_inheritance = Performance(H) - Performance(B)`

As before, the prediction target must be prospective, the rivals must be fairly resourced, evaluation must occur in held-out or genuinely new conditions, and calibration and uncertainty must remain visible.

If H does not outperform B, we have not demonstrated a Prediction Advantage for higher-level inheritance.

If H does outperform B, something interesting has happened—but the equation does not get to declare a new biological property by itself. We still need the lineage, the level, the causal pathway, and the strongest replication or triangulation the system permits.

## Let It Fail

Suppose the forest loses again.

Model A says the descendant differences are adequately explained by component inheritance, present environment, ecological inheritance, developmental reconstruction, and filtering. No forest-level inherited property is needed.

That is not a failed experiment. It is an answer.

Model B says a higher-level lineage variable improves prediction or explanation of descendant organization, but the evidence remains insufficient to establish a distinct forest-level inheritance claim.

**MODEL B IS A DISCOVERY, NOT A CONSOLATION PRIZE.**

Perhaps knowing which prior forest organized the source community improves forecasts even after component ancestry and site variables are included. Perhaps a network-level historical variable compresses many lower-level interactions into a useful predictor. That would matter even if we never call the property hereditary at the forest level.

Model C would require more. Under an explicit framework, independently justified parent and offspring units would show prospective lineage-dependent continuity in D that survives the strongest fair component, environmental, reconstruction, and selection rivals, then survives appropriate replication or triangulation.

Even that result would earn only heredity under the tested framework.

It would not establish agency. It would not establish intelligence. It would not establish consciousness. It would not automatically establish that the system is alive under every definition. And it would not yet establish evolution by natural selection at the higher level.

A forest can have history without heredity. It can have heredity without agency. And if higher-level heredity were ever demonstrated, selection would still have to earn itself separately.

## Earth Cannot Inherit From Itself

Earth has a past.

Its present atmosphere depends on earlier atmospheres. Its biosphere contains lineages billions of years old. Geological, chemical, and biological processes leave consequences that shape later conditions.

That gives Earth history. It gives Earth persistence. Some processes within Earth unquestionably involve heredity.

None of that means Earth itself has inherited a planetary trait.

For the property tested in this chapter, heredity requires a lineage relation across generations under an explicit framework. Without independently justified planetary parent and offspring units, saying that Earth “passes on” its properties remains metaphor rather than demonstrated planetary heredity.

The scale of the system cannot rescue a missing generation.

## The Difference That Changes the Future

Return once more to the family photograph.

A difference can survive a generation.

That is already remarkable. Biology depends on continuity that is neither perfect nor absent. Descendants must be connected strongly enough to have histories and different enough that lineages can diverge.

But heredity alone does not give those differences an evolutionary direction.

For that, some inherited differences must affect what happens next. They must alter survival, persistence, reproduction, or the production of further descendants under relevant conditions.

That is a different test.

A difference that survives a generation has a history. A difference that changes who leaves descendants can begin to have an evolutionary future.

**If differences among wholes can survive generations, can those differences change which wholes leave descendants?**
