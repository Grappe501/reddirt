# CHAPTER 9
# THE ANTICIPATION

Draft status: v0.1 — NOT FROZEN

At 4:59 in the morning, the valve is closed.

At five, it opens.

Water moves through a pipe, divides through smaller lines, and enters the soil while the sky is still cool. Nothing has happened yet. The air has not become dangerous. The plants have not begun losing water to the day's worst heat. Yet the system is already responding to an afternoon that has not arrived.

If you knew nothing about the mechanism, the sequence might seem almost impossible. The action comes first. The event that appears to explain the action comes later.

Then we move the heat.

Tomorrow the hottest part of the day does not arrive at the usual hour. The valve still opens at five. The next day the heat never comes at all. The valve opens at five again.

There was no message from noon traveling backward into the morning. There was a clock.

The distinction sounds obvious once we know the mechanism, but it is one of the easiest mistakes to make when we study systems whose histories, signals, and internal states are partly hidden from us. A response can occur before an event and still be generated entirely by causes already present. A schedule can be inherited. A cue can arrive early. An oscillator can carry phase. Evolution can build a response around regularities that existed long before the current organism was born.

**THE FUTURE CAN BE THE TARGET OF CONTROL WITHOUT BEING THE SOURCE OF THE SIGNAL.**

That does not make anticipation unreal. In biology, anticipation is already a legitimate scientific word. Circadian systems can prepare organisms for recurring environmental changes before those changes occur. Physiologists use anticipatory and predictive language for feedforward regulation. Regulatory networks can exploit cues that tend to precede later conditions. The mistake would be to define all of this away merely because we want a stronger word for a stronger claim.[1][2]

The problem is not whether preparation before an event exists.

The problem is what kind of preparation we have found.

## The Clock Inside the Body

Replace the irrigation timer with a living organism.

Day and night repeat. Temperature rises and falls. Food arrives at recurring times. Seasons alter the length of daylight. Biological systems do not always wait for these changes to finish happening before they respond. Endogenous clocks coordinate physiology and behavior with recurring environmental cycles, and under appropriate conditions those rhythms can continue for a time even when the external cycle is removed.[1]

That is more interesting than a timer wired to a valve. The system carries temporal organization inside itself. Its present state contains information about where it is in a recurring cycle, and that state can alter physiology before the expected transition arrives.

Science can legitimately call this anticipation.

But now change the world.

Move the event away from its usual phase. Change the schedule. Make yesterday's reliable relationship unreliable. An oscillator may continue to produce the old preparation because that is what oscillators do. The fact that it acts early does not tell us whether it can revise what early means.

A clock can anticipate a recurring event without establishing a flexible forecast of that event.

That difference matters because the systems we eventually want to investigate—a forest, an ecosystem, perhaps something larger—contain clocks everywhere. They contain organisms whose physiology is organized by day length, temperature, moisture, feeding, reproduction, and season. If millions of components begin changing before winter, the whole landscape can appear to know that winter is coming even if every component is independently following a mechanism already sufficient to explain the pattern.

We have seen this problem before.

A function can exist inside a system before the system owns the function.

Now the function is anticipation.

## When the Cue Lies

Time is not the only thing that can point forward.

The present can contain clues about what tends to happen next. Shortening days can precede winter conditions. Environmental changes can precede physiological challenges. Food-related sensory signals can arrive before nutrients alter internal variables. In feedforward regulation, a system can begin adjusting before the regulated disturbance has fully arrived.[2]

There is nothing mystical about this. The causal chain still moves forward. A cue exists now. The system detects it now. A response begins now. The later event determines whether the preparation was useful.

A cue can therefore be informative about the future without being a message sent by the future.

And that gives us an experiment.

Make the cue lie.

Preserve the cue and omit the event. Preserve the event but move its timing. Change the probability that the event follows the cue. Reverse a relationship that used to be reliable. Keep one signal constant while altering another. If a system simply follows the cue, its behavior should continue to follow the cue until some other mechanism changes it. If it tracks a changing relationship between the cue and what comes next, the response may update.

**CHANGE THE RELATIONSHIP BETWEEN THE CUE AND WHAT COMES NEXT.**

This does not create one universal test of anticipation. Different systems operate on different timescales, and an evolved mechanism may be genuinely anticipatory even if it cannot update during one experiment. But cue reliability gives us something more useful than a word. It gives us a way to separate mechanisms that can otherwise produce remarkably similar behavior.

A system that prepares correctly when the old relationship holds is interesting.

A system that changes its preparation when the relationship changes is more interesting still.

## A Bacterium and Tomorrow

The temptation is to think that the stronger forms of this problem must begin with a brain.

They do not.

In 2008, Ilias Tagkopoulos, Yen-Cheng Liu, and Saeed Tavazoie reported what they called predictive behavior in microbial genetic networks. Their work examined regulatory responses in *Escherichia coli* associated with environmental transitions and explored how correlations between sequential environments could become embedded in regulatory organization. They also examined what happened when familiar environmental relationships were altered.[3]

The phrase is startling if we hear it through ordinary human language. A bacterium predicting sounds like a bacterium thinking ahead. That is not what the evidence requires.

No tiny organism has to sit inside the cell imagining tomorrow.

A regulatory network can be shaped so that a condition encountered now activates machinery useful under a condition that historically tends to come next. If the relationship is reliable enough, responding to the first condition can improve performance under the second. The mechanism can be entirely physical, local, and nonconscious.

This is precisely why the vocabulary must be handled carefully. Predictive behavior can be real without planning. Anticipation can be real without imagination. A present regulatory state can exploit environmental structure without containing anything resembling a human picture of the future.

The bacterial example removes one prejudice and introduces another problem.

Where did the predictive relationship come from?

## Evolution Got There First

Suppose a cue has reliably preceded a challenge for thousands of generations. Organisms that responded to the cue in useful ways may have survived or reproduced differently from organisms that did not. Over time, selection can build regulatory structures that make the present response fit what historically tended to happen later.

The organism alive today did not necessarily learn that relationship during its own lifetime.

Evolution did the learning in a different sense and on a different timescale.

That sentence needs care. Natural selection is not a mind collecting lessons. It changes populations across generations because inherited variants differ in survival and reproduction. But the result can be a present organism whose structure contains usable consequences of old environmental regularities.

Phenotypic plasticity makes the problem richer. Organisms can use environmental cues during development or life to alter phenotype, and whether such plastic responses are useful depends partly on how reliably those cues predict conditions that matter later.[4] When environments change and old cues become unreliable, a response that was once adaptive can become poorly matched to what follows.

So there are several histories hiding inside the same apparently predictive act. A response may have been shaped across generations. It may be adjusted during development. It may be learned within a lifetime. It may be continuously recalculated. More than one of these processes can operate at once.

**EVOLUTION CAN BUILD FUTURE-APPROPRIATE PREPARATION WITHOUT WITHIN-LIFETIME FORECAST LEARNING.**

That is not a demotion of biological anticipation. It is a demand that we identify where the relevant adaptation or updating occurs.

The same problem becomes unavoidable when we move upward in scale. If trees, fungi, microbes, insects, and other organisms each contain evolved mechanisms that prepare them for recurring conditions, their combined activity can generate a striking landscape-scale pattern. The forest may change before drought or winter because its components are full of anticipatory machinery.

That does not yet tell us whether anticipation belongs to the forest.

## What We Can Predict

Imagine that we place sensors throughout the forest and collect years of data. Soil moisture. Sap flow. canopy temperature. microbial activity. atmospheric conditions. carbon exchange. root-zone measurements. Remote sensing adds another layer. Machine learning finds combinations of variables that reliably predict drought stress weeks before we would otherwise recognize it.

We have discovered something important.

But who made the prediction?

The model did.

A state of the forest can contain measurable information about a later state. In information theory, predictive information can be quantified as information the past or present carries about what comes later, and biological sensory systems can contain measurable predictive information.[5] Yet the ability of a scientist to decode the future from a system does not establish that the system uses the decoded information to regulate itself.

Chapter 7 gave us a rule: information can exist for the scientist before it exists for the system.

The future-facing version is just as important:

**PREDICTIVE INFORMATION CAN EXIST FOR THE OBSERVER BEFORE PREDICTIVE CONTROL EXISTS FOR THE SYSTEM.**

The difference is causal use.

Suppose variable X predicts drought extraordinarily well. If we can perturb X without otherwise changing the relevant system and the forest's preparation remains unchanged, X may be a superb marker for us and a poor candidate for a control variable used by the forest. If changing X changes present preparation in the direction predicted by a mechanistic hypothesis, the case becomes stronger.

This is difficult in biology because variables are entangled. Perturbations have side effects. Some relevant states cannot be isolated cleanly. Large systems may permit only partial interventions or natural experiments. But the logic remains: decoding is not the same as use.

Engineers give us a clean positive control. In model predictive control, a controller uses a model to evaluate possible future trajectories and selects current actions in light of those predicted trajectories.[6] The current action can therefore change because predicted later states change even when the immediate situation is similar.

That does not mean a cell, forest, or planet implements model predictive control.

**A MECHANISM CAN SHOW US WHAT FUTURE-SENSITIVE CONTROL WOULD LOOK LIKE WITHOUT SHOWING THAT NATURE USES THAT MECHANISM.**

The engineered case simply proves that the distinction is coherent. A system can respond only to the present, or it can contain a mechanism in which predicted trajectories participate in choosing what happens now.

The question is how we would tell the difference in nature.

## The Error After Expectation

One possibility is to make an expectation fail.

An event normally arrives after a cue. Then it does not. A seasonal transition shifts. A reward comes late. A familiar sequence is interrupted. Something inside the system changes after the violation, and future behavior changes with it.

It is tempting to call the difference a prediction error.

Not so fast.

A system can respond to novelty without having predicted anything. Homeostatic error can appear when a regulated variable departs from a set range. Damage can trigger a response. Adaptation can alter sensitivity. A mismatch signal is not automatically evidence that the system had an independently identifiable expectation about what should have happened.

**AN ERROR SIGNAL IS NOT A PREDICTION ERROR UNTIL THE PREDICTION IS INDEPENDENTLY IDENTIFIED.**

Prediction-error concepts are important across neuroscience and computational accounts of learning, perception, and action, but the terminology spans different models and mechanisms.[7] We cannot import the word into a new scale and treat the word itself as evidence.

The order of operations matters. Identify the candidate prediction. Specify what should happen if it is violated. Violate it. Measure the resulting change. Then test whether that change alters later preparation.

For this project, it helps to arrange the possibilities without pretending they are universal natural kinds:

`Reactive Regulation → Cue-Anticipatory Regulation → Entrained Anticipation → Forecast-Sensitive Control → Flexible Model-Based Anticipation`

The first responds after disturbance. The next can prepare from a present cue. An entrained system can use endogenous timing to prepare for recurring change. Forecast-sensitive control changes current action with future-relevant estimates that survive simple cue and phase manipulations. The strongest category would update flexibly when familiar predictive relationships change and would use the updated state causally in present control.

This is a Macroscopic Life evidentiary ladder, not a declaration that every scientific field must use the words this way.

Now we can return to the forest.

## The Forest Before the Drought

Imagine a forest in late spring.

Rainfall has been thinning. Days are longer. Soil near the surface is drying. The atmosphere is drawing more water from leaves. The seasonal trajectory resembles patterns that, in previous years, preceded severe drought.

Before the worst drought arrives, measurements begin to change. Water use shifts. Stomatal regulation changes. Root allocation may change. Microbial activity and carbon exchange move with the changing conditions. Seen from above and across time, the forest appears to be reorganizing before the crisis.[4]

The sentence almost writes itself:

The forest knows drought is coming.

Do not write that sentence into the science.

Each tree may be responding independently to current moisture and atmospheric demand. Organisms may carry seasonal clocks. Roots may follow local gradients. Microbes may respond to changing substrates. Species composition may shift which processes dominate. Soil and hydrology may already be changing before whatever date we have chosen to call the beginning of drought. Evolved mechanisms may prepare individual organisms for recurring seasonal stress.

The forest can look early without the forest owning the forecast.

And yet we should not retreat too quickly either. If higher-level interactions genuinely constrain what components do, if distributed states persist across changing components, if system-scale variables capture causal organization that component-only models miss, then dismissing the whole in advance would repeat the mistake this book has been built to avoid.

The answer is not to believe the forest.

The answer is to make the season lie.

## Make the Season Lie

In a real forest, we cannot drag summer across a calendar with our hands. But climate variability, controlled field manipulations, greenhouse or mesocosm experiments, irrigation treatments, rainout shelters, altered watering schedules, cross-site comparisons, and unusual natural events can change pieces of the relationship between cues and later conditions.

The experimental logic is simple enough to remember.

First, establish what normally predicts what.

Second, break or shift the relationship.

Third, ask what the system follows—and whether it updates.

Suppose two otherwise comparable histories contain similar present soil moisture but different temporal structures. Suppose a familiar atmospheric cue no longer predicts drought. Suppose drought arrives earlier than the seasonal phase that normally precedes it. Suppose a cue appears and the expected event is omitted. We do not need every manipulation to be possible in one forest. We need converging experiments that force competing explanations to make different predictions.

A clock model may follow phase. A cue-response model may follow the immediate cue. A component model may reconstruct the pattern from independent organismal responses. An evolutionary model may explain stable preparation that does not update within the experimental timescale. A flexible statistical model may predict the same behavior from present and past variables without requiring a distinct higher-level forecast state.

The stronger model does not get weaker opponents.

Fair Rival Resource still applies. Competing models receive comparable data quality, relevant causal variables, model capacity, tuning opportunity, and held-out validation. If a sophisticated current-state or component model explains the result as well as the higher-level future-sensitive model, the stronger interpretation has not earned an advantage.

Now add intervention.

Identify a candidate present state that carries future-relevant structure. Where technically and ethically feasible, perturb that state or a mechanism that maintains it. Ask whether current preparation changes. Then alter the predictive relationship and ask whether the state updates. Finally, test the revised model prospectively under another temporal regime, site, or realization.

A label such as “previously exposed” or “early-drought year” can help us discover a pattern. It is not a mechanism. A variable that forecasts beautifully for us is not automatically a variable used by the system.

This experiment can fail at every step.

It should.

## Who Owns Tomorrow?

Suppose the forest-scale signal survives.

We still have the ownership problem.

Can the entire pattern be reconstructed from independent component anticipation plus shared environmental forcing? If every tree, microbe, fungal network, soil process, and hydrological process contributes exactly what the higher-level model appears to predict, then anticipation may be abundant inside the forest without belonging to the forest as a distinct causal unit.

But suppose interactions matter. Suppose a variable defined at an independently justified forest scale captures organization that cannot be replaced without loss of prospective prediction or intervention leverage. Suppose changing that variable alters current preparation even after strong component and environmental rivals receive the same resources. Suppose the result survives altered cue relationships and a new realization.

Then the higher-level description has begun to earn scientific work.

We can write the comparison compactly:

`PA_anticipation = Performance(Future-Sensitive Model) - Performance(Best Fair Rival)`

This is project synthesis, not a standard field equation. If `PA_anticipation > 0`, the future-sensitive model has demonstrated predictive utility under that fair comparison.

Nothing more should be smuggled into the result.

Prediction Advantage does not establish consciousness. It does not establish subjective expectation. It does not establish foresight. It does not establish agency by itself. It does not establish that the forest is an organism. It does not establish that a predictive state is a representation in every philosophical sense. It does not establish ontological independence.

It tells us that we lose something scientifically useful when we remove the future-sensitive higher-level description.

That can already matter.

Model A remains possible: lower-level clocks, cues, environmental forcing, evolved programs, and component responses are sufficient.

Model B remains possible: a higher-level future-sensitive variable improves explanation, prediction, or intervention without earning a stronger property claim.

And Model B is still a discovery, not a consolation prize.

A property-specific Model C result would require much more: an independently justified higher-level unit, a future-relevant physical state, causal use of that state in current preparation, flexible updating when predictive relationships change, failure of strong component and common-driver replacements, prospective Prediction and Intervention Advantage, and replication across altered conditions.

Even then, the property earned in this chapter would be anticipation—not consciousness, not foresight, and not a general certificate of intelligence.

## Let the Forest Fail

Imagine the experiment ends differently.

We move drought timing and the preparation stays locked to seasonal phase. The clock wins.

Or the forest-scale pattern follows a present moisture cue even after that cue stops predicting later drought. The cue wins.

Or every apparent higher-level effect can be reconstructed from organism-level responses and shared environment. The components win.

Perhaps our sensors contain enough information to forecast drought beautifully, but perturbing the candidate predictive state does nothing to current preparation. The observer wins: predictive information existed for us, but predictive control by the proposed system was not demonstrated.

Perhaps another boundary performs equally well. Perhaps the Prediction Advantage disappears on a new site. Perhaps the strongest statistical rival absorbs the effect.

Then we should say what happened without embarrassment.

The forest prepared before drought. The preparation was real. It may have been adaptive. Its components may have contained legitimate anticipatory mechanisms. Its present state may even have contained measurable information about what came later.

The stronger forest-level forecast claim was not demonstrated.

That is not an empty result. It tells us where the function lives and which explanatory level earns the work.

A theory that cannot survive that sentence is not ready to study tomorrow.

## A Preferred Future

We began with a valve opening before the heat.

Once we knew the clock, the mystery shrank. Then biology complicated the answer again. Living systems really can prepare before recurring changes. Present cues can carry information about later conditions. Evolution can build regulatory machinery around those regularities. Microbial networks can exhibit behavior scientists legitimately describe as predictive. A system can even contain states from which the future is measurable before it arrives.

None of this requires the future to reach backward through time.

What matters is what exists now: clocks, cues, retained states, regulatory networks, learned associations, evolved structures, models, interactions, and constraints. The scientific problem is to determine which of those states merely correlate with what comes next and which causally participate in choosing what happens now.

If we ever find the stronger case at a higher level, it will not be because a forest seemed wise. It will be because a future-sensitive description survived strong rivals, intervention, altered temporal relationships, boundary competition, prospective testing, and replication.

That would be enough to change the question again.

A system can act with respect to what is likely to happen. But control often requires something else as well. It requires that some outcomes count differently from others. A thermostat does not merely predict temperature; its mechanism is organized around a range. A biological regulatory system can preserve variables within viable limits. A controller can compare trajectories because some trajectories satisfy constraints and others do not.

We encountered this distinction earlier:

Function is not goal. Goal is not purpose.

Anticipation does not erase it.

A future-sensitive system may estimate where the world is going without owning any preferred future at the level we are studying. Stable dynamics can pull a system toward an attractor. Selection can build mechanisms that preserve viability. Components can pursue local regulatory targets whose combined effect resembles a goal of the whole.

Tomorrow is one problem.

A preferred tomorrow is another.

**Can the whole have a goal?**
