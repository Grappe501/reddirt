# ACT IV — WHEN THE NETWORK BEGINS TO THINK

## Chapter 12 — Intelligence Without a Little Man

Where is the intelligence in an ant colony?

Point to it.

Not to an ant.

To the colony's intelligence.

Which ant decides how many workers should forage today?

Which ant contains the map of the colony's entire food-discovery process?

Which ant is the colony?

The question feels strange because we are accustomed to putting intelligence inside an object—usually a brain, usually inside a skull.

But intelligence does not always arrive with a little man sitting at a control panel.

### Functions Can Be Distributed

A **distributed system** is one in which multiple parts in different places contribute to a larger function.

Think about a restaurant during a packed dinner service.

One person greets customers. Another cooks. Servers move food. Someone washes dishes. A manager may coordinate portions of the operation, but no one person performs the restaurant.

Or think about the internet.

Information moves through routers, cables, servers, data centers, software, wireless links, and countless machines.

There is no computer you can unplug and say:

`There. I have unplugged the internet.`

Distribution alone does not create intelligence.

It establishes something more basic:

> **A function can belong to an organized network even when no single component performs the entire function.**

We have already seen this inside the body.

Now we are going to follow it upward.

### Do Not Call Everything a Mind

Before we do, we need to separate words that popular conversation constantly collapses together.

**Alive** means participating in processes we classify as life. Even that category becomes difficult around cases such as viruses.

An **organism** is a living biological system organized as a functional whole.

An **individual** is an entity treated as one continuing unit at the level being studied.

There is no universally accepted scientific definition of **intelligence** that settles every case. Different fields emphasize different abilities. For this book, intelligence will mean the capacity to acquire and use information in ways that support problem solving, learning, adaptation, prediction, or effective response selection.

That is a working definition.

It is not permission to call every adaptive process intelligent.

**Agency** goes farther. An agent selects and carries out actions in relation to functionally defined states or goals. Agency does not automatically imply conscious intention.

And **consciousness** goes farther still. It concerns subjective experience—the fact that, for humans and presumably many other animals, there is something it feels like to experience the world.

So keep the staircase intact:

**SIGNAL ≠ INFORMATION ≠ INTEGRATION ≠ INTELLIGENCE ≠ AGENCY ≠ CONSCIOUSNESS**

A system can climb one step without reaching the next.

This distinction will save us from some spectacular mistakes later.

### Your Brain Already Breaks the Intuition

Your brain contains vast numbers of neurons connected into networks.

No single neuron knows your name.

No neuron understands this sentence.

No neuron contains your childhood as a tiny film.

Yet coordinated neural systems support perception, memory, language, planning, learning, and behavior.

The intelligence is not hiding in one special neuron waiting to be found.

> **A system can possess a capability that none of its components possesses independently.**

That does not make every network a brain.

It destroys the assumption that every system-level capability must be located inside one component.

### Emergence Is Not an Escape Hatch

Watch a flock of birds turn in the sky.

Thousands of bodies change direction, yet there is no pilot above the flock radioing instructions to every bird.

Large-scale patterns can arise from local interactions among parts.

Scientists and philosophers often call this **emergence**.

It is a useful word with a dangerous habit.

People sometimes use it exactly where the explanation stops.

`The behavior emerged.`

Fine.

How?

Which interactions? Which constraints? Which signals? Which feedback loops? Which physical mechanisms?

> **Emergence cannot mean: and then magic happened.**

An emergent property is a higher-level pattern or behavior produced through interactions among components.

The pattern is real.

The mechanism still owes us an explanation.

### Climb the Staircase

Start with a crowd.

People can copy one another, coordinate, panic, celebrate, flee, or move together. The crowd can produce patterns no participant planned.

Move to a flock.

Birds responding to neighbors and environmental conditions can produce coordinated group motion.

Move to an ant colony.

Individual ants use local signals and interactions. Colonies can allocate labor, locate resources, defend nests, and make collective choices.

Move to slime molds.

Some organisms commonly called slime molds can form networks and display remarkably effective problem-solving behavior without a nervous system. Not every organism placed under that informal label behaves the same way, and none of this means slime molds reason like humans.

The narrower lesson is enough:

A nervous system is not the only biological architecture capable of adaptive information processing.

Now move to a corporation.

People occupy specialized roles inside a legally persistent organization. Records and procedures can survive employee turnover. Decisions can emerge from processes involving many people rather than one person's mind.

Then the brain.

Highly integrated neural tissue supports cognitive functions through distributed activity.

Then civilization.

Billions of people. Institutions. Machines. Sensors. Archives. Governments. Markets. Communication networks. Infrastructure. Artificial intelligence.

Stop.

This is not an evolutionary ladder.

A civilization is not `higher` than a brain because it is larger. A corporation is not more evolved than an ant colony. Size, complexity, and technological sophistication do not automatically create individuality.

We are comparing properties.

Nothing more has been earned yet.

### Collective Intelligence

Now we can define **collective intelligence** carefully.

It describes cases in which interactions among multiple components allow a group to solve problems, make decisions, or process information more effectively—or in qualitatively different ways—than isolated members could under comparable conditions.

Science itself is a powerful example.

No scientist knows all of modern science.

Researchers specialize. Instruments gather data. Teams analyze it. Findings are published. Other researchers criticize them, reproduce them, fail to reproduce them, extend them, or reject them. Journals and databases preserve results. Knowledge accumulates across generations.

No isolated scientist could personally reproduce the entire enterprise.

The organized scientific community can perform forms of inquiry unavailable to one person alone.

That does not make science a conscious person.

Capability is not consciousness.

### The Strongest Alternative

This is where we need the theory that may defeat Macroscopic Life.

A **complex adaptive system** contains many interacting parts whose behavior can change in response to their environment, experience, or one another, producing changing large-scale patterns.

Ecosystems, economies, immune systems, and social systems are often studied this way.

A complex adaptive system can be organized.

It can learn in broad functional senses.

It can produce surprising collective behavior.

It can look uncannily alive.

And it may still not be one biological individual.

That is not a minor objection.

It may be the correct answer.

### Give the Middle Ground a Name

We therefore need a category that does not secretly contain our conclusion.

Call it **macroscopic organization**.

> **A macroscopic organization is a system with measurable organization at scales above its constituent agents, without implying organismhood, biological individuality, intelligence, agency, or consciousness.**

The phrase is almost aggressively unromantic.

Good.

It gives us somewhere to put interesting systems without pretending we already know what they are.

From here forward, use three levels:

**MACROSCOPIC PATTERN** — a large-scale regularity exists.

**MACROSCOPIC ORGANIZATION** — the regularity is maintained through integrated causal relationships.

**HIGHER-ORDER INDIVIDUALITY** — converging evidence makes treating the organized whole as an individual scientifically useful and justified.

A traffic jam has a macroscopic pattern.

A power grid has macroscopic organization.

A human body is a biological individual composed of cells.

Civilization?

We have not earned the answer.

### A Useful Description Is Not an Individual

A model has **explanatory power** when it helps account for why observations occur and fit together.

It has **predictive power** when it successfully forecasts outcomes that were not simply added after the fact.

Higher-level descriptions can have both.

Weather systems have large-scale variables. So do economies. So does traffic.

That matters.

But:

> **Explanatory usefulness is evidence that a higher-level pattern matters scientifically. It is not sufficient evidence for biological individuality.**

Or more simply:

> **A useful macro-variable is not yet a new individual.**

### AI Changes the Wiring

Now add artificial intelligence.

**Topology** means the pattern of connections in a network—what is connected to what, and through which routes.

AI can change that topology without being conscious.

An AI system can search enormous information stores, summarize material, translate between languages, classify records, detect patterns, generate text, and connect people to knowledge that previously required many specialists to locate or interpret.

That can reduce information bottlenecks.

It can also create new ones.

If millions of people and institutions depend on the same flawed system, one mistake can spread across the network. Integration increases capability while sometimes increasing shared vulnerability.

AI does not have to become a person to change the architecture of civilization.

It only has to change how information moves.

And human beings have been changing that architecture for thousands of years.

First with speech.

Then writing.

Then printing.

Then wires.

Then radio.

Then light through glass.

Then networks covering the planet.

We did not merely build faster ways to talk.

We built something outside ourselves that increasingly performs functions once trapped inside individual bodies.

## Chapter 13 — The Nervous System We Built Outside Ourselves

Imagine sending a message from one side of the planet to the other.

Now imagine doing it in the year 1200.

Your message has weight.

Someone has to carry it.

A person walks. A horse runs. A ship crosses water. Weather intervenes. Roads disappear. Borders close. Messengers die.

Distance is not an inconvenience.

Distance is part of the communication system.

Today, a message can cross the planet in a fraction of a second.

That change is so familiar that we barely feel how strange it is.

### We Began Moving Information Without Moving People

Writing allowed information to survive the speaker.

Printing allowed one message to be reproduced at enormous scale.

Practical long-distance **telegraph** systems in the nineteenth century allowed coded messages to travel electrically through wires. For the first time at large scale, the message no longer had to move at the speed of the human, horse, or ship carrying it.

Radio sent information through electromagnetic waves without a wire connecting sender and receiver.

**Fiber-optic cables** send information as pulses of light through thin strands of glass or similar material. Vast quantities of digital information now cross continents and oceans this way.

Artificial **satellites** orbit Earth and support communication, navigation, weather observation, scientific measurement, and other functions.

The internet links machines and networks across the planet.

None of this proves an organism appeared.

But something unquestionably changed.

Human information architecture escaped the speed and sensory range of the unaided human body.

### We Built Senses Outside Ourselves

Weather satellites observe atmospheric conditions from orbit.

Radar measures precipitation and storm structure.

Ocean buoys record conditions at sea.

Seismometers monitor vibrations moving through Earth.

Telescopes collect light our eyes could never gather unaided.

Public-health systems collect disease reports across populations.

No person receives all of this directly.

The measurements move through machines, networks, institutions, and human interpreters.

> **Human civilization contains distributed sensing systems whose combined observational reach exceeds that of any unaided individual human.**

Be careful with the sentence.

It is not the same as saying `civilization sees.`

The sensing is physically implemented by instruments, people, computers, and institutions.

Whether civilization itself deserves to be called the sensing agent is a much stronger claim.

We have not earned it.

### Then We Built Memory Outside Ourselves

A **database** is an organized collection of information designed so records can be stored, searched, connected, and retrieved.

Libraries, archives, photographs, recordings, databases, and scientific repositories allow information to persist outside any one brain.

A person dies.

The measurements remain.

A research team dissolves.

The paper remains.

A generation disappears.

The archive remains.

In the broad functional sense from Chapter 10, humans externalized memory.

Again:

External memory does not establish an external mind.

But it changes what the larger network can do.

### The Hurricane No One Can See

Imagine a major hurricane crossing the ocean.

A person standing on a beach sees one horizon.

A satellite sees cloud structure from above.

Radar measures precipitation and movement.

Ocean buoys measure sea conditions.

Aircraft enter storms and gather data.

Weather stations record pressure, wind, temperature, and rainfall.

Numerical models combine measurements with equations describing atmospheric physics.

Meteorologists interpret the results.

Where is the hurricane forecast?

Not in the buoy.

Not in the satellite.

Not in one raindrop.

Not in the eyes of the person standing on the beach.

The useful representation is constructed by integrating distributed pieces.

And now we can ask one of the most dangerous questions in this book.

### What Does Civilization Know That You Do Not?

Read the question carefully.

`Know` is shorthand.

We are not claiming civilization possesses a conscious mind.

The scientific question is whether distributed components can construct an integrated representation or actionable state that no single contributing component possessed beforehand—and whether that integrated state can then change behavior elsewhere in the larger organization.

Consider a **global climate model**.

It combines physical equations, observations, computing, software, measurements, and scientific work distributed across many people and institutions. No single scientist has to personally contain every observation, equation, parameter, software operation, and model state involved in the enterprise.

A particular completed model state can still physically reside in one or more computing systems.

Nothing floats above the hardware.

The point is how the information was constructed.

The finished state can depend on a chain of observation, expertise, computation, storage, and integration extending far beyond any one contributor.

The same principle appears elsewhere.

**Genomics** studies genomes and genetic information using datasets often far beyond what one person could inspect directly.

**Epidemiology** studies patterns and causes of health-related events in populations. A disease pattern can become statistically visible before any clinician personally observes the entire population-level event.

For this book, call an integrated state **whole-only information** when its useful form depends on a distributed physical process, was not fully instantiated in any one contributing component before integration, and can influence later behavior elsewhere in the organization.

The phrase does **not** mean information exists independently of people and machines.

It remains physical—in brains, sensors, records, computer memory, network states, or other material systems.

The strange part is not supernatural ownership.

The strange part is distributed construction.

### The Whole Can Be Wrong

Now we need another brake.

Integration does not guarantee truth.

Suppose ice-cream sales and sunburns both increase in summer.

They are **correlated**—they vary together.

That does not mean buying ice cream causes sunburn.

Hot sunny weather influences both.

Large systems can detect patterns that are real, patterns that are misleading, and patterns that are entirely accidental.

Causal claims become stronger when we understand mechanism, compare competing explanations, exploit natural experiments, or deliberately intervene and observe predicted consequences.

More information does not automatically mean better understanding.

### The Missing Step Is Action

A network can contain staggering quantities of information and still behave incoherently.

Communication can spread truth.

It can also spread noise, panic, propaganda, mistakes, and coordinated falsehood.

So information alone is not the destination.

The sequence is:

**SIGNAL → INFORMATION → COMMUNICATION → INTEGRATION → REGULATION → ACTION**

In ordinary language:

First something changes.

Then the change becomes informative.

Then information moves.

Then separate pieces become coordinated.

Then the system uses that integrated state to regulate something.

Then regulation changes action.

Human civilization is extraordinarily good at producing signals, information, and communication.

It is much less impressive at unified regulation and action.

That gap may tell us more about individuality than all the glowing networks on Earth at night.

### AI Between the Parts

In computing, **middleware** is software that helps different systems communicate or work together.

AI can increasingly play portions of that role in human information networks.

It can search, summarize, translate, classify, generate, compare, and route information between enormous stores of knowledge and human decision-makers.

That may increase effective bandwidth across the network.

It can also create **correlated failure**—many parts failing together because they depend on the same bad model, bad data, bad assumption, or compromised system.

Integration creates capability.

Integration creates vulnerability.

A nervous system is powerful partly because its components are connected.

Damage those connections in the wrong place and the same integration becomes a route to catastrophic failure.

### Cut the Network

In medicine, a **lesion** is an area of damaged tissue.

Scientists have learned about biological function by observing what abilities disappear when particular brain regions, nerves, or other structures are damaged.

We can borrow the experimental logic without pretending civilization is literally a brain.

Sever a major cable.

Interrupt GPS.

Isolate a database.

Disable a communication route.

Remove a coordination system.

What predictable large-scale functions fail?

If removing a component reliably destroys a larger capability, that component was integrated into the function.

That is evidence of integration.

Not organismhood.

But for the first time, we have enough tools to stop asking whether civilization *looks* alive.

We can ask whether it behaves like an individual along dimensions that can actually be tested.

## Chapter 14 — Is Civilization Becoming an Individual?

Look at Earth at night from orbit.

Cities burn across the darkness like clusters of neurons.

Roads branch between them.

Electrical grids span regions. Fiber-optic cables cross oceans. Satellites circle overhead. Aircraft trace paths through the atmosphere. Ships carry matter between continents. Food, fuel, minerals, money, waste, and information move through a planetary human network.

It is a breathtaking image.

Now throw it away.

Rivers branch.

Lightning branches.

Tree roots branch.

Blood vessels branch.

Similar shapes can appear because different systems confront similar physical problems.

The photograph can inspire the question.

It cannot answer it.

> **Analogy is not evidence.**

So forget what civilization looks like.

Ask what it does.

### Two Questions for Every Claim

For each property we evaluate, we will ask two separate questions.

**First:** Is the large-scale property actually present?

**Second:** If it is present, does that property provide evidence that civilization is becoming one individual?

Those questions are easy to blur.

Do not blur them.

Civilization can have strong memory and still not be an organism.

It can transform enormous amounts of energy without having a metabolism of its own.

It can contain extraordinary intelligence without possessing unified agency.

A property can be real while the conclusion remains false.

### Where Is the Edge?

A human has physical, regulatory, informational, causal, and evolutionary boundaries. The skin is not the whole story, but there is a meaningful organismal inside and outside.

Civilization has no comparable single edge.

Nations have borders. Networks have technical boundaries. Institutions have memberships. Supply chains have edges. Humans and technologies occupy much of Earth and extend into space.

These boundaries overlap.

They do not form one clean civilization membrane.

**Large-scale boundary:** weak to moderate.

**Evidence for individuality:** weak.

The first major test is not encouraging.

### Matter Moves

Your body transports oxygen, nutrients, water, hormones, immune cells, and waste through organized systems.

Civilization moves food, water, fuel, minerals, manufactured goods, biological materials, and waste around the planet.

The scale is staggering.

Global transportation and supply networks are genuine macroscopic organization.

But do not smuggle in a controller.

Markets, contracts, geography, governments, regulations, companies, competition, local decisions, and countless independent actors all contribute to the pattern.

**Large-scale matter flow:** strong.

**Unified system-level coordination:** mixed.

**Evidence for individuality:** weak to moderate.

The flow is real.

The ownership of the flow is unsettled.

### Energy Moves Too

A body's **metabolism** transforms matter and energy through coordinated chemical processes that maintain the organism.

Civilization extracts and transforms coal, oil, gas, uranium, sunlight, wind, moving water, biomass, and other energy sources into electricity, heat, motion, computation, and industrial work.

Electrical grids can be deeply integrated regional systems. Fuel networks cross continents.

But `civilization uses energy` is not the same claim as `civilization has a metabolism.`

Many energy systems compete, operate independently, fail separately, and pursue different goals.

**Large-scale energy transformation and distribution:** strong.

**Evidence for civilization-level metabolism:** weak.

**Evidence for individuality:** weak to moderate at most.

Another impressive resemblance survives as organization but weakens as individuality.

### Information May Be the Strongest Case

Telecommunications, satellites, scientific institutions, governments, companies, media, databases, and computation can construct integrated information states no individual contributor possessed beforehand.

That is real.

It may be civilization's strongest macroscopic property.

But integration remains fragmented. Information systems compete. They remain disconnected. They contradict one another. They serve different purposes and different groups.

And distributed construction does not automatically mean civilization owns the resulting information as one agent.

**Information integration:** strong.

**Evidence for individuality:** moderate, but ambiguous.

**Ambiguous** means more than one reasonable explanation remains possible.

### Can Civilization Regulate Itself?

Your body regulates temperature, blood pressure, acidity, glucose, water balance, and many other variables.

Human societies regulate some variables through governments, markets, engineering standards, grid operators, public-health systems, treaties, and institutions.

But most regulation remains divided among **jurisdictions**—geographic or legal areas over which particular authorities have power—or among specialized domains.

There is no single civilization-wide control architecture comparable to organismal regulation.

**Macroscopic regulation:** moderate and domain-specific.

**Evidence for individuality:** weak to moderate.

### Does It Repair Itself?

After storms, earthquakes, wars, cyberattacks, and other disruptions, humans rebuild roads, electrical systems, communication networks, institutions, buildings, and supply chains.

That is real coordinated reconstruction.

But remember the mechanic and the transmission.

Calling the process `civilization repairing itself` may hide the fact that conscious human agents and organizations deliberately perform the work.

Stronger evidence for higher-order repair would require distributed detection of disruption, mobilization of resources, restoration of identifiable large-scale variables, and better explanation from treating the process as repair by the larger organization rather than merely people rebuilding systems they value.

**Coordinated reconstruction:** moderate to strong.

**Evidence for individuality:** weak to moderate and ambiguous.

### Can It Remember After We Die?

Here the answer becomes stronger.

Languages, schools, archives, institutions, books, digital systems, scientific records, and culture preserve information across generations of human turnover.

The people change.

The information can persist.

Civilization unquestionably contains mechanisms for historical organization through component replacement.

**Persistence-through-turnover:** strong.

**Evidence for individuality:** moderate.

Why only moderate?

Because institutions, ecosystems, traditions, and other non-organismal systems can also persist through turnover.

Again, a strong property does not automatically settle the classification.

### Can It Predict?

Weather services forecast storms.

Epidemiologists model disease.

Astronomers calculate eclipses.

Engineers estimate failures.

Institutions prepare for anticipated conditions.

Civilization contains enormous distributed predictive capacity.

But Chapter 11 already gave us the brake:

**Capability distributed across civilization is not automatically capability of civilization as one agent.**

**Distributed predictive capacity:** strong.

**Evidence for individuality:** weak to moderate and ambiguous.

### Can It Choose?

Now we reach a weakness that may matter more than all the strengths.

**Action selection** asks whether a system can choose among possible responses and coordinate implementation.

A human being can experience internal conflict and still produce an integrated action.

Civilization contains billions of people, thousands of governments, companies, institutions, cultures, and competing interests.

Sometimes global coordination happens.

Often it does not.

Civilization can know a threat is coming and fail to respond coherently.

It can possess the information required for action while its components pursue incompatible goals.

**Unified action selection:** weak to moderate.

**Evidence for individuality:** weak.

The failure is revealing.

Perhaps civilization has built senses, memory, and prediction faster than it has built a self.

### Can It Control Internal Conflict?

Multicellular organisms survive partly because lower-level competition is constrained enough that cells do not routinely destroy the whole.

Civilization remains full of war, destructive competition, collective-action problems, and cases where local advantage damages global conditions.

Disagreement is not disease. Pluralism and competing goals can be valuable.

The scientific question is narrower:

Is destructive lower-level competition constrained strongly enough that one higher-level evolutionary fate dominates?

At present, evidence is weak.

**Conflict suppression:** weak.

**Evidence for individuality:** weak or negative.

If civilization is becoming an individual, this may be one of its deepest unfinished problems.

### How Dependent Are the Parts?

Remove most human cells from the body and they cannot continue their normal organismal role independently.

Modern humans are increasingly dependent on agriculture, medicine, energy, infrastructure, logistics, communication, and institutions.

Yet people and communities retain vastly more autonomy than cells in an animal body.

**Component dependence:** increasing but incomplete.

**Evidence for individuality:** weak to moderate.

Interdependence is growing.

Obligate integration is not established.

### The Pattern Outlives Us

Individual humans are born and die.

Languages continue. Legal systems continue. Scientific knowledge continues. Religions, infrastructures, institutions, technologies, and cultural practices can continue.

Civilization-level organization clearly survives component turnover.

**Persistence through turnover:** strong.

**Evidence for individuality:** moderate.

Strong again.

Still not enough.

### Then We Hit the Wall

Reproduction.

A human organism participates in a recognizable life cycle in which new organismal histories begin.

Civilization has no demonstrated equivalent reproductive cycle as one planetary individual.

Colonies can found colonies. Institutions can create branches. Cultures spread. Humans may establish settlements beyond Earth.

Interesting.

Not the same thing.

**Civilization-level reproduction:** unestablished.

**Evidence for individuality:** absent or negative.

Then heredity.

For Darwinian evolution, descendants must inherit relevant organization with enough fidelity that differences among lineages can persist.

Civilization transmits culture and technology, but we do not possess a demonstrated population of civilization individuals reproducing and transmitting civilization-level hereditary organization.

**Civilization-level heredity:** unestablished.

**Evidence for individuality:** absent or negative.

Then higher-level selection.

To demonstrate Darwinian selection among civilizations as individuals, we would need something like multiple civilization-level entities that vary, reproduce, transmit relevant differences, and differ in reproductive success.

We do not have that evidence.

**Higher-level Darwinian selection:** unestablished.

**Evidence for individuality:** absent or negative.

These are not small missing boxes.

They are major failures for a strong biological-individual claim.

### Do Not Add the Scores

At this point the temptation is almost irresistible.

Strong information.

Strong memory.

Strong matter flow.

Strong energy transformation.

Moderate repair.

Add them together and declare victory.

No.

There is no scientifically validated organism score where enough points turn a network into a living individual.

Some properties may matter more than others. Boundary, reproduction, heredity, conflict suppression, and integrated action may be decisive in ways raw connectivity is not.

> **Many strong macroscopic properties do not add arithmetically into organismhood.**

And remember:

> **A useful macro-variable is not yet a new individual.**

### So When Was Civilization Born?

Agriculture changed settlement.

Cities increased density and specialization.

Writing externalized memory.

Industrialization transformed energy use.

Telecommunications collapsed communication distance.

Digital networks intensified information integration.

Artificial intelligence may now alter how information is connected, interpreted, and routed.

Which one was civilization's birth?

Probably none.

The more defensible possibility is that we are observing increasing macroscopic organization without evidence of a completed individuality transition.

### The Responsible Answer

So—is civilization an individual?

**Not in any established biological sense.**

That answer matters.

After fourteen chapters, after all the scale changes, all the biological strangeness, all the distributed sensing, memory, prediction, and information integration, we do not get to reward ourselves with the conclusion we wanted.

The evidence does not currently justify it.

The stronger conclusion is narrower:

> **Civilization is an unusually integrated macroscopic organization and cognitive ecology. Whether it is moving toward a new level of individuality remains an open question.**

A **cognitive ecology** is a network of interacting people, institutions, technologies, memories, models, and information systems that together support cognition-like functions without requiring one central mind.

That classification fits the evidence substantially better than `higher-order biological individual.`

And that may sound disappointing.

It should not.

We have done something more important than prove the theory.

We have made it vulnerable.

We now know where civilization looks impressive.

We know where the analogy breaks.

We know what evidence is missing.

And we know exactly where an opponent should attack.

So that is what we will do.

Before Macroscopic Life earns the right to survive, we are going to try to kill it.
