# Macroscopic Life — FIG-02 Hostile Review v1

## Figure

**FIG-02 — Different Worlds, Real Realities**

## Verdict

**P1 — MAJOR REVISION REQUIRED BEFORE CLAIM LOCK**

The concept is visually strong and on-theme, but it currently overstates subjective animal experience, uses several imprecise or misleading sensory claims, and includes quantitative details that should not survive into publication without source-specific verification.

---

# 1. Core Scientific Job

The figure's correct job is:

> Different organisms sample different physical information from the same environment. Human unaided perception is therefore not a complete inventory of what is physically present.

It should **not** claim that we know what another species' subjective world literally looks or feels like.

---

# 2. Primary P1 Problem — Subjective Experience Overclaim

The title and repeated labels use phrases such as:

- `Different Worlds, Real Realities`
- `A world of heat`
- `A world of fields`
- `What a bee perceives`
- `What a shark perceives`
- `Every species experiences a different sensory reality`

These statements collapse two distinct claims:

1. species detect different physical variables or ranges — strongly supportable;
2. we know the phenomenology of each species' subjective experience — generally not supportable.

## Required correction

Reframe around **sensory access**, not literal subjective worlds.

Recommended title:

> **Same Landscape, Different Sensory Access**

Recommended master line:

> `Animals sharing the same environment can detect different physical information because their sensory systems are tuned to different signals and ranges.`

Use labels such as:

- `Signals available to human vision`
- `Ultraviolet sensitivity`
- `Infrared-sensitive pit organs`
- `Echolocation-derived spatial information`
- `Electroreception`
- `Olfactory information`

Avoid `this is what the animal sees` unless explicitly labeled as an explanatory false-color representation.

---

# 3. Human Panel

## Issue

`We see a small slice of the electromagnetic spectrum and hear a limited range of sound frequencies.` is directionally correct.

The exact displayed ranges can vary by observer, intensity, age, adaptation, and convention.

## Required correction

If numeric ranges are retained, write approximately and source them in the manuscript/endnotes rather than allowing AI-generated numbers to function as authority.

Safer embedded wording:

> `Human unaided vision samples only a narrow band of electromagnetic radiation; human hearing likewise covers a limited acoustic range.`

---

# 4. Bee Panel

## What is supportable

Many bees have photoreceptors sensitive into ultraviolet wavelengths and can use polarization information, including celestial polarization cues, in navigation.

## Problems

`They detect polarized light to navigate, even on cloudy days` is too sweeping as a universal bee statement.

The false-color strip implies a known literal bee visual experience.

## Required correction

Use:

> `Many bees detect ultraviolet light and polarization cues that humans cannot see unaided. These signals can reveal floral patterns and contribute to navigation.`

Label any altered-color image:

> `Illustrative false-color rendering — not a reconstruction of subjective bee experience.`

---

# 5. Snake Panel

## Major precision problem

The panel says `Snake — A World of Heat`, then states `Pit vipers detect infrared radiation, allowing them to "see" heat.`

Only certain snake lineages possess specialized infrared-sensitive pit organs; this is not a general snake trait.

`See heat` is useful colloquially but can blur thermal sensing with ordinary vision.

## Required correction

Change taxon label to:

> **Pit viper**

Use:

> `Pit vipers possess infrared-sensitive pit organs that detect thermal radiation from nearby objects and prey. This sensory channel is distinct from ordinary vision.`

Avoid implying a thermographic camera image is literally the animal's visual field.

---

# 6. Bat Panel

## What works

Echolocation is an excellent example of sensory access unavailable to unaided human perception.

## Problems

The figure says bats emit high-frequency sound waves and interpret echoes to `see` their environment.

Not all bat species rely on laryngeal echolocation in the same way, and `see` should be treated as analogy.

The single numeric range shown should not be canonical without source/context.

## Required correction

Use:

> `Many bats use echolocation, emitting ultrasonic calls and analyzing returning echoes to estimate the location, motion, size, and other properties of objects.`

Do not use `see` except in quotation marks with explicit analogy.

---

# 7. Shark Panel

## What works

Electroreception is a powerful and well-established example.

## Problems

The panel visually portrays large radiating fields around the shark/prey, which risks the same field/aura rhetoric we already rejected in FIG-07.

The exact electrical sensitivity number is a high-risk quantitative claim and should be independently verified before publication.

`They also sense vibrations and chemical cues in the water from great distances` is broad and potentially misleading because effective ranges depend strongly on signal, species, environment, and context.

## Required correction

Show **localized electrical gradients near prey** and the shark's electroreceptive organs rather than large decorative field waves.

Use:

> `Sharks and rays can detect extremely weak electric fields associated with nearby biological activity using specialized electroreceptors.`

Keep smell, lateral-line mechanosensation, and electroreception distinct rather than combining them into one sweeping claim.

---

# 8. Dog Panel

## P1 Quantitative Problem

`up to 300 million olfactory receptors compared to ~6 million in humans` and `100,000× more sensitive than humans` are familiar popular-science figures but are highly context-dependent and often repeated without sufficient qualification.

Olfactory sensitivity cannot be represented by one universal multiplier across all odorants.

## Required correction

Remove the `100,000×` claim.

Safer copy:

> `Dogs possess a highly developed olfactory system and can detect some odorants at concentrations far below human detection thresholds.`

If receptor-count comparisons are used, they must be species/breed/context qualified and independently sourced.

---

# 9. Key Takeaways Panel

## Current overclaims

`Every species experiences a different sensory reality.`

`Perception is shaped by biology, not absolute truth.`

`Different sensory worlds reveal different forms of information, all of which are real.`

The philosophical language exceeds the empirical job of the figure.

## Replace with

1. `Different sensory systems detect different subsets of physical information.`
2. `Human senses do not sample every signal present in the environment.`
3. `Sensory access depends on receptor biology, neural processing, signal strength, and context.`
4. `Scientific instruments can extend human detection beyond unaided sensory limits.`
5. `Unknown or undetected signals should be investigated, not assumed.`

This fifth point is an important brake against pseudoscientific misuse.

---

# 10. Missing Scientific Instrument Bridge

The figure currently contrasts animals with humans but misses an important scientific correction:

Humans are not confined to unaided senses because science builds instruments.

Without this, the visual can accidentally support the rhetorical move:

`Humans cannot naturally sense X → therefore X may evade science.`

That is false.

## Required addition

Add a small panel or footer:

> **Senses are limited. Measurement is extendable.**
>
> `Infrared cameras, ultraviolet detectors, microphones, magnetometers, electrodes, spectrometers, and other instruments allow humans to detect physical signals outside unaided perception.`

This substantially strengthens the epistemic rigor of Chapter 2.

---

# 11. Magnetoreception Is Missing

The canonical chapter architecture explicitly includes magnetoreception/polarization among the important examples.

Dog olfaction is useful but less central to the book's argument because humans already understand chemical sensing intuitively.

## Recommendation

Replace the dog panel with a **migratory bird** or another well-supported magnetoreception example.

Preferred six-panel set:

1. Human — visible light / audible sound
2. Bee — ultraviolet + polarization
3. Pit viper — infrared-sensitive pit organs
4. Bat — echolocation
5. Shark/ray — electroreception
6. Migratory bird — magnetic compass/orientation mechanisms, carefully qualified

Magnetoreception mechanisms remain an active research area in several taxa, so phrase the bird panel cautiously:

> `Many migratory birds use Earth's magnetic field as one source of orientation information. The underlying biological mechanisms remain an active area of research.`

This is scientifically much more useful to the Macroscopic Life progression.

---

# 12. False-Color Rule

All spectral/thermal/electric/magnetic visualizations need a visible convention label:

> `Illustrative encoding — colors/lines represent measured or inferred physical variables and are not literal depictions of the animal's subjective experience.`

This rule should become canonical for FIG-02.

# 13. Screenshot Test

## Current result

**FAIL — P1**

Detached from the chapter, the current figure can be read as evidence that each species inhabits a literally different reality and that humans perceive only an evolutionarily fabricated reality.

That is philosophically stronger than the evidence shown.

## Required screenshot-safe conclusion

> `One environment can contain physical information that different sensory systems access differently. Human unaided perception is partial, but science can extend it through measurement.`

# 14. Final Design Recommendation

Keep the attractive six-column comparative design, but make it a **signal-detection comparison**, not a phenomenology poster.

### Recommended title

**Figure 2 — Same Landscape, Different Sensory Access**

### Recommended subtitle

`Animals sharing one environment can detect different physical signals and ranges.`

### Bottom conclusion

> **The signal can be physically present even when an unaided human cannot detect it.**
>
> `That limitation is a reason to measure carefully — not a license to assume that every proposed hidden phenomenon is real.`
