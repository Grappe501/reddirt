# 1. Strongest hostile thesis

The strongest skeptical attack on Act IV is straightforward:

> The book spends eleven chapters teaching the reader to see distributed organization everywhere, then quietly transfers the intelligence of humans and machines upward to civilization, dresses the internet as a nervous system, and declares a planetary organism while pretending the conclusion is still open.

If that attack succeeds, the entire book fails.

PUB-7G survives it.

The act now makes the presence of intelligent components an evidentiary complication rather than evidence for a higher-level mind.

Its governing distinction is explicit:

> **CAPABILITY DISTRIBUTED ACROSS CIVILIZATION IS NOT AUTOMATICALLY CAPABILITY OF CIVILIZATION AS ONE AGENT.**

The chapter sequence then increases rather than relaxes the burden:

`SYSTEM-LEVEL CAPABILITY → EXTERNALIZED FUNCTION → MACROSCOPIC ORGANIZATION → INDIVIDUALITY TEST`

The argument does not move from `humans are intelligent` to `civilization is intelligent.`

It asks what level legitimately owns a capability, then requires higher-level variables to earn explanatory or predictive work.

# 2. Chapter 12 — hostile intelligence review

## Attack A — system capability is asserted because no component can do the task alone

**FAILS.**

The chapter explicitly blocks both attribution errors:

> **DO NOT ATTRIBUTE A CAPABILITY TO THE WHOLE MERELY BECAUSE CAPABLE PARTS ARE PRESENT.**

and:

> **DO NOT DENY A CAPABILITY TO THE WHOLE MERELY BECAUSE NO PART POSSESSES IT ALONE.**

System attribution is made conditional on organized relationships doing reproducible causal or predictive work beyond appropriate component-only baselines.

## Attack B — the homunculus argument is used to prove distributed intelligence

**FAILS.**

The homunculus regress is used negatively: an explanation cannot finish by inserting an unexplained smaller intelligence.

It does not establish that every distributed system is intelligent.

The chapter still requires an operational task, information, mechanism, alternatives, performance criterion, and baseline.

## Attack C — intelligence is defined so broadly that thermostats, evolution, and every adaptive process qualify

**FAILS.**

The definition requires the proposed system itself to implement task-relevant information processing and state-changing mechanisms at the claimed level and timescale.

The text explicitly states:

> **COMPUTATION ≠ INTELLIGENCE BY DEFAULT.**

The operational definition remains a performance concept rather than praise for successful outcomes.

## Attack D — intelligence is quietly equated with consciousness

**FAILS STRONGLY.**

The semantic barrier is explicit:

> **SIGNAL ≠ INFORMATION ≠ INTEGRATION ≠ INTELLIGENCE ≠ AGENCY ≠ CONSCIOUSNESS**

and:

> **TASK PERFORMANCE ≠ CONSCIOUSNESS**

No consciousness inference is required anywhere in the chapter.

## Attack E — `whole-only information` is mystical information above matter

**FAILS.**

Test 8 is grounded in measurable relations among physical states: timing, topology, configuration, correlation, interaction history, and distributed state.

The test requires a component-only baseline, measurable relational variables, out-of-sample prediction, perturbation where possible, and alternative boundary comparison.

No disembodied information is introduced.

## Attack F — emergence is used as mechanism

**FAILS.**

The chapter states:

> **EMERGENCE NAMES A LEVEL RELATION. IT DOES NOT BY ITSELF EXPLAIN THE MECHANISM.**

## Attack G — extended cognition proves a larger cognitive individual

**FAILS.**

The chapter deliberately adopts only the weaker empirical claim that human cognitive performance can depend causally on external structures.

Causal participation is not automatically constitutive membership.

### Chapter 12 verdict

**PASS.**

The chapter establishes a legitimate route to system-level capability while keeping higher-order cognitive individuality as a stronger and separately testable claim.
