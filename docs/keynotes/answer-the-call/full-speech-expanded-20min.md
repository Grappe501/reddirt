# Answer the Call — expanded draft (~18–20 min)

**Note:** Stage directions `(pause)` kept from your original. **Read aloud** and time; trim if you run long. Historical paragraphs are **summary biography** only—tighten or verify if this speech becomes paid media.

---

Good evening.

*(pause)*

This is my kind of room.

*(pause)*

Because this is a room that understands the work.

And let me be plain about what I mean—because the word *work* gets worn out in politics.

I’m not talking about the work of looking busy.

I’m talking about the work that shows up when nobody’s taking pictures.

The work that happens after the meeting—when somebody still has to make the phone call, fill out the form, keep the promise, carry the weight.

Not just talking about it—

doing it.

Showing up.

Getting it done.

*(pause)*

I’ve been in rooms where everybody leaves inspired…

…and nothing moves.

This is not that kind of room.

Because you know—in your bones—what it means to **finish** what you started.

*(pause)*

That’s how I was raised.

That’s how I try to live.

*(pause)*

Leadership is action.

And action doesn’t always look cinematic.

Sometimes it looks like a spreadsheet.

Sometimes it looks like being the person who shows up early and stays late.

Sometimes it looks like taking the blame so the mission can move—

or taking the **time** so a neighbor doesn’t have to carry a burden alone.

Real leadership is rarely the mic.

Most of the time, it’s the maintenance.

It’s the boring, faithful repetitions that make institutions trustworthy—

and make communities feel seen.

I joined the NAACP because of that.

Because this is an organization that has always been about the work.

Doing what’s required.

Stepping in when it matters.

Not waiting for perfect conditions.

And when I say the NAACP, I’m not reading a line off a page.

I’m talking about an inheritance of **sustained effort**—decades of showing up in courtrooms and communities, in school boards and public hearings, in moments when it would have been easier to go quiet.

That kind of legacy doesn’t live in a slogan.

It lives in **membership**.

It lives in people who pay dues with their time—who keep the machinery running when cameras are gone.

That’s the work I wanted to be near.

That’s the work I still want to be near.

And let’s be honest about “perfect conditions.”

Perfect conditions usually means somebody else has already removed the risk.

Perfect conditions usually means you’re waiting for permission.

Perfect conditions usually means you get to keep your hands clean.

But the work that changes lives—**real** work—rarely arrives clean.

It arrives messy.

It arrives urgent.

It arrives unfair.

And it asks you a question you can’t delegate:

**Will you move anyway?**

*(pause)*

And I’ve experienced that here.

People working together.

Learning from each other.

Black, white—different backgrounds, different experiences—

but coming together around one thing:

the work that needs to be done.

*(pause)*

Listening.

Learning.

Showing up.

*(pause)*

And I want to name the quiet part—because in a divided world, we pretend collaboration is a slogan.

But collaboration is a **discipline**.

It is a decision you make when you could choose cynicism.

It is a decision you make when you could choose the cheap heat of outrage.

It is a decision you make when you decide: *we will get this done together…*

…because the people we serve don’t have time for our egos.

*(pause)*

Leadership isn’t about who gets the credit.

It’s about whether the work gets done.

That belief didn’t start with me.

It started with my mom.

*(pause)*

My mom is here tonight.

And if you know her—you know this is true—

she is the first person to fill the gap.

You need supper? She’s got it.

You need to pull off an event? She’ll handle it.

Something falls through?

She steps in before anyone even asks.

*(pause)*

She doesn’t wait.

She doesn’t hesitate.

She just moves.

And I want you to understand what that kind of love looks like in practice—

because it’s not soft the way people pretend.

It’s **strong**.

It’s the strength of absorbing chaos so somebody else can breathe.

It’s the strength of being the person who carries the details nobody claps for—

so the people you love get to stand in the light.

*(pause)*

But that didn’t come from nowhere.

That came from a lifetime of doing what was required—no matter the cost.

My mom stood by my dad as he built his career.

And she worked.

Hard.

The kind of hard where you’re doing whatever it takes to keep things moving forward.

The kind of hard where nobody sees it.

Nobody applauds it.

But everything depends on it.

And then she made a decision.

She built something of her own.

She learned how to sell.

And she didn’t just try—

she became one of the top two agents in the entire state of Tennessee.

*(pause)*

That doesn’t happen by accident.

That’s discipline.

That’s showing up when you don’t feel like it.

That’s hearing “no” over and over—and coming back anyway.

Because rejection doesn’t just wound you.

It **teaches** you.

It teaches you that progress isn’t a straight line.

It teaches you that dignity isn’t the absence of struggle.

It teaches you that you can be excellent—

and still have to fight for the next open door.

Year after year, the scoreboard changes—but the habit doesn’t.

The habit is: show up anyway.

The habit is: do the next right thing.

The habit is: don’t confuse motion with noise—**measure your life by what actually moves**.

And then life shifted.

We moved.

And she had to walk away from everything she built.

*(pause)*

And here’s what I learned from that—

you can build something great…

and still be asked to start over.

*(pause)*

And when that moment comes—

you don’t sit down.

*(pause)*

You step in again.

Because restarting isn’t failure.

Sometimes restarting is the bravest continuation of faith there is.

Years later, she gave me a poem.

A woman standing at the edge of darkness.

About to step.

“There will be something solid…

or you will be taught how to fly.”

I used to think that was about bravery.

*(pause)*

Now I think it’s about responsibility.

Because bravery can be a moment.

Responsibility is a **pattern**.

It’s the decision to stop asking the universe for guarantees—

and start asking yourself what you owe the people in front of you.

There was a moment in my life where everything changed.

A Christmas party.

My parents’ house.

Full. Loud. Busy.

And right in the middle of it—

I met her.

She was three.

*(pause)*

Her name was Destiny.

*(pause)*

And I knew.

And here’s the truth—

you don’t always get a plan.

You don’t always get clarity.

But sometimes—

you just know.

And when you know—

you have a choice.

Walk away…

or step.

So I stepped.

Monday morning—I got on the phone.

And then I got to work.

Paperwork.

Calls.

Follow-ups.

Pushing.

Showing up.

Again and again.

Because I had learned something already—

when something matters—

you move.

And let me say this plainly, because it matters in a room like this:

Love is not only a feeling you announce.

Love is what you **carry**.

It’s the schedule you keep—the appointments you don’t miss—the stamina you build when no one is cheering you on.

It’s the steady work of proving—through action—that a child is not an inconvenience.

That they are not optional.

That they are **home**.

And if you’ve ever carried a long fight, you know how this part feels:

Some days you’re moving forward.

Some days you’re just refusing to quit.

Both count.

But it didn’t happen overnight.

It took years.

Years of fighting.

Years of uncertainty.

Years of wondering—

is this going to work?

And I learned something in that process—

systems don’t always work the way they should.

People fall through cracks.

Good people.

Kids.

Families.

And if no one steps in—

those gaps stay open.

And once you’ve seen a child’s life hang in the balance because a timeline slipped, a door stayed closed, or a system shrugged—you don’t forget it.

You don’t get to pretend you didn’t see it.

Because compassion without follow-through is just comfort for the comfortable.

And I wasn’t willing to let that happen.

And somewhere along the way—

Destiny became Grace.

And she changed me.

She taught me resilience.

She taught me identity.

She taught me strength.

And now she’s a mother—

and she’s filling gaps for the next generation.

That’s how change happens.

The world is not changed only by the people whose names we know.

It’s changed by people who step.

And when I say *people who step*—

I’m not asking you to imagine perfection.

I’m asking you to imagine persistence.

And I want to linger here for a minute—because we live in a culture that wants speeches to sprint past history.

But history is not decoration.

History is **instruction**.

It tells you what stepping looks like when the cost is real.

Women like Clara Barton—

who walked into war zones without training—

because people needed help.

And when you study what she did in the historical record, what you’re really looking at is **provision under fire**—not hero branding.

In the middle of national crisis, she didn’t wait for a committee to bless her.

She moved toward need.

She organized care when care was scarce—supplies, triage, persistence in places where urgency doesn’t excuse you from responsibility.

Later, the same instinct to organize under pressure helped shape the American Red Cross.

She built structures when structures didn’t exist.

She proves the point: sometimes the credential isn’t a certificate—

it’s courage paired with refusal to look away.

Jo Ann Robinson—

who didn’t wait for permission—

and moved a movement forward overnight.

In Montgomery, she was not a bystander waiting for history to knock politely.

She was an educator and an organizer—someone who understood that moral heat has to be turned into **tactical heat** if you want morning to come.

She wasn’t looking for the perfect headline.

She was looking for **disciplined action**—the kind that turns moral clarity into organized momentum.

She helps us remember: movements don’t only need speeches.

They need people who can execute—who can communicate with speed and seriousness when the moment demands it.

They need people who can multiply courage—so one brave refusal becomes a city-facing truth.

Septima Poinsette Clark—

who taught people to navigate systems designed to keep them out.

In citizenship schools, she helped people learn what dominion tried to withhold: reading, writing, the practical mechanics of participation—so ordinary people could walk into offices and hearings with clarity instead of fear.

She understood something we still need tonight:

**information is power**, and **literacy is dignity**.

She didn’t teach people to beg systems for mercy.

She taught people how to **use** the tools that citizenship requires—

so the door could be approached with preparation, not panic.

Katherine Johnson—

who stayed in rooms that didn’t welcome her—

because the work needed her.

At NASA, during the early years of the space program, she was part of the computing work that helped move this country from aspiration to trajectory—doing mathematics that had to be right, checked, defended, trusted.

She wasn’t there to be convenient.

She was there because the math had to be true.

Because the mission had to be safe.

Because excellence is sometimes defined as: *stay*, when everything in the room pretends you don’t belong.

Different centuries.

Different uniforms.

Different kinds of danger.

Same question:

**Will you step?**

They didn’t wait.

They stepped.

And if you’re wondering why I’m telling **their** stories in **my** speech—

it’s because I need the room to remember something we forget when politics gets loud:

you are not alone in this instinct to step.

You are part of a long line.

A long line of people who decided inconvenience would not be the final word.

My dad taught me—

no ceilings.

“Conceive it. Believe it. Achieve it.”

And he taught me—

your life is about what you do for others.

My husband showed me what that looks like.

Statewide ballot initiative.

No money.

No experience.

People said it was impossible.

They were probably right.

But he stepped anyway.

And I watched what stepping costs—

sleep you don’t get, pride you swallow, doors that close, skepticism you absorb.

And I watched what stepping builds—

muscle memory for courage.

And we didn’t get all the way there.

But we got close.

Closer than anyone thought we could.

And that’s when I realized—

you don’t step because you’re guaranteed success.

You step because it matters.

Because *close* still moves the line.

Because *close* still teaches the next person what’s possible.

Because *close* still leaves the world different than it was before you refused to stay seated.

People say what I’m doing now is impossible.

*(pause)*

I don’t buy it.

I didn’t run to put my name on a ballot.

*(pause)*

I ran because there are gaps.

*(pause)*

And I can fill them.

That’s it.

And let me tell you what a gap sounds like—because it’s not always dramatic.

Sometimes it’s a parent working a double and still trying to read instructions that weren’t written to be understood.

Sometimes it’s a senior counting pills and counting dollars in the same breath.

Sometimes it’s a student being told to pull themselves up by bootstraps—

when nobody ever taught them where the boots were kept.

Gaps don’t always shout.

Sometimes they whisper.

And the work—the **real** work—is learning how to listen for the whisper before it turns into a crisis.

And once you see the gaps—

you can’t unsee them.

And once you know you can help—

you don’t get to sit still.

We don’t have time to wait.

Not because fear is fashionable—

but because somebody’s life is always waiting on the other side of your delay.

So tonight—

whatever gap you see…

whatever call you feel…

whatever thing you know is yours to do—

don’t wait.

Step.

Not for applause.

Not for permission.

For the work.

Because there will be something solid—

or you’ll learn how to fly.

Thank you.
