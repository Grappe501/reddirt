🎤 KEYNOTE: “Answer the Call”
Good evening.

(pause)

This is my kind of room.

(pause)

Because this is a room that understands the work.

Not just talking about it—

doing it.

Showing up.

Getting it done.

(pause)

That’s how I was raised.

That’s how I try to live.

(pause)

Leadership is action.

I joined the NAACP because of that.

Because this is an organization that has always been about the work.

Doing what’s required.

Stepping in when it matters.

Not waiting for perfect conditions.

(pause)

And I’ve experienced that here.

People working together.

Learning from each other.

Black, white—different backgrounds, different experiences—

but coming together around one thing:

the work that needs to be done.

(pause)

Listening.

Learning.

Showing up.

(pause)

Leadership isn’t about who gets the credit.

It’s about whether the work gets done.

That belief didn’t start with me.

It started with my mom.

(pause)

My mom is here tonight.

And if you know her—you know this is true—

she is the first person to fill the gap.

You need supper? She’s got it.

You need to pull off an event? She’ll handle it.

Something falls through?

She steps in before anyone even asks.

(pause)

She doesn’t wait.

She doesn’t hesitate.

She just moves.

But that didn’t come from nowhere.

That came from a lifetime of doing what was required—no matter the cost.

My mom stood by my dad as he built his career.

And she worked.

Hard.

The kind of hard where you’re doing whatever it takes to keep things moving forward.

The kind of hard where nobody sees it.

Nobody applauds it.

But everything depends on it.

And then she made a decision.

She built something of her own.

She learned how to sell.

And she didn’t just try—

she became one of the top two agents in the entire state of Tennessee.

(pause)

That doesn’t happen by accident.

That’s discipline.

That’s showing up when you don’t feel like it.

That’s hearing “no” over and over—and coming back anyway.

And then life shifted.

We moved.

And she had to walk away from everything she built.

(pause)

And here’s what I learned from that—

you can build something great…

and still be asked to start over.

(pause)

And when that moment comes—

you don’t sit down.

(pause)

You step in again.

Years later, she gave me a poem.

A woman standing at the edge of darkness.

About to step.

“There will be something solid…
or you will be taught how to fly.”

I used to think that was about bravery.

(pause)

Now I think it’s about responsibility.

There was a moment in my life where everything changed.

A Christmas party.

My parents’ house.

Full. Loud. Busy.

And right in the middle of it—

I met her.

She was three.

(pause)

Her name was Destiny.

(pause)

And I knew.

And here’s the truth—

you don’t always get a plan.

You don’t always get clarity.

But sometimes—

you just know.

And when you know—

you have a choice.

Walk away…

or step.

So I stepped.

Monday morning—I got on the phone.

And then I got to work.

Paperwork.

Calls.

Follow-ups.

Pushing.

Showing up.

Again and again.

Because I had learned something already—

when something matters—

you move.

But it didn’t happen overnight.

It took years.

Years of fighting.

Years of uncertainty.

Years of wondering—

is this going to work?

And I learned something in that process—

systems don’t always work the way they should.

People fall through cracks.

Good people.

Kids.

Families.

And if no one steps in—

those gaps stay open.

And I wasn’t willing to let that happen.

And somewhere along the way—

Destiny became Grace.

And she changed me.

She taught me resilience.

She taught me identity.

She taught me strength.

And now she’s a mother—

and she’s filling gaps for the next generation.

That’s how change happens.

The world is not changed only by the people whose names we know.

It’s changed by people who step.

Women like Clara Barton—

who walked into war zones without training—

because people needed help.

Jo Ann Robinson—

who didn’t wait for permission—

and moved a movement forward overnight.

Septima Poinsette Clark—

who taught people to navigate systems designed to keep them out.

Katherine Johnson—

who stayed in rooms that didn’t welcome her—

because the work needed her.

They didn’t wait.

They stepped.

My dad taught me—

no ceilings.

“Conceive it. Believe it. Achieve it.”

And he taught me—

your life is about what you do for others.

My husband showed me what that looks like.

Statewide ballot initiative.

No money.

No experience.

People said it was impossible.

They were probably right.

But he stepped anyway.

And we didn’t get all the way there.

But we got close.

Closer than anyone thought we could.

And that’s when I realized—

you don’t step because you’re guaranteed success.

You step because it matters.

People say what I’m doing now is impossible.

(pause)

I don’t buy it.

I didn’t run to put my name on a ballot.

(pause)

I ran because there are gaps.

(pause)

And I can fill them.

That’s it.

And once you see the gaps—

you can’t unsee them.

And once you know you can help—

you don’t get to sit still.

We don’t have time to wait.

So tonight—

whatever gap you see…

whatever call you feel…

whatever thing you know is yours to do—

don’t wait.

Step.

Because there will be something solid—

or you’ll learn how to fly.
